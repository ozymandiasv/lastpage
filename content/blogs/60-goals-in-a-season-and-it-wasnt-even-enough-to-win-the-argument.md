Beyond valuation, beyond forgery or imitation. 18 years since that trembling teenager of touch and tease, first tiptoed on this storied stage; Now in his immaculate maturity; CR7 - Reunited.

I think I have been watching Cristiano Ronaldo’s retirement coming for a long time. Ever since Messi stepped away, there was always this strange feeling that Ronaldo would eventually have to follow. Maybe there was a little ego involved in that too. I wanted to see how long he could keep going after his great rival had already left the stage. Ronaldo always seemed like the sort of person who would refuse to accept that the ending had arrived simply because everyone else thought it should. Eventually even the most extraordinary careers have to end, and today it feels like we are finally closing one of the greatest chapters football has ever had.
## He Played With the God
There is something almost ridiculous about the era Ronaldo played in. He didn’t just become one of the greatest players of his generation; he spent his prime playing alongside someone who seemed to operate by completely different rules. And somehow, Ronaldo was still the best player in the world himself. That is probably the thing I find most incredible when I look back at his career. In another generation, without the shadow of Messi constantly hanging over him, Ronaldo’s numbers and achievements would probably have been spoken about as almost untouchable. Instead, they became part of an argument. He scored 60 goals for Real Madrid in the 2011–12 season, while later seasons brought even more extraordinary numbers, and Real Madrid eventually watched him leave as the club’s all-time leading scorer with 451 goals in 438 competitive appearances. And somehow, because there was another player doing equally absurd things at the same time, we became accustomed to it. That is what makes this era so strange. We were watching something extraordinary and treating it as normal because there was always another extraordinary thing happening somewhere else.
## Madrid Was Where I Really Watched Him
I never really watched Ronaldo during his first Manchester United spell in the way I watched him later. Most of my memories of him are from Real Madrid, and that is where, for me, Cristiano Ronaldo became Cristiano Ronaldo. I have always been more of a Premier League person, but there was something about those Madrid nights that pulled me into La Liga. I would occasionally sit down to watch Real Madrid even though, let’s be honest, I hated Madrid. But Ronaldo made it difficult not to. And then there was El Clásico. Those matches had a different electricity when Ronaldo and Messi were on opposite sides. You could feel the entire football world waiting for the next moment, the next goal, the next ridiculous individual performance. After Ronaldo left Madrid, El Clásico never quite felt the same to me. Obviously the rivalry remained enormous, but something had disappeared. It wasn’t just one player leaving a club; one half of the defining rivalry of that era had suddenly moved somewhere else, and eventually the other half would leave too.
## We Normalised Numbers That Should Never Have Been Normal
I sometimes think the strangest legacy of Ronaldo and Messi is not even the trophies or the individual awards. It is what they did to our understanding of football statistics. We became so accustomed to them scoring 50, 60, 70 and sometimes more goals that we started treating those numbers as the standard for greatness. Ronaldo’s 60-goal club season in 2011–12 was absurd by almost any historical measure, and yet it existed alongside Messi’s 73-goal season. When players today score around 30 or 40 goals in a season, we can find ourselves discussing whether they have had an extraordinary year, while there was a period when two men were putting up numbers that looked more like video-game statistics. And it wasn’t only goals. Their assists, chance creation, movement, consistency and influence on enormous matches were equally ridiculous. The funniest part is that we didn’t even realise how abnormal it was while it was happening. We were too busy arguing about which one was better to understand that we were watching a statistical anomaly in real time.
## A Different Kind of Greatness
When I think about Ronaldo alongside players like Zidane, Ronaldo Nazário, Ronaldinho, Henry, Kaká and the other greats who defined different eras, what stands out is how completely he transformed himself. Ronaldo Nazário was devastating because of his explosiveness and ability to destroy a defence almost before it could react. Zidane had that extraordinary elegance where the game seemed to slow down around him. Ronaldinho played football like he was entertaining himself. Henry combined speed, intelligence and finishing in a way that made him terrifying to defend. Ronaldo was different. He built himself into a machine. The young winger from Manchester United gradually became one of the most devastating goalscorers football has ever seen, and when age began taking away his speed, he adapted again. His career became an almost absurd demonstration of reinvention: change the body, change the position, change the way you score, but keep finding the goal. That longevity is part of what separates him from so many great players. Being brilliant for five years is one thing. Remaining relevant for almost two decades at the highest level requires something else entirely.
## The End of an Era
There will be arguments about Ronaldo forever, that is exactly how it should be. Football doesn’t need every great player to be placed neatly into a ranking. Some careers are simply too significant for that. Ronaldo won in England, conquered Spain, succeeded in Italy, became Portugal’s greatest scorer and one of the most prolific players international football has ever seen. He became the face of an entire generation of football, and for millions of people he was the reason they started watching the game. For me, though, his greatest achievement might be simpler: he survived an era in which another player was doing the impossible at exactly the same time, and still managed to become immortal himself.
We were incredibly lucky to watch him. We were lucky to have Zidane, Ronaldo Nazário, Ronaldinho, Henry and all those players before him, but we were especially lucky to live through a period where the standard of greatness was pushed so far that even the extraordinary started looking ordinary. Cristiano Ronaldo is finally leaving the international football, and it feels strange because for so long I assumed I would always see him somewhere. Maybe that is what retirement really means with players like him. Not that football suddenly becomes less beautiful, but that the version of football we grew up with has finally become history.
The man who played with God was still one of the greatest players the game has ever seen. And somehow, he made that look normal too.
