I find it difficult to understand how we have arrived here again. The Supreme Court has now suspended the life sentences of two men convicted in the Bilkis Bano gangrape and murder case, granting them bail after more than seventeen years in prison. The two men, Bipinchand Joshi and Pradip Modhiya, are among the eleven convicts whose premature release in August 2022 was overturned by the Supreme Court in January 2024. The original crime was horrific: Bilkis was pregnant when she was gangraped during the 2002 Gujarat riots, and seven members of her family, including her three-year-old daughter, were murdered. This was not an ordinary criminal case that we can casually reduce to the number of years somebody has spent in prison. It was an attack on an entire family, on a woman who was already fleeing communal violence, and on the very idea that people should be able to live without fear of such brutality. I understand that the latest order concerns pending appeals and that the Court has cited the period of incarceration, the legal questions involved and the severe disability of one of the appellants. Bail is not acquittal, and their convictions have not been overturned. But I cannot help asking why the gravity of this particular crime seems to become a secondary consideration every time the question of their release comes up. What about the people who never came home? What about Bilkis, who has had to live with the consequences of that day for the rest of her life?
## We Have Already Seen This Once
And this is what makes the latest development particularly difficult to accept. In August 2022, eleven convicts were granted remission by the Gujarat government and walked out of prison. What followed was perhaps even more disturbing: they were welcomed with garlands and sweets by people celebrating their release as though some extraordinary achievement had taken place. Imagine what that must have felt like for Bilkis and her family. The men convicted of gangraping her and murdering her relatives were being publicly celebrated. The Supreme Court eventually intervened on 8 January 2024, quashing the remission orders. It held that Gujarat was not the appropriate government to grant remission because the trial had taken place in Maharashtra, and described the state's action as an abuse of discretion and an instance of usurpation of jurisdiction. The Court also found that the process had involved suppression of material facts and abuse of its own earlier order. All eleven were directed to return to prison. Yet the developments have continued. In September 2026, the Gujarat High Court granted Radheshyam Shah seven days' parole on the stated grounds of his family's economic condition. Now two more convicts have secured bail from the Supreme Court. I am not suggesting that parole, remission and bail are interchangeable legal concepts. They are not. But from the perspective of a survivor, the repeated prospect of seeing those convicted in the case walk out of prison must be deeply distressing. How many times must she be made to revisit the same nightmare?
## Where Is the Remorse?
People may speak about seventeen years in prison, good conduct, repentance and the possibility of rehabilitation. These are matters courts can consider, but I cannot understand why the conversation so often seems to revolve around the convicted and so rarely around the people who suffered. What does repentance mean when we discuss crimes of this magnitude? And what about the absence of it? Babu Bajrangi, for instance, belongs to the separate Naroda Patiya massacre case, not the Bilkis Bano case. His recorded statements in the 2007 Tehelka sting operation, in which he boasted about his role in the violence, remain deeply disturbing. I bring him up because the wider history of the 2002 violence makes it impossible to discuss justice without discussing the public glorification of perpetrators and the absence of remorse in some of those who participated in the violence. We should be able to ask whether the conduct of convicted perpetrators, their attitudes towards their crimes and the possibility of repetition are being taken seriously enough. The question cannot always be how much punishment a convicted person has already endured. It must also be whether justice has adequately recognised the magnitude of the harm done.
## What Should This Case Have Meant?
I wanted this case to become a precedent in how seriously India treats crimes of this nature, particularly sexual violence committed amid communal violence. I am not casually demanding the death penalty, nor am I using the phrase *rarest of rare* as though it automatically determines the punishment in every horrifying case. That is a specific legal standard governing the death penalty. But I do believe that life imprisonment for crimes of this magnitude should carry real meaning, and that decisions affecting the liberty of convicts must be made with exceptional care, transparency and sensitivity to the survivor. People often compare the Bilkis Bano case with the Nirbhaya case, and both are among the most disturbing examples of sexual violence in recent Indian memory. I would hesitate to rank human suffering, but the Bilkis case has an additional dimension: the gangrape took place during communal violence, alongside the murder of members of her family, including a small child. It should have been a lasting reminder of what happens when violence, prejudice and impunity converge. Instead, we have watched the convicted men receive remission, be celebrated publicly, return to prison after the Supreme Court intervened, and now obtain further forms of release. I feel deeply disappointed with the way this case continues to unfold. I am not asking the courts to abandon the law. I am asking them to remember why the law exists in the first place. Justice cannot become a conversation in which the convicted are always visible and the survivor is reduced to a footnote. Bilkis Bano deserves more than having to watch this case return to the headlines every few years. She deserves to be able to live without being reminded that the people who destroyed her family might once again be outside prison.
