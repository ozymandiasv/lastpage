The detention of Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, two prominent faces of the Cockroach Janata Party (CJP), followed by their being sent to five days of judicial custody on 14 October, has opened up several questions that deserve answers. Both were detained on 10 October and released by a Special Executive Magistrate on 11 October after executing bonds requiring them to maintain peace for a year. Reports describe these as template bonds, with a reported surety of ₹50,000. They were detained again on 13 October after entering Jantar Mantar by breaching barricades, despite the site having been denotified as an authorised protest venue. The police have accused them of trespassing, violating prohibitory orders, obstructing public servants and breaching the earlier peace bonds. On 14 October, they were remanded to judicial custody for 5 days. These are the police’s allegations, not a final judicial determination of guilt. The important distinction is that the reported bond was signed during their earlier release, not today; the subsequent custody followed the alleged breach. The exact wording and legal scope of the bond matter enormously because a requirement to maintain peace should not become a convenient instrument for preventing peaceful political dissent. We need to know what the bond actually prohibited, what evidence the police have presented to establish its breach, and how the court assessed the allegations. CJP’s legal team has also alleged that the detained leaders were denied access to their lawyers, a claim that warrants scrutiny. If the authorities acted lawfully, they should be able to demonstrate it transparently. If they abused their powers, that too must have consequences.
## CJP: A Popular Movement, But How Much Ground Support?
Let me make one thing clear. As far as I can see, CJP has become a recognisable and popular face of public dissent, particularly among young people. But does that popularity automatically translate into the organisational strength needed to bring thousands of people onto the streets? That remains an open question. A movement can attract attention online, generate public anger and become a familiar name without possessing the network necessary to sustain a large demonstration. I remember reports and discussions surrounding the demand for Dharmendra Pradhan’s resignation, when there were calls for Aishe Ghosh to become involved, potentially bringing in a much larger student mobilisation. The movement may provide the public face, the political messaging and the immediate spark, while established student and youth organisations provide much of the organisational machinery. That is not necessarily a weakness. Successful movements have often depended on coalitions of people with different strengths. But it does mean that we should look beyond the popularity of a movement’s leading figures and ask who is doing the groundwork, who is mobilising people, and whether the movement can work with others when the political moment demands it.
## The Student Organisations Behind the Mobilisation
Several student and youth organisations have been central to this wider mobilisation, and their contributions deserve recognition. The Students’ Federation of India (SFI), associated with the CPI(M), the All India Students’ Association (AISA), associated with the CPI(ML) Liberation, and the All India Students’ Federation (AISF), associated with the CPI, have established networks of student activists across campuses. The Democratic Youth Federation of India (DYFI), also associated with the CPI(M), brings a wider youth organisation into the picture, while Disha represents a newer, independent communist formation. These organisations may not be familiar names to everyone, but they are hardly insignificant when it comes to organising protests, mobilising students and sustaining political campaigns. As I write this, I cannot help thinking about how often student movements with left-wing and communist influences have risen to challenge authority, shaped political discourse and then faded from public memory, even when their contributions remain part of India’s political history. And, almost as an involuntary historical reflection, I find myself thinking of Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev Thapar and Chandrashekhar Azad, and how revolutionary politics has repeatedly drawn young people towards struggles much larger than themselves. The point, however, is not to romanticise any organisation or ideology. It is to recognise that the crowd on the street rarely materialises out of nowhere. It takes networks, local organisers, student unions and people willing to do the unglamorous work of bringing others together. The NSUI, the Congress’s student wing, has also been protesting over the same concerns, although it has its own organisational identity outside CJP. This makes the question of coordination even more important.
## Why Was CJP Missing From Such an Important March?
On 12 October, Rahul Gandhi led opposition leaders and supporters in a march towards the Supreme Court, seeking action over alleged irregularities in the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls and demanding accountability from Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. Leaders and members associated with several opposition parties and student organisations joined the mobilisation, including representatives of the Samajwadi Party, Trinamool Congress, the Uddhav Thackeray-led Shiv Sena faction, the Sharad Pawar-led NCP faction and left-wing student organisations. The absence of some parties, including the Aam Aadmi Party and the DMK, has its own political context, though the precise reasons for each organisation’s decision should not simply be assumed. What concerns me more is CJP’s apparent lack of a visible representative as an organisation at such a significant moment. I understand that several of its leading figures had been detained, while Abhijeet Dipke was reportedly in Maharashtra for personal reasons. But if Neha Bora and other student activists could participate, why was there no visible CJP representation in the movement’s own name? Perhaps there are explanations that have not been made public. Perhaps there were practical constraints or disagreements over the march. We should not mistake an absence for proof of a secret political arrangement. Nevertheless, the question deserves to be asked, especially when CJP has been one of the most recognisable faces of the protests. A movement demanding accountability from constitutional institutions must also be willing to explain its own political decisions to the people who support it.
## CJP, AAP and the Cost of Political Ambiguity
I have heard suggestions that sections of CJP’s membership are sympathetic to the Aam Aadmi Party, and that the movement may be politically aligned with it to some degree. I have no inherent objection to that. Movements do not have to be politically sterile to be legitimate, and activists are entitled to their own political affiliations. But there is a difference between having political sympathies and allowing those sympathies to determine whether a movement participates in a wider democratic mobilisation. If CJP is aligned with AAP, it should be honest about the nature and extent of that alignment. If it is an independent movement, it should demonstrate that independence through its actions. The question becomes particularly important when the issue at stake is larger than any individual party. Alleged irregularities in electoral rolls, the right of eligible citizens to vote, and the accountability of the Election Commission are not exclusively Congress issues, communist issues or AAP issues. They concern the integrity of the democratic process itself. A united opposition does not require everyone to agree on every political question, nor does it mean that smaller movements must surrender their identity to larger parties. It does require them to recognise when their differences are less important than the common cause. If CJP believes the electoral process is under threat, it must explain why it was not visibly represented at a march that sought to take those concerns to the country’s highest court. Silence creates room for speculation, and in a movement built on public trust, that is a problem worth addressing.
## This Is About Our Democratic Integrity
I do not want CJP to compromise its principles, and I certainly do not want to see its leaders surrender their convictions in exchange for political convenience. The phrase BJP washing machine is often used to describe allegations that political figures facing serious accusations suddenly find themselves treated differently after switching political sides. Whether such claims apply in any particular case must be established through evidence, but the broader concern is understandable: political opposition should not come with a price that encourages people to abandon their convictions simply to escape legal pressure. At the same time, CJP must be prepared to answer legitimate questions about its own conduct, alliances and decisions. Supporting a movement does not mean suspending our ability to criticise it. We can oppose what we believe to be an excessive response by the police, demand due process for detained activists, and still ask why a movement was absent from an important protest. These positions are not contradictory. In fact, they are essential to any meaningful democratic politics. Peaceful protest is a right, but accountability must operate in every direction, including towards the movements that claim to defend that right. I want to see a united opposition, not because every party or organisation is beyond criticism, but because the defence of democratic integrity cannot depend on the personal ambitions of individual leaders. This is not a fight for one party to replace another. It is a demand that our democratic rights remain intact, that every eligible citizen’s vote matters, and that no institution or political organisation becomes too powerful to answer questions. That is the principle worth standing together for.
