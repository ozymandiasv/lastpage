The argument that the BJP could not be stealing votes because it failed to win Kerala or Tamil Nadu is deeply flawed. It assumes that allegations of electoral manipulation mean the party must be capable of controlling every election in every state. Is that really what people are alleging? Nobody needs to believe that the BJP is omnipotent, or that every election result is manipulated, to question whether electoral irregularities might have influenced particular constituencies. Strategic manipulation, if it occurs, would not necessarily mean changing every vote everywhere. It could mean targeting specific constituencies, exploiting weaknesses in voter registration, or benefiting from irregularities where the electoral contest is close. The argument that the BJP lost somewhere, therefore it could not have benefited from manipulation elsewhere, confuses a specific allegation with an absurd claim of universal control.
## Maharashtra: One State, Two Very Different Results
Consider Maharashtra in 2024. In the Lok Sabha elections, the BJP won just nine of the state’s 48 parliamentary seats, while the opposition performed strongly. Yet, in the Assembly elections held a few months later, the BJP won 132 of the 288 seats, helping the Mahayuti alliance secure a decisive majority. These contrasting results are not proof of electoral manipulation. Different elections involve different issues, alliances, campaign strategies, and voter behaviour. However, they demonstrate why electoral outcomes must be examined in their specific context rather than used as blanket arguments for or against allegations. If someone raises concerns about voter deletions or irregularities in particular constituencies, responding that the BJP lost parliamentary seats in the same state does not address those concerns. The relevant question is whether the alleged irregularities occurred and whether they could have affected the outcome.
## Haryana: Votes and Seats Tell Different Stories
Haryana offers another example of why election results deserve closer examination. In the 2024 Assembly election, the BJP won 48 of the 90 seats, while Congress won 37. Their vote shares were remarkably close, with the BJP at approximately 39.94 per cent and Congress at 39.09 per cent. Congress questioned aspects of the results and raised allegations concerning electoral irregularities, which the Election Commission rejected. The narrow difference in vote share alongside the substantial difference in seats illustrates how constituency-level outcomes can produce a decisive victory without a comparable statewide vote-share advantage. Again, this does not establish that votes were stolen. But neither does the BJP’s victory, by itself, settle every question raised about the election. Allegations must be investigated on their evidence, not dismissed merely because the final result has already been declared.
## Delhi: Winning Is Not Proof of Fairness, Losing Is Not Proof of Fraud
In Delhi’s 2025 Assembly election, the BJP won 48 of the 70 seats, ending the Aam Aadmi Party’s decade-long rule. Arvind Kejriwal even lost his New Delhi constituency. There are legitimate political explanations for such an outcome, including anti-incumbency, campaign strategy, public dissatisfaction, and changing voter preferences. The result itself does not prove electoral manipulation, and it would be irresponsible to claim otherwise without evidence. But imagine someone raising a specific, evidence-based concern about voter-list deletions in a particular constituency. Would pointing to the BJP’s victory in Delhi answer that concern? No. It would simply tell us who won. Establishing whether the process was fair requires examining the voter rolls, the complaints, the evidence, and the Election Commission’s response. An election result is the outcome that needs to be understood, not a certificate that every part of the process was flawless.
## The Real Question Is Whether the Process Can Be Trusted
The same logic applies to Kerala and Tamil Nadu. The BJP’s inability to secure power there does not disprove the possibility of irregularities in constituencies elsewhere. Nor does it establish that such irregularities occurred. These are two entirely different questions, and confusing them helps nobody. If the allegation is that electoral manipulation was strategically used to influence particular outcomes, then the relevant evidence must concern those outcomes and the mechanisms allegedly involved. Nobody needs to assume that a political party is stupid enough to manipulate every election identically, or powerful enough to guarantee victory everywhere. Political parties operate in different social and electoral environments, and their prospects vary accordingly. The question is not whether the BJP wins every election. It is whether every eligible citizen can vote, every valid vote is counted, and the institutions responsible for safeguarding the process can demonstrate that they have acted impartially. A party’s victories do not automatically establish guilt, just as its defeats do not automatically establish innocence.
