Despite all the propaganda from the right wing, Lula has beaten Flávio Bolsonaro in Brazil's presidential election. I know the margin was not enormous, with Lula winning around 51.8 per cent of the vote, but the political significance of this victory goes well beyond the numbers. In a world where far-right leaders are gaining ground, where nationalism is sold as patriotism and where political opponents are routinely portrayed as enemies of the nation, Brazil has chosen a different path. To me, this feels like a rejection of more than one candidate. It is a setback for the political movement surrounding Donald Trump, for the growing international network of right-wing populism, and for the idea that a country must surrender its independent voice to powerful leaders and their allies. The Bolsonaro family had the backing of a political climate that has favoured the far right across much of the world, yet Lula managed to turn the election around after trailing in the first round. That matters. It shows that the rightward movement is not inevitable, that voters can still reject it, and that the political future is not already decided for us. 
## The Amazon Can Breathe a Little Easier
For starters, the Amazon may get to breathe a little easier. I cannot think of many environmental issues that matter as much as the future of this rainforest. It is not merely a collection of trees belonging to Brazil; it is an ecosystem of global importance, home to extraordinary biodiversity and deeply connected to the planet's climate. The decisions made in Brasília can affect communities, wildlife and environmental systems far beyond Brazil's borders. Lula has promised to protect the Amazon and fight deforestation, and his return offers a chance to continue that work. Of course, an election victory does not magically stop illegal logging, mining, land grabbing or the destruction of Indigenous territories. Brazil still has powerful economic interests that benefit from exploiting the forest, and protecting it requires more than speeches at international climate conferences. But political leadership matters. A government that treats environmental protection as a responsibility has a very different starting point from one that sees the natural world primarily as a resource to be exploited. I am glad that the Amazon has a better chance of being defended, even if the real test will be what happens on the ground over the next four years.
## Trump, Netanyahu and the Politics of Staying in Power
What interests me just as much is the wider political picture. Trump has made no secret of his desire to expand American influence and support political allies who share his worldview. Netanyahu, meanwhile, has faced sustained protests in Israel over his leadership, his government's policies and questions surrounding his efforts to remain in power. These situations are not identical, and their political circumstances should not be treated as interchangeable. But I see a troubling pattern in the way powerful leaders can turn politics into a permanent struggle to preserve their own authority. Elections become battles for personal survival, criticism becomes disloyalty, and every institution that places limits on power is treated as an obstacle. This is why I find Lula's victory encouraging. It reminds us that political movements built around dominant personalities can be defeated at the ballot box unless the ballot box itself is manipulated. No leader, regardless of popularity or influence, should be considered indispensable to a country. Democracy means that people can choose a different direction, even when powerful interests would prefer them not to. And when a leader's international allies are influential enough to shape political conversations far beyond their own borders, rejecting that leader's political project can carry significance well beyond one country's elections.
## Hope Is Not the Same as Victory
I do not want to pretend that Lula's victory solves Brazil's problems. Almost half the voters supported his opponent, and Lula will have to govern a deeply divided country while dealing with economic pressures, public concerns about crime and a conservative Congress. Nor does one election reverse the global rise of the far right. The forces behind it, including economic insecurity, cultural anxieties, misinformation and distrust of established institutions, will not simply disappear because one candidate has lost. But I think it is important to recognise moments when things could have gone differently and did. We spend so much time watching the world move in directions that leave us frustrated and worried that it becomes easy to believe resistance is pointless. This election offers a reminder that it is not. The Amazon still has a chance. Brazil still has a chance to pursue a more independent foreign policy. And the people who want a different political future have reason to keep fighting for it. I am happy about this result not because I expect Lula to be perfect, but because I would rather live in a world where leaders can be challenged, forests can be protected and voters can change the course of their country than one where power keeps consolidating itself in the same hands. Sometimes, an election does not give us the future we want. It simply prevents the future we fear from becoming inevitable. That, in itself, is worth celebrating.
