A couple of days ago, I wrote about the growing political challenges facing Donald Trump ahead of the American midterm elections. I also mentioned how the political tide seemed to be shifting against the right in different parts of the world. Israel has now added another interesting chapter to that discussion. The election held on 27 October has left Benjamin Netanyahu without a clear path to a parliamentary majority. His political bloc is projected to hold 53 seats, while the opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot has 48. Neither comes close to the 61 seats required to form a government in the 120-member Knesset. What makes this particularly interesting is that the Arab parties have emerged with approximately 19 seats, giving them a potentially decisive role in determining who governs Israel. Netanyahu has not necessarily been defeated in the sense that his political opponents have already formed a government, but his ability to continue governing is now seriously in question. And just when I thought the broader political picture was becoming easier to read, Israel has made it considerably more complicated.
## The Arab Parties Could Decide What Happens Next
This is perhaps the most significant development of the election. Israel’s Arab parties have gained unprecedented parliamentary influence, and their leaders are now openly discussing supporting an opposition government led by Eisenkot. Ahmad Tibi, who leads the Joint List’s parliamentary faction, has said they are prepared to support a minority government without demanding ministerial positions. Their stated priorities include addressing crime in Arab communities and stopping the demolition of Bedouin homes. But there is a complication: Eisenkot’s opposition bloc is not ideologically uniform. It includes parties with considerably different political positions, and some of its leaders have previously ruled out forming a government with Arab parties. Eisenkot himself has been cautious about committing to such an arrangement. So we have a rather unusual situation: Arab parties may be willing to help remove Netanyahu from power, but the very politicians who need their support are divided over whether they can accept it. I find this particularly revealing because Arab citizens make up a substantial part of Israel’s population, yet their political participation is frequently treated as a threat by the very people seeking to govern the country. Netanyahu has already accused Arab parties of benefiting from voter fraud without presenting evidence of systematic electoral wrongdoing. The question now is whether the opposition can move beyond its internal divisions and acknowledge that these parliamentary seats represent millions of citizens whose votes matter just as much as anyone else’s.
## The Right’s Political Future Is More Complicated Than It Looks
I would be careful about declaring that Netanyahu has already lost or that the global right-wing movement is collapsing. Israel’s election has produced a political deadlock, not a completed transfer of power. Netanyahu could still attempt to attract defectors, negotiate a unity government or find another route to the 61 seats he needs. Eisenkot faces the opposite problem: even with potential Arab support, he must convince his own coalition partners that working with Arab parties is politically acceptable. There is also the possibility that negotiations fail and Israel eventually faces another election. But the developments are significant. Netanyahu’s political survival has become dependent on complicated negotiations, while Arab parties have demonstrated that they cannot simply be ignored in the formation of a government. When I wrote yesterday about the possibility of a wider political shift against the right, I perhaps made it sound more straightforward than it really is. The reality is messier. Winning votes, forming governments and actually changing political direction are three different things. Still, between Brazil’s election, the growing challenges facing Trump in the United States and Israel’s current political deadlock, there is plenty happening to suggest that the international political landscape is far from settled. Whether these developments eventually produce lasting changes is something we will have to watch. For now, Israel’s next government may depend on political groups that Netanyahu has spent considerable effort portraying as illegitimate.
