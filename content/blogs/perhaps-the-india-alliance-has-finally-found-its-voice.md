I watched the INDIA alliance march on November 1, and the scale of the gathering was something else. The flags, the crowd, the different political parties sharing the same platform, and the energy surrounding Rahul Gandhi's arrival made it feel considerably more organised than the opposition demonstrations we have seen in the past. I remember the CJP protests too. They were intense, spontaneous and important in bringing public attention to the concerns surrounding the Election Commission. But when the INDIA alliance came together, it felt different. There was a sense of collective political organisation that had been missing for a long time. Of course, the exact attendance figures have become another political argument, with different outlets and parties offering conflicting estimates. But even allowing for that, the gathering was substantial. What interested me most was not simply how many people turned up, but how many different political organisations managed to occupy the same space and communicate a common demand. For an alliance that has often struggled to demonstrate unity beyond parliamentary meetings and election arrangements, that was significant.
## An Opposition That Finally Looks Organised
One of the biggest problems with the INDIA alliance has always been its leadership. It was an alliance of parties with different ideologies, regional interests, political ambitions and, occasionally, conflicting expectations. Everyone wanted to defeat the BJP, but there was never a convincing answer to the question of who would lead that effort. It often felt like a group of political parties negotiating with one another rather than a collective opposition capable of taking a common message to the public. Rahul Gandhi's increasing visibility has begun to change that perception. He has become the most recognisable face of the opposition's campaign against the voter-roll revision and the functioning of the Election Commission. The November rally brought together leaders such as Akhilesh Yadav, Mamata Banerjee, Tejashwi Yadav and several Left leaders, but Rahul was clearly the central figure. I am not particularly a fan of the Congress, and I don't think the alliance should become another Congress-led political arrangement where smaller parties are expected to follow instructions. But leadership and political dominance are not necessarily the same thing. An alliance can have a recognisable leader while still allowing its constituents to retain their identities and political independence. Perhaps that is what the INDIA bloc has been missing.
## CJP Started Something, But the Alliance Has a Different Reach
I also think it is important not to overlook the contribution of the CJP protests. They helped turn public attention towards issues that might otherwise have remained confined to political discussions and parliamentary statements. Their demonstrations brought students, activists and ordinary citizens into the conversation, and their willingness to protest despite facing restrictions gave the movement a different kind of energy. The INDIA alliance's mobilisation, however, brings something else to the table: political infrastructure. It has national parties, state-level organisations, elected representatives, workers and the ability to coordinate demonstrations across different regions. The two forms of mobilisation should not be treated as competitors. In fact, the growing overlap between civil society groups, student activists and opposition parties could make the movement more effective. Reporting after the rally also showed that people were raising concerns beyond electoral rolls, including inflation, pollution and women's safety. That is important because a political movement cannot survive indefinitely on a single slogan. It needs to connect its central demand to the everyday concerns of people. The challenge is to ensure that organised political leadership does not overshadow the citizens and independent groups who helped bring these issues into public view in the first place.
## What Happens After the Crowd Goes Home?
This is where I think the real test begins. A successful rally is not necessarily the beginning of a successful political movement. The INDIA alliance now needs a plan that goes beyond gathering people in Delhi, delivering speeches and demanding the resignation of the Chief Election Commissioner. It needs sustained public engagement, legal scrutiny of the voter-roll revision, state-level mobilisation and a way to keep the different parties working together when their interests inevitably diverge. The Election Commission maintains that the revision is intended to remove duplicate and ineligible entries, while the opposition argues that the exercise risks disenfranchising legitimate voters. Those claims need transparent examination, and anyone whose name has been wrongly removed should have an effective way to challenge it. I don't know what their next move will be, but the November 1 gathering has at least demonstrated that the alliance can organise a common public campaign. Whether it can sustain that unity when the cameras disappear is a much harder question.
For now, I think the most interesting development is that the INDIA alliance appears to have found a more recognisable public voice. Rahul Gandhi has increasingly occupied that space, and the other opposition leaders seem more willing to participate in a coordinated campaign. That does not mean Congress should become the unquestioned centre of the alliance, nor does it mean the other parties should surrender their independence. In fact, the alliance will be stronger if it avoids that mistake. I may not agree with every party or leader involved, but a functioning opposition is an essential part of a democracy. It needs to question institutions, challenge governments and give citizens an alternative political platform. Perhaps the November 1 rally was not a turning point. It may simply have been one large demonstration in a much longer political struggle. But after watching an opposition that often appeared fragmented finally share a stage, I think it is fair to ask whether something has begun to change.
