If you think the AI bubble has burst because of the recent market crashes in China and the United States, I suspect you haven't looked closely enough at how this system has always operated. Every major technological revolution attracts enormous investment, speculation, unrealistic expectations and, eventually, a correction. Investors rush to put their money into whatever they believe will define the future, valuations shoot up, companies compete to build infrastructure and everyone wants a piece of the next big thing. Eventually, reality catches up with expectations. Some companies collapse, some investors lose fortunes and the market corrects itself. We have seen this happen repeatedly, and AI isn't magically exempt from the process. In China, the STAR 50 index, which includes major technology and semiconductor companies, lost around 40% of its value in less than four months, wiping out approximately $480 billion in market capitalisation. In the US, the November sell-off pushed the Nasdaq into correction territory, with semiconductor and AI-related stocks particularly affected. Investors are questioning whether the enormous expenditure on data centres, chips and computing infrastructure will actually generate sufficient returns. These are legitimate concerns, but a correction in the stock market doesn't mean the underlying technology has failed. Sometimes the market is simply correcting the price people are willing to pay for a future that hasn't arrived yet.
## The System Has Always Worked This Way
Think about how technological revolutions develop. Railways, electricity, automobiles, telecommunications and the internet all attracted enormous investment. Everyone wanted to participate because nobody wanted to miss the next industrial revolution. Capital flooded into industries, companies expanded aggressively, infrastructure was built and valuations often became detached from reality. Eventually, weaker businesses disappeared and investors realised that not every company involved in a revolutionary technology would become successful. But the technology itself continued developing. The internet didn't disappear when technology stocks crashed in 2000. In fact, the infrastructure, knowledge and businesses that survived helped create the digital economy we depend on today. The same possibility exists with AI. We might see companies collapse, valuations fall dramatically and infrastructure projects get cancelled, but that doesn't mean artificial intelligence will suddenly become irrelevant. The recent correction in China reflects excessive valuations, export restrictions and concerns about demand, while the US sell-off has also exposed questions about infrastructure expenditure, financing and political scrutiny. These are different pressures operating within the same broader investment cycle. I wouldn't call every crash healthy, because people lose real money and jobs, but corrections are part of how speculative markets eventually distinguish sustainable businesses from those surviving on enthusiasm alone.
## The Dot-com Bubble: A Much More Dangerous Comparison?
The most obvious comparison is the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s. Back then, the internet was transforming the world, and investors were convinced that almost every company with a website would become enormously valuable. Businesses with little revenue, no sustainable business models and sometimes barely any customers were receiving extraordinary valuations simply because they were associated with the internet. Telecommunications companies borrowed heavily and spent billions laying fibre-optic cables, anticipating demand that never materialised at the expected speed. Some estimates put utilisation of the newly installed fibre capacity below 3% at the height of the oversupply. When confidence collapsed, the Nasdaq eventually lost approximately 78% of its value from its 2000 peak to its 2002 low. Thousands of companies disappeared, employees lost their jobs and investors lost fortunes. But look at where we are today. The internet didn't fail. The infrastructure survived, demand eventually caught up and companies such as Amazon and Google became central to the modern economy. AI has some similarities, particularly in the enormous infrastructure expenditure and the tendency to value future possibilities before profits arrive. However, there is an important difference: today's largest AI investors include highly profitable companies with established businesses, substantial cash flows and existing customers. Microsoft, Google, Amazon and Meta aren't speculative internet startups trying to convince investors that somebody might eventually use their websites. They already operate enormous digital businesses and are investing in AI as an extension of them. That doesn't mean their investments will necessarily generate adequate returns, but the financial foundations are considerably stronger than those of many dot-com companies.
## AI Is Not the Bubble. The Expectations Might Be.
I don't think the right question is whether AI will survive a market crash. It will. The more interesting question is how many of the companies and investments currently associated with AI will survive the correction. We are undoubtedly seeing excessive expectations, questionable valuations, circular investment arrangements and enormous infrastructure spending. Some companies will discover that their business models simply don't work, and some data centres might never generate the returns their investors expect. There is also the possibility that the technology develops faster than expected but becomes considerably cheaper, making today's expensive infrastructure and business models less profitable. That's a genuine risk. But AI is already being used in software development, scientific research, education, medicine, manufacturing and countless other fields. Its commercial applications are real, even if the long-term economic returns remain uncertain. The dot-com crash didn't end the internet; it helped separate the companies building something useful from those selling a fantasy. We might be heading towards a similar period of consolidation in AI. So, no, I wouldn't dismiss the current market correction, nor would I tell anyone that every AI company is worth its valuation. But declaring the entire AI revolution dead because investors have started questioning their returns is rather premature. The market can crash, companies can fail and valuations can collapse. Technology, however, doesn't have to follow the stock market. And perhaps that's the distinction we should be paying attention to.
