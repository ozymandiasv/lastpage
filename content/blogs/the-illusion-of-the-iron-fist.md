I was scrolling through Reddit yesterday when I came across an argument that has become increasingly common in discussions about developing economies: if a country genuinely wants to develop, it should abandon the complications of democracy and simply follow China's model. And honestly, I find this argument rather amusing. People look at China's high-speed railways, enormous infrastructure projects and modern cities and conclude that authoritarianism must be the secret ingredient. Apparently, all we need is one powerful leader who can make decisions without having to answer questions. But what about the countless authoritarian governments across Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa that centralised power, silenced opposition and still managed to deliver decades of stagnation? Strongmen aren't particularly difficult to find. Competent institutions are. The problem with this argument is that it confuses the ability to make decisions quickly with the ability to make the right decisions. Democracy can be slow, frustrating and unnecessarily complicated, but removing public accountability doesn't automatically make a government competent. It simply makes it harder to question when it gets things wrong.

## The Myth of the Enlightened Dictator

I understand why people find the idea attractive. We've all watched infrastructure projects get delayed, permissions get stuck in government offices and politicians spend more time arguing than actually doing anything. At some point, you begin wondering whether someone should simply take charge and get things done. But China's development wasn't just the result of one omniscient government sitting in Beijing and planning everything for the next forty years. A considerable part of its success came from allowing local governments to experiment, compete and adapt policies to their own circumstances while working towards broader national objectives. There was central direction, certainly, but there was also considerable regional experimentation. And India has examples of this too. Look at states like Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra, which have developed substantial industrial ecosystems within a democratic and constitutional framework. We don't need to abolish democracy to understand that states can compete with one another, attract investment and build industries. In fact, India already spent decades following a heavily centralised economic model before the 1991 liberalisation reforms. For nearly four decades, industrial licensing, import restrictions and excessive state control limited competition and made starting and expanding businesses unnecessarily difficult. We called it self-reliance, but much of it resulted in inefficiency and economic stagnation. Apparently, central planning isn't quite as magical as some people would like us to believe.

## Not All Corruption Is the Same

Another thing I find interesting is how casually we discuss corruption as though every form of it produces the same economic consequences. Let me be clear: corruption is corruption, and there is no reason to romanticise it. But its economic impact can vary depending on where it occurs and how it affects institutions. In some East Asian economies, powerful business groups and political elites developed close relationships with governments, sometimes involving preferential treatment, political favours and enormous financial benefits. Yet those relationships were also, in certain cases, tied to export performance, industrial expansion and measurable economic outcomes. That doesn't make the corruption acceptable, but it helps explain why some countries managed to industrialise despite it. India's problem is not limited to corruption at the top. It is also the everyday administrative friction that ordinary people and businesses encounter. A small business owner struggling to obtain a licence, a farmer trying to access a government office or someone simply attempting to get a basic document approved can spend days navigating unnecessary bureaucracy. These small acts of extraction accumulate into a much larger economic burden. And perhaps our culture of political hero-worship doesn't help either. We treat politicians like feudal lords, celebrate them for performing basic administrative responsibilities and then act surprised when they begin believing they are above accountability. A government employee approving a permit isn't doing you a favour. A politician building a road isn't doing you a personal favour. These are responsibilities, and we should stop treating governance like a gift from our rulers.

## The Soil We Keep Overlooking

There is another problem with blindly copying China's development model: the world is changing. For decades, several East Asian economies benefited from labour-intensive manufacturing, cheap exports and the ability to absorb enormous numbers of workers into industrial employment. But today, automation and artificial intelligence are changing the economics of manufacturing. India cannot simply assume that the same industrial pathway will remain available indefinitely. We need to think about what we actually have and what we can build around it. One of our biggest assets is our agricultural economy, yet we continue to treat agriculture almost exclusively as a sector that needs to shrink as the country develops. I think we are overlooking an enormous opportunity. Modernising agriculture, investing in irrigation, improving storage infrastructure, developing food-processing industries and building efficient supply chains could create substantial economic value. Food security and agricultural supply chains will also become increasingly important as climate change puts pressure on global food production. This doesn't mean we should abandon manufacturing or discourage people from moving into other sectors. Quite the opposite. We need advanced manufacturing, research, technology and skilled employment. But why should we assume that agriculture has no meaningful role in a technologically advanced economy? We could be exporting processed agricultural products, developing better food technologies and building industries around what we already produce. Our agricultural base need not be a burden. With the right investment, it could become one of our greatest economic advantages.

## We Don't Need an Iron Fist

I don't think India needs an authoritarian government to develop. It needs a government that can actually govern. We need functioning institutions, predictable regulations, less bureaucratic interference, better education, stronger local administration and politicians who understand that public office is not a personal inheritance. Democracy isn't an excuse for inefficiency, and accountability shouldn't be treated as an obstacle to development. We can learn from China without pretending that everything China has achieved is the result of authoritarianism. We can learn from Singapore, South Korea, Japan and other economies without copying their entire political systems. Every country has its own circumstances, history and challenges. And perhaps that is what bothers me about these endless comparisons. We spend so much time arguing about whether India should become China that we forget to ask why our own institutions aren't functioning as they should. I don't want a country where one person can make every decision without being questioned. I want a country where institutions are competent enough that decisions don't depend on one person in the first place. The real achievement isn't having a leader powerful enough to silence everyone. It is building a system that works even when the leader changes. That is the kind of development worth pursuing.
