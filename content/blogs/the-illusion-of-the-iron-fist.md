I was scrolling through a Reddit thread yesterday when I stumbled upon a claim that has become maddeningly common in economic discourse: if a developing nation truly wants to rise, it must abandon the messy friction of democracy and copy China. It is an enticingly simple narrative, built on images of high-speed rail networks and gleaming skylines assembled overnight. But it utterly ignores the long, grim list of authoritarian regimes across Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa that centralized power, silenced dissent, and managed only to deliver generational stagnation. Strongmen are easy to come by; functioning, inclusive institutions are rare.

## The Myth of the Enlightened Dictator

I understand the temptation. When you watch administrative delays stall basic infrastructure for years, there is a visceral urge to champion a heavy hand to cut through the noise. But China’s economic miracle was never simply the product of a single, omniscient command center handing down forty-year decrees. In reality, much of its momentum came from a form of organized improvisation—broad targets set at the center, with local officials given the leeway to compete, experiment, and adapt to ground realities.

Indian states like Tamil Nadu and Maharashtra have demonstrated a remarkably similar brand of regional industrial dynamism for decades, operating entirely within a constitutional framework. We often forget that India already tried the central planning, hyper-regulated route. For nearly forty years prior to the 1991 reforms, the state shielded domestic industries under the guise of self-reliance. It did not create global competitiveness; it gave us waiting lists for basic goods and decades of economic inertia.

## Not All Graft Is Created Equal

There is also a lazy tendency to flatten corruption into a single monolith. To be clear, corruption is a drain on human potential wherever it exists. But economically speaking, the nature of the theft matters. East Asia’s rise was accompanied by elite-level graft—deals struck at the top that were nevertheless tied to concrete infrastructure output and industrial scaling. The pie was raided, but the bridges still got built.

In India, we suffer from a far more insidious strain: predatory petty corruption. It is the systemic friction felt at every village office and municipal bureau, where small business owners and farmers are squeezed daily just to obtain basic permits. This is not grand statecraft; it is administrative micro-parasitism, fueled in part by a culture of hero-worship that treats political leaders like feudal lords rather than accountable public servants. You cannot build a dynamic economy when small enterprise is suffocated before it can take its first breath.

## The Soil We Overlook

As automation and artificial intelligence close the traditional door on low-cost, export-led manufacturing, India cannot simply replay the 20th-century East Asian playbook. The path forward demands a clearer look at what we actually possess. Our real competitive leverage does not lie in trying to build labor-intensive sweatshops just as robotics are making them obsolete; it lies in modernizing our vast agrarian economy and scaling agro-processing for a world rapidly heading toward food supply instability.

I know standard economic doctrine insists that a nation must migrate its population away from the land at all costs to modernize. But in the coming decades, food sovereignty and high-value agricultural processing will carry far more geopolitical weight than we currently admit. Coupled with targeted upskilling for high-value technical sectors, our agrarian base isn't a backward relic holding us back—it may well be the bedrock we are foolishly overlooking. The real question is not whether we need an iron fist to force growth, but whether we have the patience to build the institutions that actually sustain it.
