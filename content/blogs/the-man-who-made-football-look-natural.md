I have never seen a sportsperson or footballer, who feels as natural as Messi. You can have Ronaldo Nazário, who could score goals in ways that looked impossible, or Maradona, who could control a game and create things nobody else could see. But to be both at the same time, to be the goalscorer and the playmaker, the dribbler and the creator, the person who can decide a game by finishing it or by creating it, that is where Messi has always felt different. Watching him never felt like watching someone execute a skill. It felt almost accidental. He would receive the ball, take a few touches, move through two or three players and suddenly the entire shape of the game had changed. You knew what he was going to do and still somehow could not stop him.
And today, in his final game for Argentina, he did exactly what Messi does. Argentina beat Benin 3–0, Messi assisted Nicolás Otamendi, assisted Nico Paz and then scored the third himself from the penalty spot. He was named Man of the Match. At 39, in his final appearance for the country, he was still directly involved in all three goals. I don’t think you could have written a more Messi ending without making it feel fictional.
## And Then There Was That 10th Minute
I genuinely don’t think I have ever seen anything quite like the 10th-minute ovation. The game stopped because Messi wears number 10, and the entire stadium stood up for him. More than 85,000 people at the Monumental applauding the man who had carried that shirt for so many years. There were fireworks, chants, tears and that strange feeling you get when everyone in a stadium knows they are watching something that will never happen again.
And then, as if the whole thing wasn’t emotional enough, he simply went back to playing football. That is perhaps what I love most about Messi. The ceremony can happen around him, the world can stop for a moment, but give him a ball and he is still just Messi. A corner that becomes an assist. A little pass into space that becomes another assist. A penalty into the corner. And somewhere in between, those little touches, the movement, the dribbling, the way he still seems to glide rather than run. I have watched that magic for years and I still don’t completely understand how he does it. There are moments when the ball seems attached to him, as though it has forgotten that it is supposed to obey physics.
## I Feel Bad for Ronaldo
And somehow, Messi’s retirement also makes me think about Ronaldo. I never liked Cristiano as much as Messi, but I still feel bad about the way his ending has unfolded. I remember thinking for a long time that his retirement was inevitable, especially after Messi stepped away from international football. Ronaldo kept going, almost as if he wanted to prove that he could outlast even that final chapter of their rivalry. Somewhere along the way, though, I think he deserved a better ending, or perhaps he should have chosen one for himself earlier. His touch had been fading for the last few years, even before his second United spell, and now people are reducing an extraordinary career to the jokes surrounding its final stretch. That feels unfair. He deserved better than that.
But Messi leaves differently. He leaves the Argentina shirt after one final performance in which he was still the centre of everything. He leaves as the country’s record appearance maker and scorer, after 208 matches and 126 goals, and after finally giving Argentina the World Cup that had eluded him for so long. I am not even going to start counting his trophies here. Manu can do that for us. Maybe sometime later, I will sit down and try to understand the numbers properly. Today I don’t really care about the numbers.
## I Just Want to Remember the Magic
Because what I will remember is the football. The little man picking up the ball and somehow finding a way through. The dribbles that looked like somebody had edited the footage. The impossible passes that seemed to appear from nowhere. The goals where the goalkeeper knew exactly what was coming and still couldn’t reach it. The ridiculous free kicks. The nights when Barcelona seemed to be losing and you somehow felt that if Messi had the ball, the match wasn’t over. And then Argentina, a team that had spent years carrying the weight of expectation, finally winning with him at the centre of it all.
There have been brilliant footballers before Messi, and there will be brilliant footballers after him. But I don’t know if there will be another player who makes the entire game look so instinctive. He never looked like someone trying to become the greatest. He looked like a boy who simply understood the ball better than everyone else and never really stopped. Today he walked onto the pitch for Argentina one last time, received a standing ovation in the 10th minute, created two goals, scored another and walked away with the Man of the Match award.
What an emotion. What a game. What a way to say goodbye.
I think I saw magic. And for more than twenty years, I was lucky enough to watch it happen.
