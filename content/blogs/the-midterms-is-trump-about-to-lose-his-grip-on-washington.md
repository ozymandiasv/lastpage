Before understanding the midterms, it helps to understand how the American political system works. The US Congress is the country’s legislature, responsible for making federal laws, approving budgets, investigating the government and exercising checks on the President. It has two chambers: the House of Representatives and the Senate. The House has 435 voting members, called Representatives, elected from districts according to population. They serve two-year terms, so every seat is contested every two years. The Senate has 100 members, with two representing each state regardless of population. Senators serve six-year terms, with roughly one-third of the seats contested every two years. If you are familiar with Indian politics, think of the House as somewhat similar to the Lok Sabha and the Senate as somewhat similar to the Rajya Sabha. Both systems have two legislative chambers, but the comparison is not exact: America’s Senate is directly elected, and its members have much greater independence from state governments than India’s Rajya Sabha members. Together, these two chambers form Congress, and both have to agree on federal legislation. A President may occupy the White House, but he cannot simply make every decision without dealing with Congress.  
## What Exactly Is a Midterm Election?
The United States holds presidential elections every four years, but congressional elections happen every two years. The election that takes place halfway through a President’s four-year term is called the midterm election. On 3 November 2026, Americans will vote for all 435 seats in the House and approximately one-third of the Senate. This is why midterms are so important: voters get an opportunity to change the political balance of Congress without waiting for the next presidential election. Historically, the President’s party often loses congressional seats during midterms. There is a fairly simple reason: presidential elections generate enormous enthusiasm, but once a leader starts governing, the consequences of their decisions become visible. Inflation, employment, foreign policy, immigration, healthcare and public spending all become electoral issues. Voters who supported a President may become disappointed, while those who opposed him become more motivated to vote. In that sense, midterms often become a referendum on the President’s performance. Every President fears them because losing even one chamber can make governing considerably harder.  
## Why the Numbers Look Dangerous for Trump
The numbers heading into this election are particularly uncomfortable for Trump. A Reuters/Ipsos poll conducted on 26 October put his approval rating at just 32 per cent. On the generic congressional ballot, which asks voters whether they would prefer a Democratic or Republican candidate for Congress without naming individual candidates, Democrats led Republicans 47 to 36 per cent among registered voters. Among likely voters, the Democratic advantage was 53 to 44 per cent. Another polling average published on 28 October placed the Democratic lead at nearly ten percentage points. Even more interestingly, 73 per cent of respondents in the Reuters/Ipsos poll said the cost of living was moving in the wrong direction. These are worrying indicators for a governing party. The Democrats appear to have considerable momentum, and the possibility of losing the House is very real. However, winning the Senate is a more difficult proposition because only a portion of its seats are contested, and the particular states holding elections matter enormously. I would be careful about declaring a Democratic victory before the votes are counted, but the political environment is clearly unfavourable to Trump. There is also a broader feeling that the political tide is shifting against the hard-right populist movement. I would describe it as a growing anti-Trump coalition rather than assume that every Democratic voter is part of a unified left-wing movement.  
## Can Trump Actually Be Impeached?
Yes. And this is where losing Congress could become particularly consequential for Trump. The House of Representatives has the constitutional power to impeach a President. It can approve articles of impeachment with a simple majority, effectively bringing formal charges against him. The Senate then conducts the trial. However, impeachment does not automatically remove a President from office. Removal requires a two-thirds majority of Senators present to vote for conviction. Trump has already been impeached twice during his first presidency, in 2019 and 2021, but the Senate acquitted him on both occasions. If Democrats win control of the House in November, they could launch investigations, subpoena officials, scrutinise administration decisions and potentially begin another impeachment process. They could also block or significantly complicate Trump’s legislative agenda. But even if they control the House, removing him would be extremely difficult without substantial Republican support in the Senate. This is an important distinction: impeachment is a political and constitutional process, not an automatic consequence of losing an election. Still, a hostile House could make the remaining two years of Trump’s presidency very uncomfortable.  
## Is the Political Tide Finally Turning?
What interests me most is the wider international picture. We have seen political resistance emerge in different forms across countries, from Brazil’s rejection of the Bolsonaro political project to growing protests against Netanyahu’s government in Israel. These are very different political situations, and I would not put them all under one ideological label. But there is a common question running through them: how much power should an individual leader be allowed to accumulate, and how long can political movements built around one personality sustain themselves? Trump’s influence extends well beyond American borders. His relationships with political leaders, his approach to international institutions and his ability to shape political conversations have consequences for countries that never voted for him. That is why these midterms matter internationally, too. If Democrats take the House, Trump will face a legislature that can investigate his administration and challenge his decisions. If they also manage to win the Senate, the consequences could be even greater. I am not suggesting that the global left has suddenly won or that the far right is disappearing. Political movements do not vanish overnight, and elections can reverse direction just as quickly. But I do think there is something significant happening. Perhaps voters are becoming increasingly uncomfortable with leaders who treat political power as personal property. On 3 November, Americans will get an opportunity to express that dissatisfaction. And unlike a presidential election, this time they do not have to wait until 2028 to make their voices heard.
