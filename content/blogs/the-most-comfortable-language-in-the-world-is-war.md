Wars and boasting seem to be becoming more constant than I remember. I went and checked the numbers, since I couldn't tell if this was just the feeling that creeps in from reading too much news. Turns out it wasn't far off. ACLED recorded more than 185,000 violent events around the world in 2025, nearly twice what it recorded in 2021, with more than 56,000 incidents specifically targeting civilians.
And the strange thing is how normal the language around all of this has become. One country hits back, another answers that, someone claims a successful operation, someone else threatens consequences, and everyone just waits for the next statement. Yesterday it was Ukraine and Russia. Today there are warnings between Iran and the United States. Every government explains itself, every military action gets its justification, and each side always seems to have a reason the next escalation had to happen. And underneath all that language, somewhere, are people who just don't wake up anymore.
## The War That Keeps Answering Itself
Ukraine and Russia might be the clearest example of this cycle right now. Russia attacks Ukraine, Ukraine retaliates against Russia, Russia responds to the retaliation, and the retaliation becomes the justification for another response. Each action gets explained by whatever came right before it, until the chain gets so long that nobody's really asking anymore where it started.
It's hard to miss the latest escalation. On September 20, Ukraine launched what Russian officials called the largest drone attack on Moscow so far, with Russia reporting more than 1,600 drones intercepted nationwide since Saturday, roughly 450 of them headed for Moscow. Three people were reported killed in the Moscow region, and drones struck an oil refinery in the capital. It happened right as Russia was in the middle of its parliamentary elections.
Just hours before that, a Russian drone attack on Ukraine's Kyiv region reportedly killed four civilians, three of them children, according to Ukrainian officials. That is what escalation looks like when you remove the maps, flags and military terminology. One attack becomes the reason for another, and the people underneath both become numbers in someone else's explanation.
## Everyone Has a Reason
And this isn't just a Russia-Ukraine thing. The same language keeps showing up elsewhere. The US has been positioning itself more and more as the one deciding what other countries should or shouldn't do, especially when it comes to Iran. President Donald Trump has warned Iran of economic collapse and even threatened its leadership if Tehran won't make a deal, while still saying he's open to meeting Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. Iran, for its part, has warned that any fresh American attack would bring severe retaliation, and that US allies in the region could end up as targets too.
There's something almost absurd about watching powerful countries lecture each other on restraint while explaining, in the same breath, why their own use of force is necessary. Every side seems to believe it is responding rather than initiating, defending rather than threatening, enforcing rather than escalating. Perhaps this is one of the most useful tricks of war. Nobody has to see themselves as the aggressor. They only have to believe that what they are doing is the unavoidable response to something someone else did first.
## So What Has It Actually Given Us?
Wars obviously achieve things for states. They redraw territory, wipe out military capabilities, shift political calculations and force negotiations that would never happen otherwise. It would be simplistic to pretend that wars accomplish nothing. But when we talk about those achievements, we rarely mention the cost in the same breath. ACLED's latest figures estimate that more than 240,000 people died in conflict-related violence over the 12 months its latest Conflict Index covers. It also puts the number of people exposed to conflict in 2025 at around 831 million, roughly one in six people on the planet. In Ukraine, that human cost just keeps piling up. Recent Russian attacks have killed civilians and wrecked homes and infrastructure, while Ukrainian strikes inside Russia are reaching further, hitting oil facilities and other targets well past the front line. Reuters reported that this latest Ukrainian attack also hit Russian oil production, adding to fuel shortages and pushing prices up.
Then there's the cost that doesn't show up on any battlefield map. Kids growing up who already know what a drone sounds like. Families split apart by borders. Cities that won't be rebuilt for decades. Economies redirected toward weapons. People who survive but will carry the war for the rest of their lives.
## Perhaps We've Gotten Too Comfortable With the Language
Maybe what actually bothers me isn't even that wars exist. There's never been a stretch of human history without them. It's how comfortable we've gotten talking about them. We label strikes successful or unsuccessful. We call retaliation strong or weak. We celebrate when a missile reaches its target and condemn it when ours gets intercepted. We turn destruction into statistics and statistics into arguments. But something's missing from that language. The person who happened to be inside that building. The child who happened to be in the wrong house. The family still waiting for someone who's never coming home.
Maybe that's why I keep landing on the same question. What are we actually winning? A government can win territory. A military can take out an enemy installation. A president can show strength. A country can say it defended its interests. All of that can be true at once. And still, when it's over, there are piles of bodies. In 2025 alone, ACLED recorded more than 185,000 violent events worldwide, over 56,000 of them targeting civilians, and more than 240,000 conflict-related deaths in the 12 months its latest assessment covers. In Ukraine, the war rolls into another year of retaliation and counter-retaliation, while the US and Iran keep trading threats that risk another escalation. For all the victories, warnings, retaliations and demonstrations of strength, the most undeniable product of war remains the same. Human beings who will never get to go home.
