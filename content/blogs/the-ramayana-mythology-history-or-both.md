With the release of the new Ramayana film, there has been another round of arguments about whether the Ramayana is mythology or history. But I think we should begin with a rather simple question: when we discuss history, which parts of the Ramayana are we actually considering historical? People possessing extraordinary powers, flying through the sky, travelling in flying vehicles, transforming themselves or appearing as beings several times larger than ordinary humans? If we are examining these accounts as literal historical events, we run into an obvious problem. There is no credible scientific or archaeological evidence for such things, and several of these descriptions are incompatible with what we understand about the physical world. You are, of course, free to believe whatever you wish, but belief and historical evidence are two different things. That doesn't mean the Ramayana has no historical value. In fact, I think the more interesting discussion begins when we stop trying to prove every extraordinary event and start examining the human world behind the story. There is a distinction between historicity, which concerns whether something actually happened, and historical relevance, which concerns what a text can reveal about the society, geography and people of the period in which it developed.
## What Does the Archaeology Actually Tell Us?
Let's begin with Ayodhya. Archaeological excavations have established that the region has a substantial history of human settlement. Excavations at Ayodhya and nearby sites have uncovered material associated with different phases of ancient settlement, including Northern Black Polished Ware, a distinctive pottery tradition associated with early historic urbanisation in northern India. Archaeologist B.B. Lal, who led excavations at several sites traditionally associated with the Ramayana, discussed evidence suggesting that Ayodhya's earliest identifiable archaeological occupation might date to around the seventh century BCE. Other sites associated with the epic, including locations around the Ganga and Sarayu river systems, also have archaeological histories worth investigating. But here is where we must be careful. Finding evidence of an ancient settlement at Ayodhya does not establish that Rama lived there, that Dasharatha ruled it or that the events described in the epic took place. Archaeology can establish that people lived in a place, what they ate, what they made, how they built their settlements and how their societies changed. It cannot simply identify a particular king or battle because a later literary text associates that location with them. So far, there is no independently verified contemporary archaeological or inscriptional evidence establishing the Ramayana's central characters, the exile, the abduction of Sita or the war in Lanka as historical events. The absence of such evidence does not prove that no historical incident ever inspired the story, but it certainly means we cannot present the epic as a documented account of those events.
## The Society Behind the Story
Now, this is where I find the Ramayana genuinely fascinating from a historical perspective. The text gives us an opportunity to examine ideas about kingship, family, marriage, inheritance, political legitimacy, social hierarchy and the concept of dharma. It presents an idealised image of a ruler, the responsibilities of a king, the relationship between a sovereign and his subjects, and the expectations placed upon members of a royal family. Its descriptions of forests, rivers, settlements and journeys also preserve geographical knowledge and memories of how ancient communities understood the Indian subcontinent. The encounters between royal kingdoms and forest-dwelling communities are particularly interesting, although we should not automatically assume that every group described as Vanaras represents a specific historical tribe. Such interpretations require independent evidence. We must also remember that the Ramayana was not composed as a modern historical document. The Sanskrit epic developed over a long period, with scholars generally placing the formation of its central portions broadly between 750 and 500 BCE, while recognising that its oral traditions may be older and that the text continued to develop afterwards. The Bala Kanda and Uttara Kanda are generally considered to contain substantial later material, although the exact chronology and development of individual passages remain subjects of scholarship. This means the society reflected in one portion of the text need not correspond perfectly to the society reflected in another. Rather than treating the entire epic as a photograph of one particular historical period, we can examine it as a literary work that preserves, reshapes and sometimes idealises the social and political ideas of different generations.
## A Story That Travelled Beyond India
There is another historical dimension that I think deserves considerably more attention: the Ramayana's influence beyond the Indian subcontinent. The story travelled across Southeast Asia and was adapted into different cultural traditions in Thailand, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos and other regions. Thailand has its Ramakien, Cambodia has its Reamker, and Indonesia developed its own distinctive versions through literature, dance and theatre. These weren't simply copies of an Indian text. They incorporated local customs, political ideas, artistic traditions and religious influences. The story became part of royal court culture, temple architecture, sculpture, painting, dance and performance across the region. Some of the earliest surviving monumental visual representations of Ramayana narratives in India date to temple reliefs from around the sixth to eighth centuries CE. This is a particularly valuable historical record because we can examine the actual objects, their locations, their artistic styles and the political environments in which they were produced. Even if we cannot establish that every event in the original story happened, we can establish beyond doubt that the Ramayana existed as a powerful cultural and literary tradition that shaped societies for well over a millennium. That, in itself, is an extraordinary historical legacy.
Perhaps there were historical people, kingdoms, conflicts or migrations that contributed to the formation of the story. Oral traditions can preserve distant memories, and literary works can transform real experiences into something much larger than their original circumstances. But unless we find independent evidence, we should not turn that possibility into historical certainty. I don't think the Ramayana needs to be proven as a literal account of supernatural events to deserve its place in Indian history. Its literary, cultural, geographical and sociological significance is already enormous. We can study it, question it, appreciate its influence and examine the society it reflects without having to accept every description as historical fact. And I think we should apply the same curiosity to our other great epic. With the Mahabharata, things get particularly interesting because there are different archaeological discussions surrounding its geographical settings, ancient settlements, clans and possible historical memories. There is more to explore there, and perhaps we will discuss that another day.
