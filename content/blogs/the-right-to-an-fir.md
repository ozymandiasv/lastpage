It took Rahul Gandhi, the sitting Leader of Opposition, around eight hours of protest to get an FIR registered over the alleged pellet-gun injury suffered by a young man during the CJP’s “Sansad Chalo” protest. Gandhi first went to the Mandir Marg Police Station and later to the DCP office at Parliament Street, where he sat on a dharna demanding that the complaint be registered. The young man had suffered a serious eye injury and reportedly lost his eyesight. The police initially denied the use of pellet guns, while later accounts and official records indicated that non-lethal munitions, including plastic pellets, had been used during the protest. Rahul Gandhi also alleged that police officials had told him they were under pressure from higher authorities not to register the FIR. That remains an allegation and should be investigated, but the fact that it apparently took hours of protest to get an FIR registered is disturbing enough. An FIR is not a favour from the police. If a citizen approaches the police with a complaint alleging a cognisable offence, the complaint should be dealt with according to law. It should not depend on whether the person making the complaint is powerful enough to sit outside a police station for eight hours.
The larger issue is what happened during the protest itself. There were allegations of police brutality and of plain-clothes men attacking protesters, while the police also maintained that the protesters had become violent and that officers were attacked. Both claims should be investigated properly. If protesters committed violence, they should face the law. If the police used excessive force or if people were attacked unlawfully, those responsible should also face the law. This is exactly why an independent and proper investigation matters. What concerns me is the possibility that the very institution responsible for receiving a complaint can become the institution that has to be pressured into accepting it. That should never become normal. The presence or absence of Amit Shah in Parliament, and whether he answered the Opposition’s questions, is ultimately a political issue. But the FIR is a legal issue. These two things should not be confused.
## Our Right Is Not a Political Favour
The entire episode should be an eye-opener, not simply for the Opposition but for all of us. We often talk about our fundamental and lawful rights as though they exist only on paper, but a right becomes meaningful only when an ordinary person can actually exercise it. Today it may be a protester who needs an FIR after being injured. Tomorrow it could be any one of us standing at a police station asking for the law to recognise something that has happened to us. The police should not need political pressure, a sitting Leader of the Opposition, or eight hours of dharna before a lawful complaint is registered. If the allegation is true, investigate it. If it is false, establish that through evidence. But let the process begin. Our lawful rights are our rights. They are not favours given by the government, the police, or anyone in power. An FIR must be filed when the law requires it, regardless of who is making the complaint.
