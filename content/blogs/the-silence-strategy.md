Remember when Prime Minister Narendra Modi dodged a journalist's question during his recent visit to Norway? Unfortunately, that wasn't an isolated moment. Our World Press Freedom Index rank is extremely low: we are currently ranked 157 out of 180 countries, close to countries like Pakistan and Bangladesh. India's decline in this ranking over the years has been significant, and it raises serious questions about how much space journalists actually have to question those in power. What bothers me isn't just a number on a ranking, though. It's the silence when questions are actually asked. I recently watched a journalist question Uttar Pradesh Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath and get nothing back. When someone in power is asked a legitimate question and simply refuses to answer, that silence can say more than any answer would. And silence, it turns out, isn't even the only tactic.
## The Illusion of the Press Conference
When independent journalist Divya Srivastava questioned Yogi Adityanath yesterday about regional infrastructure failures, the Chief Minister chose not to respond. Srivastava then publicly criticised the administration's format, arguing that these press events are built for one-sided statements, not for handling difficult or critical questions from the media.
Yogi Adityanath rarely takes tough, unscripted questions from independent reporters. He sticks to tightly managed media briefings instead. And whenever someone does try to press the administration on major local failures or governance issues, the usual response is to shut the conversation down rather than engage with it. This backs up exactly what Srivastava was pointing to: these official press meets have turned into controlled events for one-way government messaging, not real spaces where the media can hold leaders accountable.
## Deflecting Blame: The Balaghat Playbook
Silence is one way to dodge accountability. Balaghat shows a rougher version of the same instinct: instead of staying quiet, redirect the confrontation onto someone who has no power to answer it in the first place.
Abhijeet Dipke travelled to Balaghat, Madhya Pradesh, to meet families who had lost children to infections that state healthcare failures had left unaddressed. But instead of scrutiny falling on the state government, a group of pro-regime online influencers cornered him on camera. They accused him of exploiting the deaths for politics and demanded to know why he hadn't shown up sooner. It's a strange inversion. Dipke holds no political office and no power over healthcare policy, yet he's the one being grilled, while the real questions, about contaminated water and failing hospitals, sit unanswered at the door of Madhya Pradesh's Chief Minister. Pointing that anger at an independent voice instead of the government responsible does exactly what it's meant to: it keeps the people actually in charge from having to answer for anything.
## The Pattern Underneath
Different tactics, same result. In Lucknow, the Chief Minister just doesn't answer. In Balaghat, the state's supporters make sure the question never reaches the people it was meant for. One way or another, the person asking walks away with nothing, and the person who should be answering never has to. That's what a declining press freedom score looks like in practice. Not one dramatic act of censorship, but power that's slowly stopped feeling obligated to respond at all.
