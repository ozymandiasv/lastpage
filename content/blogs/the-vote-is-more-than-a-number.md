For a long time, I have had a feeling that something around India's electoral system was becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. It was not one particular incident, one election or one political speech. It was the accumulation of questions around voter lists, deletions, SIR and the way the Election Commission was handling them. I even found myself affected by the process when my own name was at risk during SIR. I was fortunate to get it back, but that is almost beside the point. What matters is what happens when millions of people are reduced to entries on a database and the process determining whether those entries remain begins to raise serious questions. Then came today's Indian Express investigation, and suddenly the questions became much harder to dismiss.
## Two Out of Three
The report says Election Commissioners Sukhbir Singh Sandhu and Vivek Joshi, two of the three members of the Election Commission, formally objected at least 14 times over ten months to decisions and processes concerning electoral rolls. These were not minor administrative disagreements. Their objections reportedly concerned new voter registration, deletion and restoration of names, appeals, and the software controlling the electoral roll. One particularly striking issue concerns Form 6, the form used by people registering as new voters. The two Commissioners had reportedly objected to a new requirement connecting new registrations to the previous SIR electoral roll, with Sandhu describing the change as "unauthorised and illegal" and calling for its removal.
Then there is the question of the electoral database itself. The two Commissioners reportedly raised concerns that control over the system was becoming increasingly centralised in Delhi, potentially limiting the ability of local Electoral Registration Officers to carry out their statutory functions. The Indian Express reported a case in Goa where 97 voters had satisfied the local ERO's requirements but the software prevented those decisions from being recorded.
Read that again.
The problem is no longer simply what politicians are saying about the Election Commission. The Election Commission's own members were raising questions about how the machinery of the electoral roll was functioning.
## So What Happened to the Questions?
This is where I find myself going back to Rahul Gandhi's repeated allegations about electoral rolls and what he has called "vote chori". He has held press conferences and publicly presented allegations concerning voter deletions, duplicate voters and the functioning of the electoral process. The Election Commission has rejected his allegations and maintained that its processes are lawful and that the Commission's decisions are taken collectively.
I don't think an allegation should become a fact merely because a politician says it. But neither should a serious allegation disappear simply because it was made by a politician. If the allegations are false, prove them false. If the evidence presented is incorrect, dismantle it point by point. If there are genuine irregularities, investigate them. That is how an institution responsible for conducting elections should respond. What makes today's report different is that the questions are no longer coming exclusively from opposition politicians or commentators. The Indian Express says it examined internal correspondence in which two of the three Commissioners themselves repeatedly raised objections.
## A Paper Response Is Not Enough
The Election Commission has responded to the report by saying that differences and observations are normal during deliberations and that the decisions ultimately taken by the Commission were unanimous. Fine. But then answer the questions.
What happened to the objections? Why were the changes made despite the objections? Who authorised them? Who controlled the software? Why were the Commissioners concerned about centralisation? Why were some communications reportedly being issued without the approval they believed was required? These are not questions that should be answered with a general statement about unanimous decisions. The Election Commission is not an ordinary government department. It is the institution entrusted with something much more fundamental. The mechanism through which citizens choose their government.
## The Numbers Are People
SIR has already affected an enormous number of voters. The Indian Express reports that more than 13 crore names were struck off draft electoral rolls across 30 States and Union Territories during the process. In West Bengal, the Election Commission told the Supreme Court that 27.16 lakh voters had been deleted and that more than 22 lakh subsequently appealed for reinstatement. It is very easy to discuss 13 crore as a statistic. It is much harder when you realise that every number represents somebody's right to vote.
That is why the integrity of the electoral roll matters just as much as the integrity of the voting machine, the counting process or the election result. Democracy does not begin when I enter the polling booth. It begins with the state recognising that I have the right to enter that booth.
## This Is Where I Stand
I don't know whether every allegation made against the Election Commission is true. I don't want to replace one form of blind faith with another. I want evidence. I want answers. I want the institution responsible for protecting the electoral process to be transparent enough that ordinary citizens do not have to choose between believing the government and believing the opposition. But today's report makes something very clear to me.
We should not be looking away. If two of the three members of the Election Commission really did raise repeated objections about decisions affecting voter registration, deletion of names and control of the electoral database, then those objections deserve to be examined publicly and seriously. If the Commission has perfectly reasonable explanations, it should provide them. If the Indian Express has misunderstood the documents, the Commission should demonstrate exactly where.
Because democracy cannot survive on "trust us." It survives when institutions are willing to answer questions. And perhaps this is what I have been feeling all along. This isn't fear. It is an awakening. The vote is not a favour given to us by a government, a political party or an Election Commission. It belongs to the citizen. And if the machinery that determines who gets to exercise that right begins to raise questions of its own, then asking those questions is not cynicism. It is democracy doing what democracy is supposed to do.
