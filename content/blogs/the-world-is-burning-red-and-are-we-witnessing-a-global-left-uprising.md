The world is burning, and it is burning red. I woke up to images of a town hall in France engulfed in flames, thousands of people taking to the streets, and communist flags rising above crowds protesting against deteriorating education, government spending cuts and the rising cost of living. The protests that began around education and public services have expanded into something much bigger, with clashes, arrests and attacks on public buildings accompanying demonstrations across the country. And France is not the only place where discontent has spilled onto the streets. Italy has witnessed violent confrontations during cost-of-living protests, while Albania has seen student-led demonstrations over education, corruption and deteriorating living conditions. In Argentina, students and university workers are challenging Javier Milei’s austerity policies and cuts to public education. Spain is experiencing its own struggles over housing and the cost of living. Chile and Brazil, too, have their own histories of mass mobilisation against economic and political grievances. The United States has witnessed another round of No Kings demonstrations, with crowds protesting against what they consider authoritarian tendencies and calling for greater democratic participation. And in India, people are taking to the streets over the controversial revision of electoral rolls and allegations that eligible voters risk being excluded from the democratic process. These are not all the same protests, nor are they necessarily connected to one another. Yet, when so many societies are experiencing significant public mobilisation within such a short period, it becomes difficult not to wonder whether we are witnessing a broader political moment.
## Why Is Everyone Angry?
Look beyond the immediate triggers, and a pattern begins to emerge. Students are worried about the quality and accessibility of education. Workers are struggling with living costs. Families are finding housing increasingly unaffordable. Public services are under pressure, while governments argue that spending cuts and austerity are necessary. In some countries, the anger is directed at corruption and political privilege; in others, it concerns democratic institutions, electoral fairness or the concentration of executive power. The specific circumstances differ, but the underlying frustration often comes down to a familiar question and who is expected to bear the cost when governments make difficult economic and political decisions? When education budgets are cut, it is students and teachers who feel the consequences. When housing becomes unaffordable, it is ordinary families who struggle to keep a roof over their heads. When living costs rise faster than incomes, people who were already managing carefully find themselves falling behind. And when citizens begin to believe that institutions meant to represent them are no longer listening, their frustration can extend beyond a single policy or government. 
## Why Is Red Everywhere?
One of the most striking features of these protests is the visibility of red flags and communist symbolism in several of the movements. In France, Italy, Albania and India, left-wing student organisations, communist groups, trade unions and activists have participated in demonstrations. The red flag has a long history as a symbol of labour movements, socialism, communism and resistance to economic inequality. Its appearance is therefore significant, particularly when it accompanies protests against austerity, education cuts and the rising cost of living. But we should be careful not to confuse the presence of communist flags with proof that an entire movement is communist. People march together for different reasons, and a student demanding better university facilities may have very different political beliefs from a trade unionist calling for higher wages or a citizen demanding electoral transparency. In some demonstrations, communist organisations may be among the principal organisers; in others, they may simply be one part of a much broader coalition. Still, their visibility begs us to question if left-wing organisations finding renewed opportunities to mobilise people around issues that directly affect their everyday lives? After years in which political debate in several countries has been shaped by the rise of nationalist and right-wing movements, these images offer a reminder that the left has not disappeared from the streets. Whether this translates into lasting political influence is another question entirely.
## Straw Hat is the modern pop culture revolution
The appearance of the Straw Hat, the symbol associated with Monkey D. Luffy and the One Piece universe. What began as an emblem of a fictional pirate crew has increasingly appeared in real-world demonstrations, adopted by protesters as a symbol of resistance, solidarity and defiance against authority. Its appearance alongside communist flags is fascinating because the two symbols come from entirely different traditions, yet can coexist within the same crowd. The red flag represents a long history of labour movements and left-wing politics, while the Straw Hat carries a more contemporary cultural identity that younger protesters can rally around. Neither symbol means that everyone carrying it subscribes to the same ideology. Instead, their presence suggests that today’s protest movements draw upon a mixture of established political traditions and newer, internet-driven symbols of resistance. When a communist flag and a Straw Hat rise above the same crowd, it is worth asking whether we are seeing not just the return of old political movements, but the emergence of a new language of dissent.
## Is the World Tired of the Rightward Shift?
For years, right-wing and nationalist politics have gained ground in different parts of the world, often through promises of stronger leadership, national identity, economic revival and a rejection of established political elites. Yet I don't think they can permanently satisfy the people who support it. Economic hardship does not disappear because a government promises national renewal. Universities do not become better funded because leaders speak about the country’s indefinite future. Nor do citizens necessarily become more confident in democratic institutions simply because elections continue to take place. The protests in Argentina against Milei’s austerity measures, the demonstrations in France over education and living costs, and the No Kings movement in the United States illustrate very different forms of opposition to government policies and political authority. India’s protests over electoral-roll revisions raise a different but equally fundamental question about democratic participation about if citizens trust that their right to vote will be protected? Some participants are motivated by economic concerns, others by civil liberties, and still others by local political disputes. Nevertheless, the simultaneous visibility of these movements suggests that opposition to governments and their policies is finding expression through mass mobilisation rather than remaining confined to elections or online debate.
## A Revolution, or the Beginning of Something Bigger?
Are we witnessing the beginning of a global left uprising? It is an intriguing possibility, but it is too early to call it a revolution. A revolution involves more than large crowds, burning buildings or flags rising above a protest. It requires a deeper transformation of political power, institutions and social relationships. Even sustained mass demonstrations do not necessarily produce such a transformation. Some movements win concessions and gradually disperse; others lose momentum, fragment over competing demands or are absorbed into established political parties. Some may even strengthen the political forces they initially opposed. Violence, too, complicates the picture. The burning of public buildings and clashes with police may become the most visible images of a protest, but they do not tell us what every participant believes or wants. Nor should the destruction of property be mistaken for evidence that a movement has achieved political success. What matters now is whether these demonstrations develop lasting organisations, coherent demands and the capacity to influence public policy. Will the anger over education lead to meaningful reformations? Will protests over living costs produce material improvements? Will demands for democratic accountability result in more transparent institutions? And will the people who have filled the streets continue to organise once the immediate excitement fades?
For now, I think we should watch closely. France, Italy, Albania, Argentina, Spain, the United States and India are not experiencing one universal revolution, and the flags in their streets do not erase their differences. But neither should we ignore the striking recurrence of public anger across countries facing different political circumstances. Something important is happening when students, workers and ordinary citizens repeatedly conclude that taking to the streets is necessary to make themselves heard. Whether history eventually describes this moment as a left-wing resurgence, a series of separate popular movements or another cycle of protest and political reaction remains to be seen. The world may or may not be turning red, but the streets are sending a message that governments everywhere would be unwise to ignore.
