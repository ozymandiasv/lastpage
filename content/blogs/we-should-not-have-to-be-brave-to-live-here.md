It brings me a strange kind of fear rather than anger. Yesterday I saw a video from Bihar in which two Class 10 students were stopped by a group of men while returning from coaching. What happened afterwards is difficult to even describe without feeling uncomfortable. The girl was harassed and dragged by members of the group while the boy was assaulted when he tried to intervene. The incident happened on September 19 in Jamui and the videos later appeared on social media. Police have identified several people and arrested three so far, while an SIT is investigating the case. What frightened me wasn't just the violence. It was the thought that I am an ordinary person. I am not a superhero, and I don't know how I would react if something like this happened in front of me. I don't want to live in a place where ordinary people are expected to be brave enough to protect themselves from groups of men who have decided that they can stop strangers on the road and demand explanations for their existence.
## The Question We Keep Asking the Victim
What disturbed me almost as much were some of the reactions online. Why was the girl outside at that time? Why was she travelling with a boy? Why did they go there? Why didn't they avoid that place? I don't understand why we keep asking these questions when the obvious question is what gives a group of strangers the right to stop them in the first place. Even if the girl had been travelling at midnight, even if she had been travelling with ten boys, even if she had done something her parents would disapprove of, none of that gives another person the right to touch her, threaten her or drag her away. We have somehow made personal freedom conditional on whether a person behaves in a way that strangers consider appropriate. That is not safety. That is surveillance.
## This Is Bigger Than One Video
I don't want to pretend that Bihar or Uttar Pradesh are uniquely unsafe while the rest of India has somehow solved this problem. They haven't. Crimes against women happen everywhere, and comparing states purely through raw crime numbers can also be misleading because reporting rates, population and policing differ. But there is enough data to take the problem seriously. NCRB data for 2023 recorded 22,952 crimes against women in Bihar and 60,581 in Uttar Pradesh. The numbers alone don't tell us everything, but neither should we pretend they tell us nothing. And the latest incident makes the concern harder to dismiss as an abstract statistic. In Jamui, police reportedly identified around seven people from the footage and arrested three. The video itself became the mechanism that brought the case to public attention. That should worry us too. A society should not need a horrifying video to make people care about what happened to two children.
## The State Has One Very Basic Job
I don't expect governments to create a perfect society. No government can eliminate every crime, every violent person or every moment of stupidity from millions of people's lives. But there is a basic contract here. I should be able to leave my house without calculating whether a group of men might decide that they have the authority to question me. A girl should be able to travel with a male friend without becoming an invitation for strangers to police her morality. Children should be able to return from coaching classes without becoming targets. This is where law and order stops being an abstract political phrase and becomes something very personal. Politicians can talk about development, identity, culture, religion and elections for hours. But for an ordinary citizen, the meaning of a functioning state can sometimes be much simpler. Can I go home safely?
## Start With Ourselves
I don't know what the perfect solution looks like. Better policing matters. Faster investigation matters. Courts matter. Punishment matters. Safe public spaces matter. But there is another part that doesn't require a new law or another government scheme. It begins at home. The next time someone says, "Why was the girl out so late?" we should be able to tell them that they are asking the wrong question. The problem wasn't that a girl and a boy were outside. The problem was that a group of people believed they had the right to decide what those two children were allowed to do. We should stop teaching our daughters how to avoid becoming victims and start teaching our sons, brothers, friends and neighbours not to become the people we need to protect ourselves from. Because I don't want to live in a country where being safe requires being brave. I just want to be ordinary.
