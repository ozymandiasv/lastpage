What is happening in this country? Today, Delhi witnessed an extraordinary crackdown ahead of the planned protest against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. Former Kerala Chief Minister Pinarayi Vijayan, along with CPI(M) leaders John Brittas and K. N. Balagopal, was detained near Jantar Mantar. Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) founder Abhijeet Dipke, co-convener Saurav Das and spokesperson Ashutosh Ranka were detained while they were still aboard their flight after landing in Delhi. YouTuber Dhruv Rathee was detained at his residence in Gurugram, while activists, students and other people attempting to join the protest were also reportedly taken into custody. This was not simply a case of police managing a crowd that had already gathered. The authorities were taking action against people before they could even reach the protest venue. When citizens travelling from different cities to question the government find themselves detained before they can exercise their right to protest, we have a serious problem.
## An Emergency Does Not Always Begin With an Announcement
The restrictions extended beyond individual detentions. Metro stations were closed, train services were cancelled or disrupted, and movement around central Delhi was restricted. The Supreme Court had already expressed concern about the proposed shutdown of public transport ahead of the protest, observing that a complete stoppage of metro and railway services was unacceptable and that regulated operations should be considered. Yet the disruptions became a major issue on the day of the demonstration itself. Delhi began to look less like a capital preparing to accommodate a political protest and more like a city being locked down to prevent one. Security arrangements may be necessary, and the police can impose lawful restrictions when there is a genuine threat to public order. But those measures must be proportionate. When ordinary commuters are inconvenienced, protesters are detained before reaching their destination, and public transport is restricted on such a scale, the authorities owe the country a clear explanation.
The comparison with 1975 is difficult to avoid. The Emergency declared that year formally suspended fundamental freedoms and brought censorship, mass arrests and severe restrictions on political opposition. India today is not formally under an Emergency, and the circumstances are different. But that distinction should not prevent us from questioning actions that restrict peaceful dissent. Democracy is not protected merely because elections continue to take place or because an Emergency has not officially been declared. It also depends on whether citizens can criticise the government, organise protests, travel to demonstrations and demand accountability without being treated as enemies of the state. The allegations surrounding the Election Commission and the electoral process have already created serious public distrust. If the government’s response is to prevent people from gathering and questioning the institution responsible for conducting elections, it risks deepening that distrust rather than resolving it.
## The People Are Not the Enemy
The most troubling aspect of this entire episode is the question it raises about the government’s attitude towards dissent. The controversy surrounding voter-roll revisions and allegations of electoral irregularities demands transparency, not silence. These allegations must be examined through evidence and due process, but the Election Commission cannot expect citizens to stop asking questions simply because it has issued a response. Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar must answer legitimate questions about the institution’s decisions, and the government must explain why the response to a planned protest has involved such extensive restrictions and detentions. If the allegations are unfounded, the appropriate response is to demonstrate that through transparent procedures and independently verifiable records. Preventing people from assembling does not make the questions disappear.
Protesting against the government is a democratic right, not an act of hostility against the country. Pinarayi Vijayan, Abhijeet Dipke, Saurav Das, Ashutosh Ranka, Dhruv Rathee, students and ordinary citizens do not lose their rights simply because their protest is inconvenient to those in power. The government may disagree with them, challenge their allegations or insist that protests follow the law, but it must also respect the freedoms that allow people to hold it accountable. A government that begins treating its own citizens as enemies whenever they question its decisions is taking a dangerous path. India has experienced what happens when dissent is suppressed, and we should not need to relive that history to understand the warning signs. This is not just about one protest or one election official. It is about whether the people can still question those who govern them without being treated as a threat to the state.
