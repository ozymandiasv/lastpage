The opposition’s protests over the last two days have been striking, not simply because of their size, but because of the breadth of political forces coming together around one issue; the credibility of the electoral process. INDIA bloc parties have taken to the streets, MPs have marched towards the Election Commission, several leaders were detained, and protests have continued beyond Delhi. It is difficult to remember a moment in the post-Emergency period when so many opposition parties have come together so visibly around a question as fundamental as the conduct of elections. The allegations surrounding the voter-roll revision and SIR are still allegations and must ultimately be established through evidence and due process, but they have now created a level of political and public anxiety that cannot simply be dismissed as another opposition campaign. The Election Commission has rejected the allegations, yet the controversy continues to grow, and that itself tells us something important about the level of trust that has been lost.
## This Is Not How Democracy Should Become Silent
What troubles me most is not even that the opposition is protesting. That is democracy functioning. What troubles me is the apparent absence of a comparable political response from the ruling establishment and the Election Commission. The CEC has not held the kind of detailed public press conference that many people would expect in the middle of such an extraordinary controversy. The Election Commission has issued responses and defended its procedures, but when millions of voters and the integrity of electoral rolls are being discussed, a few statements are not enough. If the allegations are wrong, this is precisely the moment to open the records, explain the process and answer every question publicly. Institutions become stronger when they confront scrutiny, not when they appear to retreat behind procedure and institutional authority.
I have seen protests in India before. I remember the demonstrations around Anna Hazare in 2011 and the way the Manmohan Singh government engaged with the crisis. Singh’s government had its own controversies and ultimately faced enormous public pressure, but he met Hazare and his colleagues in March 2011, wrote to Hazare during the later hunger strike, appealed publicly for him to end his fast and engaged ministers and negotiators in discussions with the movement. That does not mean the government accepted everything the protesters demanded. It means the government understood that a mass democratic movement had to be confronted politically and publicly. That is the difference I am struggling to see today. I have watched Indian democracy function in moments of enormous pressure, and I know that disagreement, protest and even confrontation can coexist with institutions answering to the public.
## The Frustration Is Bigger Than One Protest
The most worrying thing on the ground is the frustration I hear from people who otherwise disagree on almost everything. Some are angry about the electoral process. Some do not particularly care. Some still trust the system. But increasingly, even people who are not politically invested seem to understand why the allegations are troubling. That is where this becomes bigger than the opposition or the government. An election can be legally conducted and still lose public confidence if enough people begin believing that the process cannot be trusted. Once that happens, every result becomes harder to accept, every voter-roll correction becomes suspicious and every institutional explanation is viewed through doubt. The greatest danger to a democracy is not merely disagreement with its institutions. It is the widespread belief that those institutions no longer work fairly.
That is why the government and the Election Commission should not look at these protests merely as an opposition mobilisation to be contained. The protests can be political, the allegations can be contested and the opposition can be challenged on the evidence. But the underlying question belongs to every Indian, regardless of which party they support: Can I trust that my vote will exist when I go to cast it, that my neighbour’s vote will be treated in exactly the same way, and that the institution conducting the election will be transparent enough for me to verify it? If the answer begins to become uncertain, we have a problem much larger than any political party. Power in a democracy ultimately belongs to the people, and the electoral system exists to carry that power into government. It cannot become the very thing people are afraid to trust.
