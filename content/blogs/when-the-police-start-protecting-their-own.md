The allegations against a Delhi Police ACP are serious. Three women who identified themselves as journalists have complained of manhandling and sexual harassment during the detention of protesters at Jantar Mantar. The complaint has reportedly been transferred to the Crime Branch for an inquiry, but questions have already been raised over the police’s response and whether an FIR was initially being avoided. At this point, the allegations must be investigated properly and the facts established. But the larger concern is what this episode says about accountability when the person being accused is himself a senior police officer.
## Accountability Cannot Have Two Standards
If the allegations are false, the investigation should establish that clearly. If they are true, the officer should face the law. It should really be that simple. Rank, uniform and position cannot become shields against scrutiny. Yet when the institution itself appears more interested in defending or protecting one of its officers than immediately addressing the complaint, it creates a perception that the rules are different for those inside the system. That perception is dangerous because public trust in the police depends not only on how they deal with ordinary citizens, but also on how seriously they act when one of their own is accused.
This is where the government needs to be questioned as well. Police accountability cannot be treated as an internal matter whenever allegations involve police personnel. If an institution is unable or unwilling to hold its own officers accountable, then there has to be external scrutiny. Otherwise, we slowly create a system where the citizen is expected to answer to the law, while the people enforcing that law are protected by the institution itself.
## This Is What Unaccountability Eventually Produces
The real danger is not just one ACP or one complaint. It is the culture that develops when people within an institution begin to believe that their position will protect them from consequences. Today it may be allegations of assault or harassment during a protest; tomorrow it could be something far more serious. The answer cannot be silence, procedural delays or institutional protection. It has to be a fair investigation, transparency and consequences wherever wrongdoing is established.
We should start questioning the government whenever accountability appears to stop at the uniform. Who watches the police when the police themselves are accused? Who protects the complainant when the accused belongs to the institution meant to protect her? If the answer is simply another layer of the same system, then this is exactly the kind of unaccountability that eventually destroys public trust.
