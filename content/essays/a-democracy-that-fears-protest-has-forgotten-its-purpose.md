I have been following the arguments surrounding the arrests of Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, and what strikes me is how quickly a discussion about the right to protest becomes a discussion about whether the protesters deserve to be arrested. They allegedly breached restrictions at Jantar Mantar, and people are arguing that they should have anticipated the consequences. But I keep coming back to a more fundamental question: when a government restricts the space available for protest, should the act of challenging that restriction itself become a reason to silence those who object to it? I am not arguing that every law should be ignored or that every action taken in the name of protest is automatically justified. I believe laws are necessary, public safety matters, and peaceful protest does not grant anyone the right to assault others or cause indiscriminate harm. But I also believe that legality and justice are not always interchangeable concepts, and that a restriction imposed by the state must remain open to scrutiny. If we respond to every controversial protest by asking why the protesters did not simply obey the authorities, we risk overlooking the very reason democratic protest exists. Protests are often organised precisely because citizens believe that the authorities are wrong. If obedience were the only acceptable response to government decisions, dissent would become meaningless.
## Why Does the Location of a Protest Matter? 
Consider the history of Delhi’s protest sites. Before Jantar Mantar became synonymous with demonstrations, the Boat Club lawns near India Gate served as a major gathering place for political movements. In October 1988, farmer leader Mahendra Singh Tikait led a massive mobilisation there, with reports estimating that around five lakh farmers gathered to demand better prices and relief from agricultural expenses. The scale of that protest demonstrated the political force ordinary people could exercise when they assembled collectively. Following the political tensions surrounding the Ram Janmabhoomi-Babri Masjid movement and the demolition of the Babri Masjid in 1992, the government restricted large gatherings at the Boat Club. By 1993, Jantar Mantar had emerged as the principal location for demonstrations in central Delhi. It was still relatively close to Parliament and the government’s offices, allowing protesters to bring their grievances within sight of the institutions they wanted to influence.  
Now Jantar Mantar itself has been denotified as an authorised protest site, with Ramlila Maidan, Burari and the CBD grounds designated as alternatives. I understand that the government has responsibilities concerning security, traffic and public order. No city can allow every gathering to occupy every road without restrictions. But I think we must ask what is lost when the place historically associated with dissent is taken away. A protest site is not simply an empty piece of land where people are permitted to stand. Its location determines how visible a demonstration becomes, how easily people can reach it and how close protesters can come to the institutions they are addressing. Moving demonstrations further away may be administratively convenient, but convenience for the authorities cannot be the only consideration. If the alternative venues are less accessible, more expensive to organise or less visible to the public, the practical ability to protest may be weakened even when the right technically remains intact. A right that exists on paper but becomes increasingly difficult to exercise in practice deserves serious scrutiny.  
## A Peace Bond Cannot Become a Ban on Dissent
The argument being made against Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka is that they breached the conditions of their release bonds and entered a restricted area. That allegation must be examined through the legal process. However, I think it is essential to distinguish between a bond intended to prevent a breach of peace and a blanket prohibition on peaceful political activity. A preventive bond under Section 126 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita is concerned with maintaining peace and good behaviour in circumstances covered by the law. It should not automatically be interpreted as a general suspension of a person’s constitutional rights. Articles 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b) protect freedom of speech and expression and the right to assemble peacefully and without arms, subject to constitutionally permitted restrictions. These rights are not absolute, but neither can they be set aside merely because exercising them inconveniences the government. The Supreme Court’s judgment in Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan v. Union of India recognised the importance of peaceful protest while also addressing the need to balance it against public order and the rights of other citizens. The judgment did not establish that every restriction on a protest site is unconstitutional; equally, it did not make public protest a privilege that authorities can withdraw without justification.  
This is why I do not think the question can be settled by declaring either that the activists were unquestionably right or that they were simply foolish for getting arrested. We need to know the precise terms of the bond, the legal basis for the restrictions, the conduct alleged to constitute a breach and the reasons the court considered sufficient for custody. If they violated a lawful and constitutionally valid restriction, that fact matters. If the conditions were being used to prevent peaceful dissent more broadly, that matters too. I cannot honestly declare the bond unconstitutional without examining its actual wording and the circumstances in which it was imposed. But I equally cannot accept the idea that signing a preventive bond means surrendering the right to protest altogether. A legal restriction must remain proportionate, reviewable and consistent with fundamental rights. Otherwise, preventive law risks becoming a convenient instrument for discouraging people from exercising their constitutional freedoms.
## What Does Arresting Protesters Actually Achieve?
I keep thinking about the reasoning that these activists could have protested somewhere else and therefore brought their arrests upon themselves. Perhaps they could have. But that does not answer why they chose to challenge the restriction in the first place. Their action may have been strategically unwise, or it may have been a deliberate attempt to draw attention to what they considered an unjustified shrinking of democratic space. I cannot know their precise intentions merely from the fact that they were arrested. What I do know is that arresting protesters does not automatically resolve the grievance that brought them to the streets. Sometimes an arrest removes an individual from a demonstration. Sometimes it deters others. Sometimes it attracts more attention to the very issue the authorities would prefer to contain. None of these outcomes, by itself, establishes whether the protester’s argument was correct. That requires examining the underlying issue. In this case, the wider mobilisation concerns allegations of irregularities in electoral rolls and questions about electoral accountability. Those allegations must be investigated on evidence, and the Election Commission’s responses must be assessed on their merits. But arresting the people demanding answers cannot substitute for providing those answers. If the allegations are unfounded, transparent evidence and credible institutional scrutiny should help establish that. If there are genuine irregularities, detaining the people raising concerns will not make them disappear.
## If Gandhi and Nehru Had Never Challenged Authority
One argument in the discussion particularly stayed with me: if Gandhi, Nehru, Bose and Patel had strictly operated within the framework dictated by the British, India might never have become a republic. I do not think present-day protesters should automatically be compared with the freedom fighters, and I would not use the independence movement to justify every act of civil disobedience. The historical circumstances were different, and the legal and political questions require their own examination. But the broader lesson remains important. The history of India’s freedom struggle was not a story of people receiving permission from the authorities to demand their rights. It was a struggle in which people challenged laws, institutions and systems of power they considered unjust. Civil disobedience, mass demonstrations and political resistance helped expose the limits of a system that claimed the authority to govern Indians without granting them genuine democratic self-determination. The republic that emerged from that struggle subsequently placed fundamental rights and constitutional government at the centre of its political framework. That history should make us cautious about treating obedience as the highest democratic virtue. A citizen’s responsibility is not merely to comply with every instruction issued by the state. It is also to question whether the state is acting within its constitutional responsibilities. The freedom fighters did not establish a republic so that future generations would have to accept every restriction imposed upon them without asking whether it was justified.
## The Right to Dissent Must Survive the Government of the Day
I think the most dangerous consequence of normalising the arrest of protesters is that it can gradually change what society considers acceptable. Today, a movement may be protesting against electoral regulations. Tomorrow, another group may be protesting against rising prices, education cuts, corruption, environmental destruction or the denial of basic services. The political affiliations of the protesters will differ, and I may agree with some of their demands while disagreeing with others. That is precisely why the right to protest cannot depend on whether I like the people exercising it. If I defend peaceful assembly only when the protesters share my ideology, I am not defending a constitutional right; I am defending a political preference. Nor should we assume that a government is acting correctly simply because it invokes public order, just as we should not assume that every restriction is illegitimate simply because protesters oppose it. The responsibility is to examine the law, the evidence, the necessity of the restriction and whether the response is proportionate. Governments must be able to maintain public order, but citizens must also be able to challenge the way that power is exercised.
I return to the question I began with: what is the purpose of a democracy if citizens cannot visibly object when they believe the system is failing them? Elections are essential, but democracy cannot be reduced to voting once every few years and then remaining silent until the next election. It also depends on public scrutiny, independent institutions, freedom of expression and the ability to assemble peacefully. A protest may be inconvenient. It may disrupt the carefully managed image of a capital city. It may embarrass the government or attract attention to questions it would rather avoid. But inconvenience is not, by itself, a sufficient reason to extinguish dissent. I do not believe that protesters should be above the law. I believe the law must be examined just as carefully when it is used against protesters as when it is used against anyone else. A government confident in its legitimacy should be prepared to answer criticism, not merely demonstrate its ability to contain it. For me, this is not about defending every decision made by CJP, Saurav Das or Ashutosh Ranka. It is about defending the principle that peaceful dissent belongs to every citizen, including those whose arguments are unpopular, uncomfortable or directed against the government of the day. The right to protest is not a favour granted by those in power. It is part of what makes that power answerable to the people.
