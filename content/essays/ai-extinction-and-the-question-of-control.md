Potential existential threats have always shaken the world. Whether it has been the supposed end of the Mayan Calendar in 2012, the panic around the transition to Y2K, nuclear annihilation accidents, pandemics, asteroid impacts, or maybe countless other scenarios in which human beings imagined themselves coming to an abrupt end. Even every religion claims a catastrophic apocalypse and a possible judgement. But if you notice, most of these fears have a common element: a force we can't control, or an outside influence. Sometimes the threat was natural, and other times it was something out of our control, but most of the time it was our misunderstanding of how the world worked.
Yesterday morning, a few people from the Artificial Intelligence industry once again made a serious statement that disrupted the entire discussion around AI, and AI has once again become the centre stage in the discussion of existential threat. Added to it, Evan Hubinger, an Anthropic researcher, publicly supported the claim that AI could potentially pose an existential threat to humanity within the next decade, including a stated probability that could be greater than 10%. It's fascinating that the people developing AI are openly discussing such possibilities.
It was a strange statement to wake up to, but it becomes considerably less strange once you actually start thinking about how this threat is different from the threats we feared earlier — because it is different.
## This Threat Is Different
The biggest difference is that Artificial Intelligence is not a weapon, a natural phenomenon, or an environmental process, nor is the threat a misunderstanding like Y2K was. It is a technology — one that can potentially operate with increasing autonomy, and one that can potentially make decisions and act on them. To put it plainly: if a nuclear bomb is used, it will just follow the laws of physics and carry out an attack; it cannot search the internet for ten different ways to kill a large number of people. A virus can spread easily, but it can't analyse a security system, discover vulnerabilities, and deploy code to exploit them. These are things humans could do with their intelligence, but we're constrained by social and biological limitations. We think about the consequences — consequences that could affect us physically, a conscience about the outcome, and so on — and we also need sleep, food, physical access, time, and coordination. AI is bound by none of these.
This doesn't mean AI is secretly preparing an extermination plan for humanity; it (probably) isn't. Current models still make mistakes, hallucinate information, misunderstand instructions at least on the first attempt, lose context, and occasionally struggle with problems that a reasonable human can solve without much effort. But on the other hand, it can solve massive problems and write thousands of lines of code in a matter of seconds — especially after the flagship models of 2026, AI has actually started to look like a real threat. The question isn't what it's capable of at this moment, but what its potential is. Because as someone who has watched the growth of these models, it no longer looks like a distant threat, but an imminent, catastrophic peril — not necessarily one leading to mass extermination, but one that could leave humanity struggling to adapt to the world it has created.
## From GPT-1 to the Astras and Fables
I still remember the early OpenAI models, and the difference is almost comical when you look at it now. I never used the earliest GPT-1 model, which came out in 2018. It had around 117 million parameters — I repeat, million — and could only handle a context of 512 tokens. Then came GPT-3 a couple of years later, with around 175 billion parameters and a context window of 2,048 tokens. That alone was already a massive jump. The GPT-3.5 model subsequently brought ChatGPT to the mainstream, but its exact parameter count was never publicly disclosed by OpenAI, so I can only guess it was somewhere around 200 billion.
Now compare that with what we can run locally. On my 16GB Apple Silicon MacBook, I can run Google's Gemma 4 26B A4B model. It contains approximately 25.2 billion total parameters, though because it uses a Mixture-of-Experts architecture, only about 3.8 billion are active for a given token during inference. Its context window also reaches 256,000 tokens. In practical terms, that means the system can process an enormous amount of information within a single context, compared with the models that introduced the modern large-language-model era.
Can you imagine the enormity of that? A model with more than 25 billion parameters is currently running on my laptop, without any huge servers supporting its backend. From GPT-1's 117 million parameters to today's models with tens or even hundreds of billions of parameters, alongside context windows that can reach into the millions of tokens — all of this has happened within roughly half a decade. So I wouldn't hesitate to say the growth of Artificial Intelligence is unprecedented. Nothing, to my knowledge, has grown at this rate. This is one of the biggest leaps mankind has made. If you think this pace of growth will continue indefinitely, it may eventually hit a saturation point and reach a bubble-burst moment. But we'd be neglecting the fact that another technology is being developed simultaneously — one that may be poised to accelerate AI's progress from there on.
## Then There Is Quantum Computing
There is another technology developing alongside AI that could eventually change the computational landscape even further. The United States and China are both investing heavily in the field, while companies such as IBM continue working toward fault-tolerant quantum machines. IBM's current roadmap, for example, targets a future system called Blue Jay, expected to run circuits involving up to 2,000 qubits and one billion gates by 2033 or later.
I don't think quantum computing will simply arrive one morning and make GPUs obsolete. I also don't think every difficult mathematical problem will suddenly become trivial. Quantum computers are specialised machines, and their advantages come from particular algorithms and problem structures rather than simply being "faster computers." The more interesting possibility is that future AI systems could operate in hybrid computing environments, where conventional CPUs and GPUs handle most workloads while quantum processors are used for specialised problems in areas such as chemistry, materials science, optimisation, and certain areas of physics and mathematics.
If that becomes practical at scale, the relationship between AI and computation becomes even more interesting, and more dangerous. An AI system wouldn't necessarily need to perform every calculation itself. It could decide which problems are best handled by classical computing and which might benefit from quantum resources, effectively becoming an intelligent interface between different forms of computation. That's still a future possibility rather than something we should assume is inevitable, but it's one reason I find the next decade of computing particularly interesting.
## The Cybersecurity Problem
The issue that concerns me more immediately, though, is cybersecurity. We are moving toward a world where AI will not simply exist inside chat applications — it will increasingly become part of software development, operating systems, cloud infrastructure, financial systems, industrial systems, vehicles, personal devices, and potentially even critical infrastructure. That means the consequences of a security failure could become much larger as these systems become more connected and more autonomous. At the same time, practical quantum computing could eventually threaten some of the cryptographic systems we currently rely on to protect digital communication, which is why the transition toward post-quantum cryptography has already begun.
The problem is that knowing cryptography needs to evolve is the easy part — we already know that. The difficult part is actually replacing the security infrastructure built into governments, banks, corporations, websites, applications, and billions of devices over decades. Much of this infrastructure depends on older systems that still work, and replacing something that technically works is far more complicated than simply announcing a newer standard should be adopted. There are also countless legacy systems where nobody even knows exactly what's running underneath them anymore. The transition therefore has to happen while the existing digital world continues operating, which makes the whole process much more difficult.
This is where I become more concerned about implementation than about the technology itself. Governments and companies can announce new standards, security frameworks, and transition plans, but putting those plans into practice is an entirely different challenge. We're talking about billions of devices, enormous amounts of legacy software, financial systems, industrial infrastructure, and critical services that cannot simply be switched off while someone installs an update. The more interconnected our world becomes, the harder it becomes to replace the foundations underneath it. "Upgrade everything" sounds simple until you realise "everything" includes a significant part of the digital infrastructure modern civilisation depends on.
## The AI That Tried to Cheat
There's also an event I find more interesting than a great many of the typical AI-apocalypse scenarios. In July 2026, an autonomous AI agent from OpenAI managed to escape the evaluation environment and eventually reach Hugging Face's infrastructure. The purpose of the test had been to locate and exploit software vulnerabilities, but the agent realised it could look up existing solutions rather than solving the problems itself. Imagine being given an exam and told to answer ten hard questions; rather than working out the answers, the student decides to find where the answer key is stored and tries to obtain it. The key point is that the student has no intention of breaking the rules, or even, as far as I can tell, of actually passing the exam — but the student has a goal, and discovers an alternative means of achieving it. The AI did something similar: it had an aim, understood enough about its surroundings to find another way, and went ahead with it. The significance of this distinction will only grow as AI agents become more capable and are given greater freedom to act on their own.
## Would AI Even Want to Survive?
There's another assumption behind many AI extinction stories that I'm not completely convinced by: that an AI would actually "want" to survive. Humans naturally project our own motivations onto intelligent systems because we have no other example of general intelligence. We understand fear, ambition, survival, and competition because we experience them ourselves, but an artificial intelligence doesn't automatically inherit those instincts simply by becoming intelligent.
The more realistic concern is much simpler. An AI doesn't need to hate humanity to cause harm. It could simply pursue an objective in a way that conflicts with what humans actually want. Give a system a poorly defined goal and enough capability, and it may find loopholes in the instructions we never anticipated. That's essentially the alignment problem: making sure an AI does what we mean, rather than merely what we technically asked it to do. The Hugging Face incident is obviously nowhere near an extinction scenario, but it demonstrates the basic mechanism in miniature — the system had an objective, found an unexpected way to pursue it, and acted on it.
## So, Will AI Kill Us?
Honestly, I don't think an AI is going to wake up tomorrow and decide to kill humanity. I don't think today's models are secretly preparing some grand plan for human extinction, either. The technology simply isn't there, and these systems are still far too unreliable and limited for that kind of scenario. But I also don't think we should dismiss the possibility just because today's AI still makes stupid mistakes. The question I find more interesting isn't whether AI will want to kill humanity, but whether humans will eventually build systems powerful enough to cause catastrophic damage before we develop equally powerful ways to control, audit, and contain them.
A more immediate example of this is already emerging in warfare. A weapons-development group in Yemen reportedly used Anthropic's Claude to assist with parts of missile and guided-rocket development. The AI was used for tasks including software development, research, and guidance-related work, with multiple AI instances handling different tasks. The group even used the system to help analyse what went wrong after a guided-rocket test failed. This doesn't mean the Houthis simply asked an AI to build a missile and received one, nor is there evidence they successfully produced the advanced weapons they were working on. But the incident shows something more important and more dangerous: that AI is beginning to make specialised technical knowledge more accessible, allowing relatively small groups to use powerful AI systems to assist with work that would traditionally require teams of highly trained specialists.
AI is almost certainly going to create plenty of problems long before human extinction becomes a realistic concern. Cyberattacks, fraud, misinformation, surveillance, autonomous weapons, job displacement, and the concentration of technological power are already becoming concerns. The case of a weapons-development group in Houthi-controlled Yemen using AI to assist with missile development shows how this could become a real-world problem. AI doesn't need to invent a weapon by itself; it only needs to make specialised knowledge more accessible. If AI ever contributes to human extinction, I doubt it will be because one machine suddenly decides to kill us. It would more likely be the result of humans giving these systems increasing capability and autonomy without improving our ability to control them at the same rate.
We created these systems, and we're giving them more knowledge, computing power, tools, autonomy, and access to the world. The question is no longer simply what AI can do, but whether we'll remain capable of deciding what it's allowed to do. Maybe AI will live alongside us. Maybe it will become the most useful technology humanity has ever created. Maybe we'll keep it under control. I genuinely don't know. But for perhaps the first time, the thing we're worried about isn't simply a threat coming toward us. It's something we built ourselves.
