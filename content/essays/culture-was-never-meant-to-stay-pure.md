I recently came across a post criticising people in India for celebrating Halloween, describing it as a Western cultural influence and suggesting that we have somehow started finding foreign traditions cooler than our own. Now, I personally wouldn’t celebrate Halloween. I find the whole thing a little weird, but that’s just me. What I find even weirder is how difficult it seems for people to accept that others might enjoy something they don’t. Why does somebody dressing up as a vampire or decorating their house with pumpkins suddenly become a threat to Indian culture? How fragile must our cultural identity be if a few Halloween parties can supposedly undermine it? I understand wanting to preserve traditions, languages and cultural identities, but preservation doesn’t have to mean isolation. We wear trousers and shirts, use European-style toilets, shake hands, follow the Gregorian calendar and communicate in English. None of these originated in India, yet they are perfectly ordinary parts of our lives. We have adopted things from other societies for centuries, often without even thinking about where they came from.
## Culture Is an Amalgamation
The funny thing is that almost every culture we proudly identify with is already an amalgamation of influences. Take Diwali, for example. A festival associated with light, celebration and different religious and regional traditions has, in contemporary India, also become associated with fireworks and Chinese-manufactured crackers. Christmas celebrates the birth of Jesus, but its modern imagery is equally filled with snow, pine trees, Santa Claus and decorations that developed through different European traditions. I’m not even sure Bethlehem gets the memo about the snowy Christmas aesthetic every December. Cultures borrow, adapt, exchange and reinvent. Sometimes we adopt something because it is useful, sometimes because it is beautiful, and sometimes simply because we find it entertaining. And this exchange isn’t always a bad thing. Sati was once practised in parts of Indian society, but nobody should argue that we must preserve it simply because it was part of our history. We reject practices we recognise as harmful, retain those we value and incorporate new ones along the way. That’s how societies evolve. Culture isn’t a sealed historical document that we’re required to reproduce word for word.
## Live and Let Live
I don’t think celebrating Halloween makes someone less Indian, just as celebrating Diwali doesn’t make someone less connected to the rest of the world. You don’t have to enjoy every cultural practice, participate in every festival or abandon your own traditions to appreciate somebody else’s. There is a difference between cultural appreciation and blindly accepting everything, just as there is a difference between preserving your identity and policing someone else’s choices. Of course, cultural exchange can involve exploitation, commercialisation and appropriation, and those are worth discussing. But somebody attending a Halloween party in Pune isn’t exactly a civilisational emergency. We should be confident enough in our own culture to let people explore others without treating it as a betrayal. The irony is that the very traditions people are trying to protect have survived precisely because generations before us adapted, borrowed and changed them. If culture had remained untouched, much of what we celebrate today would probably look very different. So celebrate Halloween, celebrate Diwali, celebrate Christmas, or celebrate absolutely nothing. Just let people live. Culture belongs to the people who live it, not to those who want to freeze it in a particular century.
Peace.
