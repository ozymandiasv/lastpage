I am not against bursting crackers. I am against bursting crackers when our air quality is already struggling to remain breathable. Last year, the morning after Diwali, I could barely see five metres ahead of me. And this isn’t some minor inconvenience that disappears when the festivities are over. We are talking about the air we breathe, the environment we live in and the health of people who have absolutely no choice but to inhale whatever we collectively decide to release into the atmosphere. I cannot honestly say I don’t enjoy watching fireworks. They look beautiful, and there’s something fascinating about watching the night sky light up. But beauty doesn’t make something harmless. When the air is already polluted, adding tonnes of smoke and particulate matter to it is not exactly an intelligent way to celebrate. Research conducted across Indian cities has repeatedly recorded significant increases in particulate matter and other pollutants during Diwali, with unfavourable weather conditions sometimes trapping that pollution close to the ground. We are not imagining the problem. We are measuring it and then choosing to ignore it.
## What Does Tradition Actually Say?
Diwali, derived from the Sanskrit Deepavali, literally means a row of lights. Lighting diyas, decorating homes, performing prayers and celebrating the triumph of light over darkness have long been central to the festival. Fireworks, on the other hand, have a rather different history. Gunpowder and early fireworks originated in China, and the technology eventually travelled across Asia. India has its own long history of pyrotechnics too. Historical accounts describe fireworks at the Vijayanagara court in 1443, and by the eighteenth century, elaborate firework displays had become part of grand Diwali celebrations organised by Indian rulers. But for centuries before, people celebrated with lamps, prayers, sweets, gatherings and festivities. The point is not to reject fireworks because they have foreign origins. After all, cultures borrow and adapt all the time. The point is that we should be able to distinguish between a tradition’s central meaning and the additions it has accumulated over time.
## A Few Seconds of Celebration, A Much Longer Consequence
I am not lecturing anyone about moral responsibilities, nor am I asking people to stop enjoying themselves. I am simply pointing out a reality that most of us already know but conveniently choose to ignore. Firecrackers release fine particulate matter, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and other pollutants. These emissions can cause sharp, short-term deterioration in air quality, and when combined with low wind speeds and winter atmospheric conditions, the pollution can linger long after the fireworks have stopped. Children, elderly people and those with respiratory conditions are particularly vulnerable. And the consequences aren’t distributed equally. Some people can celebrate indoors with air purifiers running, while others have to work, commute and live in the same polluted environment without that luxury. We have managed to turn a festival of lights into a competition over who can make the loudest noise and fill the sky with the most smoke. I understand the excitement, but perhaps we should occasionally question whether every tradition needs to become bigger, louder and more excessive every year.
## Celebrate, But Think About What Remains
I don’t want to take away anyone’s happiness, and I certainly don’t want Diwali to become an argument about who is more culturally aware or environmentally conscious. Light your diyas, decorate your homes, meet your friends, eat your sweets, enjoy the celebrations. If you love watching fireworks, I understand that too. But we should be mature enough to recognise when our enjoyment comes at a cost that extends far beyond the celebration itself. Traditions aren’t sacred simply because they are old, and they aren’t beyond criticism simply because millions of people participate in them. We have the ability to adapt the way we celebrate without losing what makes the festival meaningful. I would much rather wake up the morning after Diwali to a sky still visible, clean air and the smell of sweets than a city covered in smoke. Be smart. I’m not lecturing you on moral responsibilities; I’m just telling you the reality, one that even you are aware of but perhaps choose to ignore. Happy Diwali.
