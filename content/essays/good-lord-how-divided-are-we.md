Yesterday, I saw a rather fascinating advertisement on television. Elite Matrimony, apparently a matchmaking service for the affluent sections of society. Hmm, what? An exclusive matrimonial service for the wealthy? I was curious enough to look it up, and apparently they have different categories for professionals, business owners and even an Aristocrat tier for people with a net worth exceeding ₹200 crore. Two hundred crore! I wonder what the matrimonial profile looks like. Anyway, jokes aside, the advertisement got me thinking about something much bigger than a matchmaking service. When did we become so comfortable with the idea of categorising people according to how much they earn, what they own and which social circles they belong to? I know matrimonial services have existed for decades, but something about seeing wealth marketed so explicitly as a qualification for finding a partner felt rather strange. We are comfortable turning marriage into another opportunity to establish where we stand in the social hierarchy.
## Everyone Has Their Preferences, Apparently
Now, I am not particularly bothered by people wanting certain kinds of compatibility. Religion can be important to someone, and I can understand a person wanting to marry someone who shares their beliefs and practices. Professional compatibility is another matter. If two doctors want to marry, or someone wants a partner from the UPSC, IIT or IIM circles, well, good for them. Perhaps they share similar experiences, ambitions and expectations. I am not against wealthy people marrying wealthy people either. Their lifestyles and social circles might be considerably different, and they may find it difficult to relate to someone from a completely different economic background. That is a problem in itself, and the extent to which class determines our social interactions is something worth discussing separately. But what exactly are we celebrating when we turn these preferences into exclusive social categories? There is a difference between wanting someone with whom you share common interests and actively building a system that reinforces the idea that people from certain backgrounds belong together and others do not. The distinction might seem small, but I think it matters.
## And Then There Is Caste
What makes the whole thing even more annoying is that we already have religious and caste-based matrimonial services. Caste, particularly, is something I find difficult to reconcile with the idea of a society that claims to have progressed. We have spent decades talking about equality, social justice, the abolition of untouchability and the need to move beyond inherited social hierarchies. Yet, when it comes to marriage, caste continues to be treated as an entirely ordinary qualification. Entire platforms exist to help people find partners within their caste, preserving the very boundaries that generations of social reformers have challenged. Of course, people are free to make their personal choices, and I am not suggesting that a matrimonial advertisement alone explains the complexities of caste discrimination. But surely we can ask why these divisions continue to matter so much. We talk about atrocities, discrimination and the humiliation faced by people because of their caste, and then we casually maintain the social arrangements that keep caste identities relevant in everyday life. How are we supposed to move beyond a hierarchy if we keep reproducing it in our most intimate social relationships?
## The Strange Respectability of Classism
This particular incident is revealing about the way society responds to these divisions. Openly defending caste exclusion faces considerably stronger pushback in urban public discourse today, at least compared to a few decades ago. People are more careful about expressing caste prejudice openly, and that is a welcome change. But class hierarchy seems to enjoy a rather different reputation. Wealth, exclusivity and luxury are marketed as signs of success, and keeping one's social circle restricted to people of a similar economic background is often treated as perfectly respectable. We might raise an eyebrow at an advertisement explicitly excluding someone on the basis of caste, but an advertisement inviting only people with hundreds of crores in wealth into an exclusive matrimonial circle can be presented as a premium lifestyle service. Somehow, class discrimination has acquired a rather sophisticated vocabulary. It is no longer about keeping people out; it is about exclusivity, standards, lifestyle and finding someone from your own league. I find that distinction fascinating, because underneath all that polished marketing, the underlying idea is still that some people belong together because of what they have, while others simply do not qualify.
I used to think that by now we would have moved considerably further ahead. We have more access to education, technology, information and opportunities than previous generations could have imagined. We can communicate with people across continents in seconds, yet we continue to organise our own societies around inherited identities, wealth and social status. From the rich and the poor to the upper and lower castes, from one religion to another, from one profession to the next, we keep finding new ways to establish who belongs where. I don't expect everyone to abandon their preferences or marry someone they have absolutely nothing in common with. That is not the point. What bothers me is how readily we accept these divisions as normal, how easily we turn social barriers into personal preferences and how little we seem to question the hierarchy beneath them. We keep talking about equality while finding newer, more polished ways to separate ourselves. Good lord, how divided are we?
