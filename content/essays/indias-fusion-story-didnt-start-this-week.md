## What "Artificial Sun" Actually Means
"Artificial Sun" is the term people use for nuclear fusion experiments, where scientists try to reproduce, on a much smaller scale, the process that powers the Sun itself. The Sun has one advantage we don't have on Earth. Its own gravity creates enough pressure at its core to force hydrogen nuclei close enough together to fuse. When they do, they form helium and release a huge amount of energy. A tokamak has to solve the same problem in a completely different way. In India's SST-1, fuel is turned into plasma and kept away from the chamber walls using powerful magnetic fields inside a doughnut-shaped vessel. The new gyrotron heats that plasma. It produces extremely powerful microwaves at a very specific frequency, and those microwaves transfer energy directly into the plasma, pushing it to extraordinary temperatures. So when people hear "artificial Sun," they shouldn't picture India trying to build a star. This is about learning to control fusion here on Earth. And maybe, eventually, turning that into a usable source of energy.
## The New Gyrotron
India's latest step is the installation and commissioning of a new 82.6 GHz, 400 kW gyrotron on the SST-1 tokamak at the Institute for Plasma Research in Gujarat. It sounds like a small, technical detail, but it isn't. This gives researchers a far more powerful way to heat plasma, and to actually study how it behaves under extreme conditions. China is pushing its tokamaks toward increasingly long plasma durations. The US has made major experimental advances. France is home to ITER, the world's largest international fusion project. Compared to all that, India is still some distance behind when it comes to actually producing usable fusion energy. Even so, getting a machine like this commissioned on an Indian tokamak is a genuinely big deal. It means India is building the specialised expertise needed to work on some of the hardest physics and engineering problems in the world.
## A Programme Four Decades in the Making
But what actually caught my attention wasn't the gyrotron. It was the history behind India's tokamak programme, and that's where the story got a lot more interesting to me. India's fusion programme didn't appear out of nowhere with SST-1. The groundwork was laid decades earlier. India began its plasma research programme in the early 1980s, and the Institute for Plasma Research was set up in 1986. Professor P. K. Kaw was one of the key figures in that early phase. He's largely the reason plasma physics became a serious field of research in India at all. Then came ADITYA, India's first indigenously built tokamak, commissioned in 1989. It was a small experimental machine, but that wasn't really the point. India had to learn how to build and operate a tokamak from scratch. How to handle extremely hot plasma. How to build diagnostic systems, heating systems, vacuum systems, often by figuring out why things simply didn't work. ADITYA ran for decades and produced thousands of plasma discharges. What that experience really built wasn't a machine at all. It was a generation of people who actually knew how to work with fusion technology.
The next big step was SST-1, a far more ambitious superconducting tokamak, conceived in the 1990s. This pushed India into a completely different tier of engineering. Superconducting magnets. Cryogenics. High-power microwave systems. Ultra-high vacuum technology. And on top of all that, the challenge of keeping plasma stable for much longer stretches than before. None of it went smoothly. SST-1 ran into technical problems during its early commissioning, including issues with its cooling and vacuum systems, and the machine needed major modifications before it actually worked. That's just part of how technological progress happens. India had to solve those problems itself, and every fix added to the country's own knowledge base. On June 20, 2013, SST-1 finally achieved its first plasma. India had gone from its first experimental tokamak to running a superconducting one, built largely through its own scientific and engineering effort.
What I find genuinely fascinating is how much of today's progress rests on those earlier decisions. ADITYA gave Indian scientists decades of hands-on experience. SST-1 forced Indian engineers to build real expertise in superconducting magnets, cryogenics, plasma heating, long-duration operation. India's participation in ITER added another layer on top of that, putting Indian scientists and companies in the position of contributing components to one of the most complex scientific projects ever attempted. The new gyrotron isn't some achievement that appeared overnight. It's the product of decades of accumulated knowledge, experimentation, failure, improvement, and of people who kept the programme alive long enough for that knowledge to actually compound.
## Leadership, Patience and Where India Stands
I think leadership deserves more credit in this story than it usually gets. A programme like this doesn't produce dramatic results in a few years. It needs institutions that can survive changes in government, shifting budgets, changing priorities, failed experiments, all of it. Someone has to keep deciding a programme is worth continuing even when there's no immediate result to point to. India still has a long way to go here. It lacks the depth of specialised talent, industrial capability, advanced materials, R&D infrastructure and investment needed to genuinely compete with the biggest fusion programmes in China, Europe and the US. China in particular has moved extremely fast in recent decades, and Europe and the US have enormous scientific and industrial ecosystems behind their efforts. So no, I wouldn't call India a leader in the global fusion race yet. But that doesn't make this achievement insignificant. India started building its own tokamak capability decades ago and just kept at it. It learned from its failures. It got better, slowly, at building increasingly sophisticated fusion technology. We may not be at the front of the race today, but simply reaching this point is worth recognising. Maybe that's what struck me most once I actually looked into it. The new gyrotron isn't the start of India's artificial Sun story. It's just the latest chapter in one that began with ADITYA.
