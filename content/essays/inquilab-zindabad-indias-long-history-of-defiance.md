India’s history is not merely the history of kings who ruled, empires that rose and fell, or governments that came to power. It is also the history of people who challenged authority, resisted exploitation and refused to accept that power gave anyone the right to silence them. From the Koonan Kurishu Sathyam of 1653 to the Attingal Revolt of 1721, from the uprising of 1857 to the revolutionary cry of Inquilab Zindabad, the subcontinent has witnessed countless struggles against authority. The story continued through the Swadeshi movement, the Salt March, the Quit India Movement, the resistance during the Emergency, the farmers’ protests and the demonstrations of recent years. These events emerged from different circumstances, and their participants did not always share the same objectives. Some fought foreign domination, others challenged social injustice, and many demanded accountability from governments of their own country. Yet they form part of a larger historical tradition, the determination of people to question power and fight for what they believe is right. Dissent is not an interruption of India’s history. It is one of the forces that has repeatedly shaped it.
## Before Independence: Resistance Across the Subcontinent
Resistance to foreign power in India predates the establishment of British rule. In 1653, the Koonan Kurishu Sathyam at Mattancherry saw members of the Saint Thomas Christian community collectively reject Portuguese ecclesiastical authority, defending their religious autonomy against outside control. The Attingal Revolt of 1721, meanwhile, involved an uprising against the British East India Company’s presence and conduct in the region. These were distinct events, driven by different political, commercial and religious circumstances. They were not modern nationalist movements, but they demonstrated that resistance to European power existed in different parts of India long before the emergence of the national freedom struggle.
Across the subcontinent, resistance also emerged against local rulers, oppressive revenue systems, dispossession and social hierarchies. The Santhal rebellion of 1855–56 challenged the exploitation and loss of land experienced by tribal communities under colonial rule and associated systems of moneylending and landlordism. Other peasant and tribal movements challenged the interference of colonial authorities and powerful local interests in their livelihoods. Such struggles rarely received the same attention as the campaigns led by famous political figures, but they were no less important to the communities involved. For people whose land, livelihood or autonomy was threatened, resistance was not an abstract political idea. It was a response to conditions they could no longer accept.
## The Revolt of 1857: The First Great Challenge to Company Rule
In 1857, a major uprising erupted against the British East India Company, beginning among Indian soldiers and spreading to several regions of northern and central India. The rebellion brought together soldiers, rulers, peasants and other groups whose grievances included military discrimination, annexation of territories, economic disruption and interference in established social and political arrangements. Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, Begum Hazrat Mahal, Nana Sahib and Bahadur Shah Zafar became associated with different centres of resistance. Their aims were not identical, and the uprising did not represent a unified national independence movement in the modern sense. Nevertheless, it posed a serious challenge to the Company’s authority.
The British suppressed the uprising through military force and reprisals. In 1858, the Crown assumed direct control over India, bringing Company rule to an end. Although the rebellion failed to remove British power, it became a lasting symbol of resistance. It demonstrated that the Company’s control was neither universally accepted nor invulnerable. It also formed part of the historical background against which later generations developed a more organised struggle for independence. The story of 1857 reminds us that resistance often begins with grievances rooted in everyday life, even when its consequences eventually reshape an entire political system.
## From Swadeshi to Quit India: The National Movement Takes Shape
During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, opposition to British rule increasingly developed through political organisations, newspapers, public meetings and mass mobilisation. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, initially sought constitutional reforms and greater Indian participation in administration. Over time, however, the national movement expanded beyond petitions and negotiations. The partition of Bengal in 1905 triggered the Swadeshi movement, which encouraged the boycott of British goods and the development of indigenous industries. Students, traders, workers and other groups participated, turning opposition to colonial policy into a wider public movement.
The Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, when British troops fired on a gathering in Amritsar, intensified anger against colonial rule. In the following years, Mahatma Gandhi helped transform the freedom struggle into a mass movement through non-cooperation and civil disobedience. The Salt March of 1930 challenged the colonial salt laws through an act of protest that ordinary Indians could participate in themselves. The Quit India Movement of 1942 demanded an immediate end to British rule. The colonial government arrested major Congress leaders and suppressed demonstrations, but resistance continued across the country.
The independence movement also included revolutionaries who believed that armed resistance was necessary. Subhas Chandra Bose organised the Indian National Army, seeking to end British rule through military struggle. Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, Sukhdev and their comrades challenged colonial authority through revolutionary action and used their trials to publicise their political ideas. Their methods differed from Gandhi’s philosophy of non-violence, and the movement contained serious disagreements over strategy and ideology. Yet the demand for independence continued to grow through decades of protests, strikes, political campaigns, sacrifice and resistance. On 15 August 1947, British rule finally ended. Independence was not the achievement of one leader or one organisation alone; it emerged from a long and complicated struggle involving millions of Indians.
## Inquilab Zindabad: The Revolutionary Spirit
The slogan Inquilab Zindabad, meaning “Long Live the Revolution”, became closely associated with Bhagat Singh and the revolutionary movement. It represented a refusal to accept colonial domination as permanent and expressed the conviction that the existing order could be challenged. Bhagat Singh’s significance extended beyond his willingness to sacrifice his life. His writings explored socialism, exploitation, communal harmony and the relationship between political independence and social justice. He believed that replacing British rulers with Indian rulers would not, by itself, guarantee an equal society. His execution alongside Rajguru and Sukhdev in March 1931 made them enduring symbols of the freedom struggle.
The revolutionary movement was only one part of India’s resistance to colonial rule, but its legacy remains powerful. Gandhi’s campaigns, the revolutionary organisations, peasant mobilisations, workers’ strikes and the activities of countless local organisers all contributed to the broader struggle, even when their methods differed. Inquilab Zindabad continues to resonate because it captures the determination to challenge injustice rather than accept it as inevitable. Its historical meaning should not be reduced to a call for violence or used carelessly to describe every disagreement. At its heart lies the spirit of refusing to submit unquestioningly to authority.
After Independence: The Struggle for Equality Continues
Independence ended British rule, but it did not automatically eliminate caste discrimination, poverty, economic inequality or the concentration of power. The Constitution, which came into force in 1950, established fundamental rights and a democratic framework intended to protect individual liberties and allow citizens to participate in public life. Yet the promises of independence required continued effort to become a reality for everyone.
B. R. Ambedkar’s struggle against caste oppression was central to the demand that political freedom must be accompanied by social equality. Dalit movements continued to challenge discrimination and violence, while workers and farmers organised around wages, land rights and economic security. The Dalit Panthers, formed in Maharashtra in 1972, emerged as a radical movement against caste oppression. Elsewhere, campaigns for land reform and the rights of Adivasi communities challenged the power of landlords, commercial interests and state institutions. These struggles did not all share a single political vision, but they demonstrated that independence had opened a new chapter rather than brought the history of dissent to a close. The question was no longer simply how to end foreign rule, but how to ensure that the freedoms promised by the Constitution reached people whose lives remained shaped by inequality.
## The Vimochana Samaram: A Contested Struggle in Kerala
The Vimochana Samaram of 1958–59 was a major political movement against the Communist government led by E. M. S. Namboodiripad in Kerala. The government’s education reforms and other policies generated opposition from religious organisations, private educational managements, political parties and sections of society concerned about the extent of state control. Supporters of the movement presented it as a struggle against excessive government power and in defence of institutional autonomy. Critics argued that established social and economic interests were mobilising against an elected government pursuing reforms. The movement grew into a mass campaign and ultimately contributed to the dismissal of the government by the central government in 1959.
The episode remains controversial because it raises difficult questions about dissent, reform, electoral mandates and the influence of powerful interests. It demonstrates that a protest against a government cannot automatically be understood as either a defence of democracy or an attempt to obstruct it. Its meaning depends on the demands being made, the interests involved and the constitutional context in which the conflict unfolds. The Vimochana Samaram is one among many episodes in India’s post-independence history in which the relationship between public protest and political authority became contested. Its significance lies not in presenting one side as unquestionably right, but in showing how complicated dissent can become when elected governments, social movements and established institutions clash.
## The Emergency: When Democratic Freedoms Were Suppressed
In June 1975, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi declared a national Emergency. Civil liberties were severely restricted, press censorship was imposed, and opposition leaders, activists and journalists were detained. The government exercised extraordinary powers, while criticism of those in authority became dangerous. Jayaprakash Narayan, who called for Total Revolution, became one of the most prominent figures opposing the government’s actions. Students, political workers and activists continued to resist, while underground publications and networks helped circulate information that censorship sought to suppress.
The Emergency ended in March 1977, when elections were held and Indira Gandhi’s Congress party was defeated. The result demonstrated that citizens could use the ballot to hold the government accountable, even after a period of severe repression. The episode remains a warning about the fragility of democratic institutions when executive power expands and civil liberties are weakened. Elections are essential to democracy, but they are not sufficient on their own. A functioning democracy also requires a free press, independent institutions, freedom of assembly and the ability to criticise those who govern. The resistance to the Emergency remains an important part of India’s history because it showed that defending democracy sometimes means defending the right to oppose the government itself.
## From Anti-Corruption Protests to the Farmers’ Movement
In the decades that followed, public protests continued to influence national debate. The Narmada Bachao Andolan challenged the displacement of communities and the environmental consequences of large development projects. The India Against Corruption movement of 2011 brought large crowds into public spaces and placed corruption at the centre of national politics. In December 2012, the brutal sexual assault of a young woman in Delhi triggered enormous demonstrations demanding justice, women’s safety and accountability. Students and citizens gathered across the country, pressing institutions and political leaders to respond to a problem that had long affected women’s lives.
The protests against the Citizenship Amendment Act, beginning in 2019, brought citizens from different backgrounds into demonstrations across India. Shaheen Bagh became particularly prominent for its sustained sit-in led by women, who invoked constitutional values while opposing the law. Supporters defended the legislation as a measure to protect persecuted religious minorities from neighbouring countries, while opponents argued that its religious criteria raised serious questions about equality and secular citizenship. In 2020, farmers launched a major movement against three agricultural laws, with large protest camps forming around Delhi. Farmers feared the laws would weaken their security and bargaining power, while the government argued that the reforms would benefit the agricultural sector. Following months of mobilisation, the government announced the repeal of the laws in November 2021, and Parliament subsequently passed the repeal legislation. The movement demonstrated that sustained public pressure could compel a government to reconsider its policies.
## October 2026: Dissent Returns to the Streets of Delhi
On 10 October 2026, a planned protest in Delhi against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar, concerning allegations about the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls, was met with extensive police restrictions and detentions. Political leaders, activists, students and organisers associated with the Cockroach Janta Party were among those reported detained. Restrictions on access to protest locations and disruptions to transport added to the controversy. The allegations concerning the electoral process require evidence and proper scrutiny, just as the authorities’ response to the demonstration deserves examination in its own right. Neither the claims made by protesters nor the explanations offered by officials should be accepted without considering the available evidence.
The Election Commission occupies a crucial position in India’s democratic system, and public confidence in its impartiality matters. When concerns arise about voter registration or the deletion of names from electoral rolls, citizens should be able to verify their status, access clear explanations and challenge errors through transparent procedures. Peaceful protest is also part of democratic life, and restrictions should be lawful, necessary and proportionate. A government does not have to agree with every protest, and every demonstration cannot proceed without regulation. But citizens should not be treated as enemies merely because they question an institution or challenge a decision. In a country whose independence was won through decades of public resistance, the right to ask difficult questions should not be dismissed as an inconvenience.
## The People Have Never Been Absent From India’s History
From resistance to Portuguese expansion and the uprising of 1857 to the national movement against British rule, from struggles against caste oppression to the Vimochana Samaram, from resistance during the Emergency to the farmers’ protests and demonstrations of recent years, dissent has appeared in many forms across India’s history. These events were not identical, and their causes cannot be reduced to one simple explanation. Some challenged foreign domination, some sought social equality, some defended institutional autonomy, and others questioned the policies of elected governments. Their outcomes varied, and their participants often disagreed about what justice required. But they demonstrate that the history of India cannot be told only through the decisions of those who held power. It must also include the people who challenged those decisions.
Dissent is not automatically virtuous, and protest does not make every demand correct. Movements can be mistaken, political organisations can exploit public anger, and demonstrations can sometimes become violent. Democratic societies must examine claims critically and protect public order. But the existence of disagreement is not itself a threat to the nation. In a democracy, the right to question power is essential because no government, institution or political movement should be beyond scrutiny. Citizens must be able to criticise decisions, demand evidence and seek accountability without having their patriotism questioned simply because they disagree with those in authority.
Inquilab Zindabad emerged from India’s revolutionary struggle, but its enduring place in national memory reflects a broader history of resistance. The people who fought colonial rule did not all share the same vision of the future, just as citizens who protest today do not all share the same politics. What connects these moments is the conviction that people have a right to question those who govern them. Governments will change, parties will rise and fall, and the arguments of one generation will become the history lessons of another. But the freedom to speak, assemble, criticise and demand accountability must endure. India’s history of dissent is not a history of hatred for the country. It is the history of people who believed that their country was worth fighting for, questioning and holding to its promises. Inquilab Zindabad.
