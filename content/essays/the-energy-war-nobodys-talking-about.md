I keep coming back to one idea whenever I read about fusion. Whoever actually cracks this problem doesn't just solve an energy problem. They rewrite what's possible for the next century. That sounds dramatic, but the more I read about fission and fusion side by side, the more I think it's actually true.
## What Fission Actually Does
Fission is the technology we already have. Every nuclear plant running today works on this principle. You take a heavy, unstable atom like uranium, and you split it apart. That split releases a huge burst of energy, and it also kicks off more splits in nearby atoms, which is what a chain reaction actually means. It works, and it works at scale. France gets most of its electricity from fission. It's clean in the sense that it doesn't burn anything and doesn't pump carbon into the air.
But fission comes with baggage that never fully goes away. It produces radioactive waste that stays dangerous for thousands of years, and someone has to store that waste somewhere, forever, without it leaking or falling into the wrong hands. The fuel itself, once refined enough, can be weaponised. And when something does go wrong, whether it's Chernobyl or Fukushima, it goes wrong in a way that scares entire countries into rethinking their energy policy for decades. None of that makes fission useless. It just means fission's downsides are permanent even when everything works exactly as intended.
## What Fusion Actually Does
Fusion is the opposite process. Instead of splitting a heavy atom apart, you force two light atoms together, usually isotopes of hydrogen, until they fuse into something heavier and release energy in the process. This is what actually happens inside the Sun and every other star. On Earth, doing this on purpose means recreating conditions that don't naturally exist here. You need extreme heat, tens of millions of degrees, and you need to hold that superheated plasma in place without it touching the walls of whatever's containing it. Scientists do this using either powerful magnetic fields inside a doughnut-shaped chamber called a tokamak, or by using lasers to compress a tiny fuel pellet so hard it ignites.
The fuel for fusion is abundant. Deuterium can be pulled out of seawater, and there's no realistic way to run out of it anytime soon. Fusion also doesn't produce long-lived radioactive waste the way fission does, and there's no chain reaction to run away with itself, so a meltdown in the fission sense isn't really a risk here. If something goes wrong in a fusion reactor, the reaction just stops. It doesn't cascade.
## Why Fusion Wins the Argument
Once you actually understand both processes, the case for fusion writes itself. Fission gives you power today, but hands you a waste and safety problem that outlives everyone currently alive. Fusion, if it can be made to work at scale, gives you enormous energy output using fuel you'll never run out of, with none of the long-term waste and none of the meltdown risk. The catch, and it's a massive one, is that fusion is astonishingly hard to actually pull off. Containing plasma at those temperatures for long enough, and getting more energy out than you put in, has taken the better part of a century of research just to get close.
## China, France and the US Are Already Racing Ahead
This is where the last few years have actually gotten interesting, and not in a theoretical way.
In January 2025, China's EAST tokamak held steady-state, high-confinement plasma for 1,066 seconds, nearly eighteen minutes, at temperatures well over 100 million degrees. That smashed its own previous record of 403 seconds from 2023. Barely a month later, France answered back. The WEST tokamak at Cadarache, run by the French atomic energy commission, sustained a similar plasma state for 1,337 seconds, pushing the record even further. These aren't small, isolated lab experiments anymore. They're sustained operations lasting long enough to actually matter for a future power plant.
The US has been chasing a different but equally important milestone. In December 2022, the National Ignition Facility in California fired 2.05 megajoules of laser energy into a fuel capsule and got 3.15 megajoules of fusion energy back out. For the first time ever, a fusion reaction produced more energy than was put directly into it. That single result answered a question scientists had been chasing since the 1960s. And it didn't stop there. By 2025, NIF had repeated ignition multiple times, at one point getting a yield of 8.6 megajoules from just over 2 megajoules of input, a gain ratio nobody had come close to before.
None of these three results, on their own, gives us a working fusion power plant. EAST and WEST prove you can hold the plasma stable for a genuinely long time. NIF proves you can get net energy gain from a single shot. Putting both of those things together, sustained duration and net energy gain, inside a design that can actually feed a power grid, is still the real challenge ahead. But three different countries, using three different approaches, all clearing major hurdles within a few years of each other, is not something that would have seemed plausible even a decade ago.
## Energy Will Decide the Next Century
Every major shift in human history has really been an energy shift underneath everything else. Coal built the industrial age. Oil built the century after that, along with most of the geopolitics that came with it. Whoever controls the next big energy source doesn't just get cheaper electricity. They get an economic and strategic advantage that reshapes everything downstream of it, from manufacturing to computing to military capability. Fusion, if it actually gets there, isn't just a cleaner version of what we already have. It's a fundamentally different kind of leverage, because it removes the scarcity that has shaped energy politics for the last two hundred years.
That's really why these tokamak and laser records matter more than they look like they should on the surface. They're not just impressive physics. They're early signals of who's going to hold that leverage when fusion finally does become practical. And going by how fast these breakthroughs have started stacking up, that future might be arriving faster than most people expect.
