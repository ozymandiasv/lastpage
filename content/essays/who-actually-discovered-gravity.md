An old clip of Piyush Goyal has started circulating again. In it, he makes a rather interesting remark about Einstein and gravity, suggesting that mathematics didn't help Einstein discover it. The clip resurfaced after another comment of his about a "360-degree change" in India's image, which was stupid because a 360-degree turn brings you right back to where you started. Mathematics became a recurring character in these conversations. But the Einstein remark reminded me of something more interesting. I commented on a comment criticising Goyal's statement that if "technically if Newton discovered gravity, Einstein redefined it." I was surprised by how many people thought I was simply defending Goyal. I wasn't. I don't know what Goyal intended to say, or how would he make any logic in saying math isn't important in calculating GDP (in the original context of Einstein statement). But I do think the history of gravity deserves more than a viral clip and a few arguments in the comments section. So, who actually discovered gravity? Well, let's go back a little.
## Aristotle: When Everything Had an Explanation
I would like to begin with Aristotle, because this man seems to have had an opinion on practically everything. Politics, literature, biology, physics, mathematics, ethics, you name it. If there was a subject, Aristotle probably had something to say about it. When it came to gravity, he believed that objects fell because it was their natural tendency to move towards their natural place. Earth and water naturally moved downwards, while fire and air moved upwards. He also believed that heavier objects fell faster than lighter ones. Today we know that this isn't quite right, but remember that Aristotle was trying to understand the world through observation and reasoning, without the experimental tools and mathematical framework we have today. His ideas became enormously influential and shaped scientific thinking for centuries. Unfortunately, being influential and being correct are two very different things. Aristotle got plenty of things wrong, but he helped establish the questions that later thinkers would challenge.
## Galileo: Perhaps We Should Actually Test This
Centuries later came Galileo Galilei, who wasn't particularly interested in accepting an explanation simply because Aristotle had said it. He began examining motion through experiments and mathematics. One of his important contributions was showing that objects fall with the same acceleration when air resistance is negligible. In simple terms, if you drop a heavy iron ball and a lighter iron ball from the same height, they will hit the ground at practically the same time. The reason a feather falls more slowly isn't because it has less natural tendency to move downwards, but because air resistance affects it much more. Galileo also developed the principle of inertia, helping establish that an object doesn't need a continuous force to keep moving at a constant velocity. This was a significant departure from the old understanding of motion. Instead of explaining everything through what seemed natural, Galileo wanted to measure, experiment and calculate. Apparently, mathematics was already proving useful, even before Einstein entered the conversation.
## Kepler: The Planets Were Not Drawing Circles
Then we have Johannes Kepler, who deserves considerably more recognition in this story. By studying astronomical observations, particularly those collected by Tycho Brahe, Kepler established that planets travel around the Sun in elliptical orbits rather than perfect circles. He formulated three laws of planetary motion that described how planets move, how their speed changes and how their orbital periods relate to their distances from the Sun. These were mathematical descriptions of planetary movement, not yet a complete explanation of what caused it. Kepler knew how the planets moved, but the deeper question remained: what was making them move that way? Why did an apple fall towards Earth while the Moon continued travelling around it? Were these separate phenomena, or were they connected by some universal principle? Kepler had helped describe the dance of the planets. Someone still had to explain the music.
## Newton: The Apple and the Universal Law
Enter Isaac Newton. In 1687, Newton published *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, one of the most important scientific works ever written. He formulated the law of universal gravitation, which stated that every object with mass attracts every other object with mass. The strength of this attraction depends on the masses involved and the distance between them. Put simply, larger masses attract more strongly, while the gravitational attraction becomes weaker as the distance between objects increases. Newton connected what appeared to be two entirely different things: an apple falling towards the ground and the Moon orbiting the Earth. Both were governed by the same law. This was revolutionary. He didn't simply explain why objects fall; he showed that the same mathematical principles could describe motion on Earth and throughout the heavens. Newton's theory was extraordinarily successful and remained the foundation of gravitational physics for more than two centuries. So, yes, Newton deserves enormous credit. But science, as it turns out, wasn't finished with gravity.
## Einstein: What If Gravity Isn't Really a Force?
And then came Albert Einstein. Newton's theory worked remarkably well, but there were certain things it couldn't fully explain. One famous example was the unusual movement of Mercury's orbit, which didn't quite match Newtonian predictions. Einstein approached the problem from an entirely different direction. In his general theory of relativity, published in 1915, gravity wasn't simply a force pulling objects towards one another. Instead, mass and energy curve spacetime, and objects move along paths shaped by that curvature. Imagine placing a heavy bowling ball on a stretched rubber sheet. The sheet bends around it, and a smaller ball rolling nearby follows a curved path. It is an imperfect analogy because actual spacetime is four-dimensional, but it gives us a basic idea. The Earth curves spacetime around itself, and the Moon follows a path through that curved spacetime. What Newton described as gravitational attraction, Einstein explained through the geometry of spacetime. This wasn't a small correction to Newton. It was a fundamental change in how we understood gravity. Einstein didn't make Newton irrelevant, either. Newton's equations still work extremely well for everyday situations, from calculating falling objects to sending spacecraft into orbit. Einstein's theory becomes necessary when we deal with extreme gravity, very high speeds or measurements requiring extraordinary precision.
## Even Einstein Needed a Mathematician
Now, here's another interesting part of the story. Einstein was undoubtedly a brilliant physicist, but general relativity required some very complicated mathematics. He needed mathematical tools that went beyond what he was comfortable working with at the time. This is where his friend Marcel Grossmann enters the picture. Grossmann was a mathematician who introduced Einstein to the mathematical framework of Riemannian geometry and tensor calculus, which became essential to formulating general relativity. They even collaborated on early papers. So when someone suggests that mathematics wasn't particularly useful to Einstein, I would gently recommend looking into the mathematics behind general relativity. Einstein's genius was in developing the physical ideas, but he also needed the right mathematical language to express them. And that language was not something he simply invented overnight. It was built over generations by mathematicians and scientists. Newton had Galileo and Kepler before him. Einstein had Newton's foundations and Grossmann's mathematical expertise. Scientific progress is rarely a one-person show, no matter how much we enjoy putting one genius on the cover of a textbook.
So, who discovered gravity? Aristotle tried to explain why things fall. Galileo challenged our understanding of motion. Kepler described the movement of planets. Newton unified terrestrial and celestial gravity through a universal mathematical law. Einstein then transformed our understanding of gravity itself by describing it as the curvature of spacetime. Each contributed something different, and each deserves recognition for it. Newton didn't get everything wrong because Einstein came along, just as Aristotle's mistakes don't erase his importance in the history of thought. Science isn't a competition where the last person to arrive gets to declare everyone before them an idiot. It is a continuous process of questioning, correcting and building upon what came before. And perhaps that's the most interesting thing about gravity: humanity has spent thousands of years trying to understand something that has been holding us down all along.
