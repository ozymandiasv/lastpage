Beautiful Ugly began with a premise that immediately caught my attention. Modern thrillers can be predictable, especially when a husband or wife goes missing and the other spouse inevitably becomes a suspect. But I don’t think a familiar premise is necessarily a problem. You can tell the same story a hundred times, and with the right characters, circumstances and storytelling, it can still be brilliant. What matters is how the author makes us question what we think we know. And for a while, Beautiful Ugly did exactly that. Once Grady and the island entered the picture, everything started feeling suspicious. Every character, every interaction and every new revelation seemed to conceal something, and I found myself developing theories, discarding them and coming up with new ones. The atmosphere was intriguing, the mystery kept expanding, and I genuinely struggled to put the book down. The problem is that the author kept building the suspense without giving me a satisfying reason to believe that all this buildup was leading somewhere worthwhile.
## Grady, the Unreliable Narrator Who Wasn’t Allowed to Be Unreliable
My biggest disappointment was what happened to Grady as a character, particularly because so much of the story depends on his point of view. An unreliable narrator can be a wonderful storytelling device, but there has to be a purpose behind the unreliability. If Grady was constructing a version of events to protect himself, manipulate the reader or convince himself that he was the victim, that could have been fascinating. He could have been a man so desperate to escape the truth that he gradually rewrote his own memories, until even he could no longer distinguish what happened from what he needed to believe had happened. But the book never develops that idea convincingly. Instead, the final revelations seem to dismantle the character we have spent the novel following. The opening chapter is particularly frustrating because it presents Grady’s experience of that night as though we are witnessing events unfold directly through his eyes, rather than listening to a story he is telling the police. If he is deliberately concealing something from us, the narrative needs to establish how and why. Who is he lying to? What does he believe he is protecting himself from? An author can absolutely mislead readers through a character’s perspective, but the deception must make sense in retrospect. Here, I was left feeling less as though I had uncovered a carefully concealed truth and more as though the rules of the story had changed just before the end.
## The Pregnancy Test, Abby and the Missing Logic
I can understand the emotional reasoning behind Grady killing his wife, at least in principle. A relationship collapsing under the weight of resentment, betrayal and an unwanted pregnancy could certainly provide the foundation for a disturbing crime. But the way the book handles the pregnancy test left me asking far too many questions. Grady sees a positive test and concludes that it belongs to Abby. How does he know that? It could have belonged to a friend, or there could have been another explanation entirely. What makes this more frustrating is that Grady actually confronts Saddy to find out whether she knew about the draft, rather than simply killing her without asking questions. So, if he was capable of seeking clarification in that situation, why was he so quick to draw conclusions from a pregnancy test? Why not confront Abby and establish what was happening before deciding what it meant? More importantly, why would Abby choose to surprise her husband with a pregnancy when she knows he does not want a child, particularly one conceived through IVF using a donor? What exactly was she expecting to happen? Perhaps there is an emotional explanation for her actions, but the book does not make the situation convincing enough for me to accept it without hesitation. Abby’s point of view suffers from a similar problem. Rather than adding a coherent new dimension to the mystery, her perspective often feels disconnected from the rest of the narrative. I wanted her chapters to deepen my understanding of the characters and make earlier events look different in retrospect. Instead, they frequently left me wondering how these pieces were supposed to fit together. A twist should make the story more meaningful, not force the reader to spend the next few pages repairing its logic.
## A Mystery That Saved Everything for the Last Twenty Pages
The pacing is perhaps the most frustrating part of the experience. The suspense kept building, then building some more, until I was convinced that the final chapters would deliver something extraordinary. I was willing to follow the book through its increasingly strange developments because I expected the payoff to justify the journey. Instead, the answers arrived in roughly the final twenty pages, and even then, they failed to make the preceding events fit together satisfactorily. There is nothing inherently wrong with a late revelation, and I enjoy thrillers that hold back crucial information until the end. But suspense is not simply the act of withholding answers. It is the process of creating questions whose eventual answers transform our understanding of the story. When the answers contradict earlier details or depend on assumptions the narrative has not adequately established, the revelation feels arbitrary rather than clever. The book had so many opportunities to make Grady’s perspective more interesting, to give Abby greater narrative coherence and to connect the island’s mysteries to the central crime in a meaningful way. Instead, it seemed determined to keep the reader guessing for as long as possible, without giving equal attention to whether the final explanation actually worked. By the time I reached the ending, my curiosity had turned into frustration.
## The Best Investigative Journalist, Apparently
And then there are the newspaper articles. For a character presented as an exceptional investigative journalist, Abby’s writing did very little to convince me of her talent. The articles felt weak, unconvincing and surprisingly amateurish. I found myself thinking that some of my own blog posts were better written, which is a ridiculous thing to think while reading a novel about a supposedly brilliant journalist, but here we are. The problem is not that every fictional article needs to read like award-winning journalism. It is that the writing itself is used to establish something important about Abby’s professional identity. If we are supposed to believe that she is exceptionally good at uncovering the truth, the evidence of that ability should be present in her work. Instead, the articles felt more like props inserted into the story to remind us of her profession than genuine examples of her investigative skill. This is a smaller problem compared with the narrative contradictions and the disappointing resolution, but it contributes to the feeling that the book’s ideas are often more compelling than their execution.
## An Amazing Premise, Wasted
Ultimately, Beautiful Ugly is the kind of book that frustrates me precisely because I enjoyed so much of the journey. I finished it in two days, which says something about how effectively the mystery pulled me along. The island was intriguing, the atmosphere was unsettling, and the constant suspicion made it difficult to stop reading. Yet a thriller cannot be judged only by how desperately it makes us want to discover the truth. The truth itself has to be worth discovering, and it must make sense within the story the author has constructed. I can forgive a predictable twist if the execution is excellent, and I can appreciate an unconventional ending if it gives the characters and the mystery a meaningful conclusion. What I struggle to accept is a story that spends hundreds of pages creating questions, only to answer them in a way that undermines the very narrative that made those questions interesting. Grady could have been a fascinating unreliable narrator. Abby could have been a more coherent and compelling character. The island could have led to a revelation that made the entire book fall into place. Instead, the final chapters left me questioning the author’s decisions rather than reconsidering the story. A six out of ten feels fair: an intensely readable thriller with an excellent premise, an atmospheric mystery and an ending that squandered almost everything I had enjoyed about it.
