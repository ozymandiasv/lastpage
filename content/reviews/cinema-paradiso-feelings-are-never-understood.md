I recently watched *Cinema Paradiso* by Giuseppe Tornatore and felt drawn into a type of cinema that seems almost lost today. These lighthearted, beautiful films have a tenderness that speaks louder than action and spectacle. *Cinema Paradiso* reminded me that stories do not need to be grand to have power. They can be quiet and subtle, and within that silence there can be an emotional depth that stays with you long after the final frame. I realised how much I miss this kind of storytelling, where the true essence of a film lies not in its scale but in its gentleness. Modern blockbusters have their own appeal, and I enjoy many of them, but there is something irreplaceable about a film that takes its time and trusts the audience to simply sit with a moment. It does not constantly tell us what to feel. It allows us to find the feeling ourselves, and perhaps that is why it can remain with us for so long.
## Alfredo and Toto
The friendship between Salvatore and Alfredo is central to *Cinema Paradiso*. It is different from the friendships we often see in movies because it is not really a bond between equals. Alfredo is much older and wiser, sometimes gruff, while Salvatore is a boy full of curiosity and innocence. Yet their differences are what make their relationship so beautiful. Alfredo becomes a teacher and guide to Toto, but there is something more than that between them. Their connection resists simple definitions of friendship, family or companionship, and perhaps it does not need to be defined at all. We often think friendship is about finding someone who is like us, someone who understands our experiences because they have lived something similar. But sometimes a relationship becomes meaningful precisely because two people are different. They fill each other's voids in ways they may not even realise. Watching Alfredo and Toto, I felt like I was witnessing a type of love that is rare in today's cinema, an affection rooted not in romance but in respect, patience and a deep understanding that does not always need words.
## Feeling Can Never Be Understood
Among the many memorable moments in the film, one conversation between Alfredo and Toto stayed with me. Alfredo tells Toto that “feeling can never be understood.” When Toto wants to know the ending of a story, Alfredo refuses to explain it, insisting that some things have to be experienced for themselves. He then tells him the story of the princess and the soldier, leaving it incomplete. Toto does not fully understand its meaning at the time, but he carries the story with him and only understands it later in life. Those words stayed in my mind because they made me think about empathy. We use the word so casually, as though understanding another person's feelings is something we can simply do by imagining ourselves in their place. But can we ever really do that? We can listen to someone, remember our own experiences, and try to imagine what they might be feeling, but we are still looking at their experience through our own lives. We never completely fit into another person's shoes. At best, we interpret. At best, we approximate. The pain that seems small to one person might be devastating to another. A heartbreak that someone recovers from in a few months might stay with another person for years. Even when two people experience the same kind of loss, they do not necessarily experience it in the same way because they bring different memories, fears and wounds to it.
Perhaps this is why we are so quick to dismiss certain emotions. The world often encourages us to move on because if we stopped for every grief, disappointment and small loss, perhaps we would find it difficult to continue living normally. We say things like “you'll get over it” because most of the time, eventually, we do. But getting over something does not mean that it was insignificant. Something can completely change us and still become something we learn to live with. We cannot know the weight of another person's feelings simply by looking at what happened to them. There may be an entire history behind a reaction that we cannot see. Our emotions are shaped not only by what happens to us but by everything that happened before it. This is why I think Alfredo's words carry such weight. Perhaps feelings cannot really be understood from the outside. They can only be felt by the person who lives them.
## What We Leave Unexplained
Maybe empathy, then, is not about completely understanding another person. Perhaps it is about accepting that there will always be something we cannot understand. We can listen without claiming to know exactly how someone feels. We can care without pretending that their experience is the same as ours. We can acknowledge someone's pain without deciding whether it is reasonable or unreasonable. There is a certain humility in accepting that another person's inner world will always remain partly unknown to us. And perhaps that is not a failure of empathy but a part of it. We do not have to explain every feeling in order to respect it.
That is ultimately what *Cinema Paradiso* represents to me. It is not only a story about cinema or friendship or nostalgia, but about memory, time and the fragile connections that remain with us even after the people and places connected to them are gone. Cinema allows us to experience something without necessarily giving us the words to explain it. A scene can remind us of something we have forgotten. A character can make us feel something we cannot name. A story can stay with us for years without us ever fully understanding why. Perhaps that is the highest form of cinema for me. Not the kind that tries to overwhelm us, but the kind that quietly leaves something behind. *Cinema Paradiso* reminded me that tenderness, silence and the mysteries of the heart still matter. We may never completely understand what another person feels, but perhaps we do not have to. Sometimes it is enough to simply recognise that their feeling is real, even if it remains something we can never fully know.
