While writing about Kumbalangi Nights, I found myself wondering what I would consider the greatest Malayalam film ever made. The first name that came to mind was Thaniyavarthanam (1987). What a film. And no, it didn't leave me smiling the way Kumbalangi Nights did. It left me questioning almost everything: rituals, inherited beliefs, mental health, social conditioning, oppression and the frightening power of a community to decide what is normal and what isn't. To imagine that this was Lohithadas's very first screenplay makes me immensely proud of Malayalam cinema. I personally consider Thaniyavarthanam one of the greatest Malayalam films ever made, and perhaps my favourite. Its brilliance lies in how it takes something as ordinary as a family history and turns it into a devastating examination of society. The story follows Balan, a schoolteacher from a family believed to carry a hereditary curse of mental illness. When circumstances begin to resemble the family's tragic past, the people around him slowly start treating him as though he is destined to lose his sanity. The tragedy isn't simply about mental illness. It is about what happens when society decides that you are ill before it even considers whether you are.
## Balan and Gopi: Two Ways of Losing
One of the most fascinating contrasts in the film is between Balan and Gopi. Gopi represents the desire to escape, to resist the fate that society has assigned to the family, while Balan gradually becomes a man who accepts his position within it. Balan is educated, respected and perfectly capable of living an ordinary life, but he is surrounded by people who interpret every action through the lens of an inherited superstition. His attempts to explain himself become increasingly futile. His reputation, relationships and dignity are taken away, and eventually even he begins to question his own sanity. What a character Balan is. He doesn't fight back in the way we expect a protagonist to. He submits, accommodates and obeys, almost as though he has accepted that everyone else must know something about him that he doesn't. And that obedience becomes his downfall. I kept wondering what would have happened if Balan had resisted, if he had refused to accept the identity imposed upon him. But perhaps that is precisely the point. How do you fight an entire society when even your own family begins to believe that you are beyond saving?
## Who Was Actually Mad?
The film also makes us question the family's history itself. Was the brother genuinely mentally ill? Was Gopi's resistance simply an attempt to escape a belief system that had trapped generations before him? Could the behaviour attributed to madness have been a response to trauma, grief and unbearable social pressure? We cannot diagnose fictional characters retrospectively, but the questions are important. A person experiences a traumatic event, behaves differently, withdraws from society or struggles to cope, and suddenly an entire community has an explanation ready for them. The family history becomes evidence, every unusual behaviour becomes confirmation, and nobody seems interested in considering another possibility. Balan's tragedy is that he is not given the freedom to be an individual. He is treated as the successor to a family condition, almost as though his future has already been written. A respected schoolteacher can become an outcast within days, not necessarily because his behaviour has changed beyond recognition, but because the people around him have changed the way they see him. The film makes you wonder whether society is observing madness or participating in its creation.
## A Tragedy That Refuses to End
I sometimes feel the film could have ended with Balan's death. It would have been devastating enough. His mother, unable to bear his suffering and convinced that his fate is irreversible, ultimately poisons him. It is an extraordinarily painful moment because even this final act comes from within the same world of fear, helplessness and inherited beliefs that destroyed him. But the film doesn't stop there. It ends with the family performing rituals in the hope of protecting Balan's young son from inheriting the same supposed madness. And that ending is perhaps even more horrifying than Balan's death. A ten-year-old child, who has done absolutely nothing, is already being placed inside the same cycle. The family hasn't learnt anything. The rituals continue, the beliefs survive, and another generation is prepared to carry the burden. You cannot help wondering whether society will eventually destroy this child just as it destroyed his father. The title, Thaniyavarthanam, itself carries the idea of repetition, and that is exactly what makes the ending so powerful. The tragedy isn't confined to Balan. It is the possibility that the same tragedy will happen again.
## Mammootty: A Performance Beyond Replication
And it would be impossible to write about this film without talking about Mammootty. What an actor. What a performance. I genuinely struggle to imagine another actor carrying Balan Master with the same intensity and restraint. He doesn't turn the character into a spectacle of suffering. He allows us to watch an ordinary man slowly lose his place in the world. His expressions, his silences, his helplessness and the gradual change in his behaviour make the performance extraordinarily convincing. There is no need for exaggerated drama when the character himself is so heartbreaking. Mammootty makes you feel the humiliation of being disbelieved, the exhaustion of trying to explain yourself and the loneliness of watching everyone around you turn against you. It is one of those performances that makes you appreciate the difference between acting and inhabiting a character. I have immense respect for him, and perhaps I'll write separately about Mammootty and Mohanlal someday, because Malayalam cinema has been extraordinarily fortunate to have actors of their calibre. For now, I will leave it at this: Thaniyavarthanam is a film I find difficult to review because I don't think I can adequately describe what it made me feel. It is a film about a man, but its real subject is the society that decides what happens to him. And nearly four decades later, its questions about superstition, mental health, conformity and collective cruelty remain painfully relevant.
