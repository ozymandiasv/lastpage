Apple Is Selling Us Things We Don’t Need

From touchscreen MacBooks to glowing logos, the company that once told us not to chase gimmicks seems increasingly interested in selling them. Perhaps the problem is not what technology can do, but what capitalism wants us to buy.

I still remember using my friend’s Microsoft Surface laptop. It had a touchscreen, a fancy removable display and all sorts of features that seemed exciting at first. It was fun to play around with for a while, but eventually I got bored and wanted my trackpad or a mouse. Honestly, I have never been particularly comfortable using Windows laptop trackpads, so I usually prefer a mouse anyway. But that is beside the point. I hated the touchscreen laptop experience. Steve Jobs had argued years ago that vertical touchscreens were ergonomically awkward and that touchscreen laptops were a poor idea. Apple itself spent years maintaining that position. And now, guess who has made a touchscreen MacBook? Apple. The new MacBook Pro with M5 Pro and M5 Max has a touchscreen, an OLED display and a redesigned interface. The starting price is already ₹3,49,900, but the fully customised M5 Max configuration can reach an astonishing ₹17.2 lakh in India. Yes, a laptop costing more than many cars. At that point, I genuinely wonder who this product is for and whether we have completely lost perspective on what a computer is supposed to do. I understand that technology evolves and companies change their minds, but I cannot help wondering whether we are witnessing innovation or simply another attempt to manufacture desire. If someone genuinely needs a touchscreen for their professional workflow, fine. But why should the rest of us be persuaded that a laptop without one is somehow incomplete?

The Gimmick Economy

What bothers me is that Apple already occupies a position most technology companies would dream of. People buy its products because they are desirable, because they work well together and because the brand has built an extraordinary reputation. It does not need to convince everyone that their existing devices are suddenly outdated. Yet here we are, with touchscreen MacBooks, foldable phones and increasingly elaborate product designs. Even Apple’s entry into the foldable phone market, years after the first commercial foldables appeared, has been surrounded by enormous expectations and questions about its design and practicality. I sometimes wonder whether these companies have confused technological progress with the ability to introduce something new every year. One of the features I actually like is the return of the glowing Apple logo on the MacBook Pro. It is nostalgic, recognisable and, admittedly, rather cool. But think about it, a small illuminated logo on the back of a laptop becomes another reason to distinguish the expensive Pro models from the rest of the lineup. Why not bring it to the Air? What exactly is stopping a company with Apple’s engineering resources from doing that? It is a small feature, but that is precisely my point. We have reached a stage where even tiny design decisions become part of a carefully constructed product hierarchy. You pay more, and you get the privilege of owning something that glows.

The Crisis of Overproduction

I have never understood the obsession with buying a new phone every year. My iPhone 13 Pro still runs smoothly. My upgraded MacBook Pro from 2012 continued to handle ordinary tasks perfectly well until a couple of years ago. These are not ancient machines that became unusable overnight. They did what I needed them to do. Yet the industry operates on the assumption that consumers must constantly replace devices, even when their existing ones remain perfectly functional. This brings me back to Marx and his argument about the crisis of overproduction. Capitalism has an inherent tendency to produce more commodities than people can afford to consume, creating a contradiction between productive capacity and purchasing power. The problem is not necessarily that society lacks useful things to produce; it is that the economic system needs continuous consumption to sustain its expansion. So companies have to keep creating demand, encouraging people to replace what they already own and presenting perfectly adequate products as outdated. A phone that works for five years becomes a problem for a business that wants you to buy another one after two. A laptop that lasts a decade is wonderful for its owner, but not necessarily for the company’s annual sales targets. Planned obsolescence, software limitations, artificial product differentiation and relentless marketing all help sustain this cycle. We are not always buying something because we need it. Sometimes we are buying it because we have been persuaded that we should want it.

When Is Enough Actually Enough?

I am not against technological progress. I love technology, I enjoy experimenting with new devices, and I am probably more interested in computers than the average person. But I also think we should be able to appreciate technology without becoming its permanent consumers. There is something rather absurd about spending several lakhs on a laptop because it now responds to your fingers, when the keyboard and trackpad were already doing their jobs perfectly well. We should be asking whether a new feature genuinely improves our lives, not whether it gives us another reason to upgrade. My iPhone 13 Pro does not become a worse phone because a newer model exists. My old MacBook did not suddenly lose its usefulness because Apple released another generation. I wish more people felt comfortable keeping their devices until they actually needed replacing. Perhaps the most radical thing a consumer can do today is simply refuse to buy something. There is an old line often attributed to the revolutionary sentiment of the labour movement, “The last capitalist we hang shall be the one who sold us the rope.” I would rather leave the metaphor there and make a simpler point. The companies selling us these products are not necessarily interested in how long we can use them. They are interested in how soon we will want something else. We should at least learn to recognise the difference.
