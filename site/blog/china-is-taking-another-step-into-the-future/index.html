China Is Taking Another Step Into the Future

The CE-25A, electric aviation and a country that seems determined to surprise the world.

Every now and then, I come across something that genuinely makes me stop and admire what human engineering is capable of. This time, it is the CE-25A, China's newly developed, fully electric commercial aircraft. A couple of days ago, the aircraft completed its first piloted flight in Shandong, carrying two pilots and a flight-test engineer. And what makes it particularly fascinating is its design. Instead of conventional jet engines or turboprop engines, the aircraft uses ten electric ducted fans distributed across its wings, five on each side. It is a 10-tonne aircraft designed to carry 19 passengers, with a cruising speed of around 300 kilometres per hour and a reported range of 400 kilometres. Just imagine boarding a passenger aircraft that operates entirely on electric propulsion. We have spent decades electrifying cars, then buses and trucks, and now we are beginning to see serious attempts to take that technology into commercial aviation. The CE-25A is still a prototype undergoing testing, but its first piloted flight is a remarkable engineering milestone. And I find the idea of electric regional aviation particularly exciting.

From the Road to the Runway

What interests me most about the CE-25A is not simply that it flies on electricity, but the engineering philosophy behind it. Its distributed electric propulsion system uses multiple smaller fans rather than relying on a conventional engine arrangement. This offers possibilities for improving propulsion efficiency, reducing noise and potentially operating from shorter runways, although these advantages will have to be demonstrated through further testing. The aircraft is being developed by COMAC in collaboration with CATL, one of the world's largest battery manufacturers, Shanghai Jiao Tong University and other partners. That combination is important. Electric aviation isn't just an aircraft engineering problem; it is also a battery chemistry, energy density, thermal management, software and safety problem. A passenger car can afford to carry a relatively heavy battery, but aircraft have to account for every kilogram. They must also maintain sufficient energy reserves for emergencies and alternative landing arrangements. This is why electric commercial aviation has been so difficult to develop. The CE-25A's reported 400-kilometre range makes it a regional aircraft rather than a replacement for long-distance passenger jets, but that is precisely where electric aviation could begin to make sense.

China's Relentless Technological Progress

And this brings me to something I find equally fascinating: China's progress. Look at what has happened over the last decade. China has transformed itself into the world's largest electric vehicle manufacturing powerhouse, built an extraordinary battery industry, expanded its renewable energy capacity and developed increasingly sophisticated manufacturing capabilities. Companies such as BYD and CATL are now influencing entire global industries. COMAC has also entered the commercial passenger aircraft market with the C919, challenging an industry historically dominated by Boeing and Airbus. Now it is experimenting with fully electric commercial aviation. What makes this impressive is that these developments aren't isolated achievements. They are connected by an industrial ecosystem involving universities, research institutions, manufacturers, government investment and domestic supply chains. China is building capabilities across multiple layers of technology rather than simply purchasing finished products from elsewhere. Of course, it still faces significant challenges in advanced semiconductor manufacturing, aircraft engines, international certification and competing with established aerospace companies. But the pace at which it is developing alternatives is difficult to ignore. I sometimes wonder whether the rest of the world has fully appreciated how much the technological landscape has changed.

What Does This Mean for the Future?

I genuinely believe electric aircraft are the future of aviation. Perhaps not tomorrow, perhaps not for long-haul international flights just yet, but I wouldn't be surprised if we see electric aircraft capable of travelling 1,000 kilometres sooner than we expect. Battery technology is improving, and every breakthrough brings us closer to making this a reality. And honestly, after a long time, I am seeing China develop things that genuinely make me admire its progress. Sustainable development, engineering and technology that actually matter. Not another stupid iPhone release with a slightly better camera, a marginally faster processor and a new button that nobody asked for, convincing people to spend another lakh on a phone they bought last year. This is technology that could fundamentally change how we travel, reduce our dependence on fossil fuels and make transportation cleaner and more accessible. I don't know whether China will lead every technological race, but watching developments like this genuinely excites me. Perhaps this is what technological progress should look like: not finding another way to sell us the same thing, but building something that actually moves humanity forward.
