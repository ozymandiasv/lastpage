When Dissent Lands in Jail, Political Differences Must Take a Back Seat

The detention of CJP leaders Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka raises questions about the right to protest, the limits of state power and the need for opposition unity.

The detention of Cockroach Janata Party (CJP) leaders Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka, followed by their being sent to five days of judicial custody, has brought the question of democratic dissent back into focus. The Delhi Police alleges that the two breached peace bonds signed after their earlier detention and entered Jantar Mantar despite restrictions on protests at the site. CJP, meanwhile, has challenged the action, with its supporters arguing that the protest involved raising slogans and unfurling the national flag. These competing accounts must be examined through the legal process, and the allegations against the activists should not be treated as established guilt. But there is a larger question here: what happens when the space available for peaceful political dissent begins to shrink? Jantar Mantar has long been associated with public demonstrations, giving ordinary citizens and political movements a visible platform to bring their grievances to the national capital. Its recent denotification as an authorised protest site has therefore raised concerns about where citizens can demonstrate and how restrictions are applied. A government can regulate public spaces, but those regulations must not become a means of making dissent practically impossible. The right to protest is not a favour granted to people by those in power. It is an essential part of democratic life, and the circumstances surrounding these arrests deserve transparent scrutiny.

When Political Differences Give Way to Democratic Rights

Rahul Gandhi’s public support for Saurav Das and Ashutosh Ranka adds another dimension to this episode. Only days earlier, questions were being raised about the participation of CJP as an organisation in the wider opposition mobilisation against alleged irregularities in electoral rolls and the functioning of the Election Commission. Now, with CJP leaders in custody, the response from a prominent opposition leader offers an opportunity to move beyond questions of political affiliation and consider the principle at stake. This does not mean that CJP and the Congress must agree on everything, nor does it erase the differences between independent movements and established political parties. It simply recognises that defending the right to dissent should not depend on whether the person exercising that right belongs to a friendly organisation. If the opposition believes that electoral integrity and democratic accountability are matters of national importance, then the protection of people raising those concerns must also be part of that conversation. Political parties will inevitably have their own calculations, rivalries and disagreements, but democratic rights cannot be defended selectively. Rahul’s statement does not resolve the legal questions surrounding the arrests, and public support is no substitute for due process. Nevertheless, it brings the question of solidarity into sharper focus: can different political formations stand together on a common democratic principle without demanding that they first become politically identical?

The Movements Behind the Movement

CJP’s growing visibility also invites us to look at the people and organisations that sustain public mobilisation. A movement may have recognisable leaders and a strong presence on social media, but bringing people onto the streets requires much more than public attention. Student unions, youth organisations, local organisers and volunteers often provide the networks through which a protest becomes a sustained collective effort. Organisations such as SFI, AISA and AISF, alongside DYFI and the independent communist organisation Disha, have been involved in student and youth mobilisation, while NSUI has participated in protests over related concerns through its own organisational network. Their political affiliations differ, but their presence demonstrates that public movements are rarely the work of a single organisation. As I think about the history of student politics in India, I cannot help recalling how young revolutionaries such as Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev Thapar and Chandrashekhar Azad became part of a much larger tradition of resistance, one that repeatedly drew young people into struggles over the future of the country. The circumstances today are different, but the broader question remains relevant: how do individual acts of dissent become collective action? CJP’s importance should not be measured only by its popularity or by the visibility of its leaders. Its ability to work with other organisations, communicate its decisions and build lasting networks will also determine the kind of movement it becomes.

Unity Without Surrendering Independence

There is still a legitimate question about why CJP did not have a clearly visible organisational presence at Rahul Gandhi’s march towards the Supreme Court. Some of its prominent figures were detained, and there may have been practical reasons for the absence of others. We should not assume that the decision reflected a political arrangement or an attempt to distance itself from the Congress. At the same time, a movement that seeks to defend democratic integrity should be willing to explain its choices, particularly when it is calling for a united response to concerns about electoral fairness. I have heard suggestions that sections of CJP are sympathetic to the Aam Aadmi Party. There is nothing inherently wrong with activists having political preferences, and an independent movement need not abandon its identity to cooperate with a political party. But no political affiliation should become a reason to avoid working with others on an issue that affects citizens across party lines. The same applies to the INDIA bloc: unity cannot mean that smaller movements must simply follow the decisions of larger parties. It should mean that different organisations can cooperate where their objectives overlap while retaining the freedom to disagree elsewhere. If Rahul Gandhi’s support for the detained CJP leaders can open a channel for such cooperation, the important question is whether that solidarity can extend beyond statements of support and translate into a sustained defence of the right to peaceful protest.

This Is About Democratic Integrity

Ultimately, I do not want this episode to become another contest over which party deserves credit for standing with the protesters, or which organisation should be blamed for not appearing beside another. Those questions may matter, but they are secondary to the rights at stake. The allegations surrounding the Special Intensive Revision of electoral rolls require evidence, transparent investigation and credible answers from the institutions responsible for conducting elections. Similarly, the detention of activists requires legal scrutiny, and any allegation of misconduct by the authorities must be examined rather than dismissed. Neither a political leader’s support nor a police statement should be enough to settle questions that require evidence and due process. CJP should be free to question the government, the Election Commission and even the opposition parties that publicly support it. The Congress and other opposition parties should be able to support the activists without expecting political loyalty in return. That is what genuine democratic solidarity should look like. We can disagree about parties, ideologies and strategies while recognising that the ability to question those in power belongs to everyone. This is not a fight for one party to replace another. It is a question of whether we can preserve our democratic integrity, protect the right to dissent and ensure that no citizen has to surrender their political voice simply because those in power find it inconvenient.
