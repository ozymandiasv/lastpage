Delimitation: Is India About to Redraw Its Political Balance?

The proposed expansion of the Lok Sabha may preserve the existing proportion of seats across states, but the question is whether that is enough to protect India's federal balance.

Delimitation is back in the news, and this time there is a strong possibility that the government may actually get it through Parliament. The proposed expansion of the Lok Sabha, reportedly by a uniform 50 per cent across all states, sounds reasonable at first glance. Every state gets more seats, nobody loses their existing proportion, and the House becomes larger to accommodate the country's growing population. What is there to object to? But I think this is precisely the kind of proposal we should examine very carefully before accepting it as a simple mathematical adjustment. India's federal structure is already complicated, and representation in Parliament is not merely a matter of increasing the number of chairs in the House. It determines how political power is distributed, how regional interests are represented and how governments are formed. The southern states have been raising concerns about delimitation for years, particularly because they have managed to reduce their population growth while several northern states have continued to grow much faster. Now, a uniform increase may protect their existing share of seats, but that does not automatically resolve every concern about the political balance of the country. We are talking about a major constitutional change that could shape India's parliamentary structure for decades. Surely, something of this magnitude deserves more than a hurried political consensus.

The Mathematics of Representation

The government appears to be proposing a uniform 50 per cent increase in Lok Sabha seats, which would take the House from 543 to approximately 815 seats, although reports have also mentioned a possible maximum of 850 or more. Under an illustrative 50 per cent formula, Uttar Pradesh would go from 80 seats to around 120, while Tamil Nadu could rise from 39 to around 58 or 59, Karnataka from 28 to 42, Andhra Pradesh from 25 to around 37 or 38, and Kerala from 20 to 30. The four southern states together would gain approximately 55 to 57 additional seats, while UP alone would gain 40. Now, I understand the argument that the proportion of seats remains the same. If every state receives the same percentage increase, then yes, the existing relative distribution is preserved. But elections are ultimately decided by the number of seats won, and the political consequences of expanding the House cannot be dismissed simply because the percentages look familiar. At the same time, I don't want to make a mathematical argument that doesn't hold up. A uniform increase does not, by itself, increase UP's relative share of the Lok Sabha compared with other states. The concern is more about preserving the existing distribution, based on an older population framework, rather than allowing the latest population figures to determine representation. And that brings us to the question: should the political weight of states remain frozen because some states successfully controlled their population while others did not?

The Southern States and the Population Question

The southern states have a legitimate concern here. Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh have made significant progress in population stabilisation, education, healthcare and other social indicators. If delimitation were carried out purely on the basis of population, these states could lose a portion of their relative representation, while states with much larger population growth could gain considerably more. That would create a deeply uncomfortable situation in which states that invested in population control and human development might find themselves politically penalised for doing so. The uniform 50 per cent formula appears designed to avoid that outcome by preserving the existing distribution. But the larger question remains: how long are we going to keep using an old population basis to determine political representation? We cannot indefinitely freeze the distribution of seats without considering how India's population, economy and society have changed. At the same time, we cannot simply reward population growth with more political power and expect states to remain indifferent. This is not a simple north-versus-south argument. It is a question of how a federal democracy should balance the principle of equal representation with the need to protect states from being disadvantaged because they followed national population-stabilisation goals. There has to be a serious, transparent discussion about this, not just a formula presented as though it solves everything.

Who Draws the Boundaries?

There is another aspect of delimitation that I find equally concerning: the actual drawing of constituency boundaries. Increasing the number of seats is one thing, but deciding where those constituencies begin and end is another. The process can have a significant impact on electoral outcomes, political representation and the ability of communities to elect candidates who understand their interests. A constituency can be redrawn in ways that alter its demographic composition, merge different communities or divide existing voting blocs. This is why the independence and credibility of the Delimitation Commission are so important. The commission must be seen as impartial, transparent and insulated from political pressure. Given the controversies surrounding the functioning of various institutions in recent years, I think it is entirely reasonable for people to demand stronger safeguards. This is not an allegation that every boundary will be manipulated, nor should we assume that the commission will act improperly. But the possibility of political influence is precisely why the process must be designed to prevent it. We cannot afford a situation where a major constitutional exercise is completed and half the country is left questioning whether the boundaries were drawn fairly. If the government wants consensus, then the rules, criteria, data and safeguards should be made clear before the bill is passed, not after.

More Seats, But What About the Quality of Our Leaders?

Something else that bothers me is that we keep discussing how many representatives Parliament should have, but very rarely do we discuss the quality of the people who represent us. What exactly are we hoping to achieve by increasing the number of seats if we continue electing leaders who are uneducated, uninformed and completely disconnected from the realities of the people they govern? I don't mean that a university degree automatically makes someone a good politician. Some of the most educated people can be terrible leaders, and some people without formal education can possess extraordinary wisdom. But there is a difference between lacking a degree and lacking the basic knowledge, competence and humility required to hold public office. We have leaders who behave as though they are superior to the people who elected them, who speak down to citizens, who treat criticism as an insult and who seem to believe that winning an election gives them the right to rule rather than the responsibility to serve. And then, after years in office, many of them continue filling their pockets while the people they represent struggle with unemployment, inflation, poor schools and inadequate healthcare. We can increase the size of Parliament, redraw constituencies and change the entire distribution of seats, but if the political culture remains the same, what exactly have we improved? More representatives do not automatically mean better representation. Before we celebrate a larger House, perhaps we should also ask whether the people occupying it are capable of doing the job, whether they are accountable to their constituents and whether they understand that public office is not a personal investment scheme.

Women's Reservation: Representation or Political Accommodation?

The question of women's reservation makes this entire exercise even more skeptical. The 33 per cent reservation for women is a long-overdue step towards correcting the extraordinary underrepresentation of women in Parliament. But I cannot help wondering why the expansion of the Lok Sabha is being tied so closely to its implementation. If the reservation were applied to the existing 543 seats, approximately 179 seats would be reserved for women. If the House expands to around 815 seats, that number rises to approximately 269. Of course, the exact allocation would depend on the final framework and delimitation, but the political implications are worth examining. Expanding the House before implementing reservation could make it easier for the existing political establishment to accommodate women without having to surrender as many of its current constituencies. If reservation were applied within the existing 543 seats, some sitting MPs would inevitably face the possibility that their constituencies could become reserved for women. That would create a much more direct political adjustment. But when the House expands by hundreds of seats, the establishment gets a much larger pool of constituencies through which it can accommodate reservation while preserving much of its existing political arrangement. I am not against increasing the number of seats if there is a genuine need for it. Nor am I suggesting that every male MP will automatically retain his seat. The point is that women's representation and the expansion of Parliament are two different questions, and they deserve to be examined separately. If the objective is to give women their rightful place in Parliament, why should that require such a dramatic expansion of the House? Could we not first implement the reservation within the existing strength and then have a separate, serious discussion about the future of parliamentary representation? I would like to know whether this expansion is primarily about improving representation or whether women's reservation is also being used to make a politically difficult delimitation exercise more acceptable.

The Political Numbers Are Changing

What makes this attempt particularly interesting is the political arithmetic. The earlier constitutional amendment attempt failed because the government could not secure the required two-thirds majority. This time, the situation appears to be different. The government has reportedly been engaging with several opposition parties, and there are indications that some MPs who previously opposed the proposal may now support it, subject to conditions. Supriya Sule has said that the legislation should be examined first and that support could be considered if it serves the national interest. The NCP (SP) has also discussed conditions such as a uniform increase in seats and the simultaneous implementation of women's reservation. Meanwhile, a group of rebel TMC MPs is reportedly prepared to support the government, although their political status and proposed merger remain matters of process. DMK has also raised the question of protecting Tamil Nadu's existing share. These are not all unconditional endorsements, and the final position of several parties remains uncertain. But the numbers are clearly moving, and the government may now be closer to securing the majority it failed to obtain earlier. I would not be surprised if the bill passes this time. In fact, given the current political developments, I think there is a strong possibility that it will. But whether a bill can pass and whether it is a good bill are two entirely different questions.

Will Delimitation Do More Good Than Harm?

I am not opposed to delimitation in principle. India's population has changed enormously, and representation cannot remain frozen forever. The Lok Sabha must eventually reflect the realities of a country that has grown, urbanised and transformed itself over several decades. But the process must be fair, and the consequences must be carefully evaluated. Women's reservation should not become a political bargaining chip, and the expansion of the Lok Sabha should not be treated as a substitute for a serious discussion about federalism. The government must explain how the new seats will be allocated, how constituency boundaries will be drawn, what role the census will play and what safeguards will prevent political manipulation. There must be a clear and credible process that all states can trust. If the bill passes, we will have to see how it unfolds, and whether the promises made today are reflected in the actual legislation and its implementation. I am particularly concerned about the possibility of renewed protests in the southern states if they feel their interests have been neglected. India is not merely a collection of constituencies to be rearranged for administrative convenience. It is a union of states with different histories, economies, languages and political realities. Delimitation may be necessary, but it must strengthen that union rather than create another source of resentment. We have a chance to get this right, and we should not waste it simply because the government may finally have the numbers to pass the bill.
