Free Market Means Something Different on Each Side of the Border

How Congress just handed Trump a tariff weapon that exposes America's own double standard on Russia.

Back to back Trump blogs. A new beginning, perhaps. But that man never seems to stop being controversial. Yesterday I was writing about the White House and press freedom. Today it's India, Russia, trade, and a decision that could let Donald Trump impose tariffs of up to 100% on countries that keep buying certain things from Russia. Congress has approved legislation giving the President that authority, though that doesn't mean India or China automatically face a 100% tariff. The final call still rests with Trump. According to several reports, India is among the countries that could be hit. What makes this particularly uncomfortable is that it's all happening while India and the United States are trying to expand their economic relationship at the same time.

A Contradiction At The Centre

India has agreed to purchase around 500 billion dollars worth of American products over five years, more than twice what it currently imports from the United States each year. At the same time, America remains India's largest export market. China might be comparable to the United States in economic weight, but China has a massive domestic market plus its own manufacturing and export muscle. India doesn't have that luxury. For Indian businesses, losing easy access to the American market would be a much bigger problem. And then there's the strange contradiction sitting at the centre of the relationship. The United States wants greater access to the Indian market and has pushed India to cut tariffs and open more sectors to American products, while at the same time imposing or threatening steep taxes on Indian products entering the American market. Free market, it seems, means something different depending on which side of the border you're standing on.

The Quieter Problem

India also has another problem that's much less visible than tariffs. Technology. Our dependence on American technology is a serious issue. From advanced computing and software to the infrastructure needed for artificial intelligence and modern digital industries, there are whole areas where India remains heavily dependent on foreign companies and foreign technology. Strategic autonomy gets difficult when some of the most important tools needed to build the future are controlled somewhere else. We usually talk about foreign policy as though it's only about armies, oil and diplomatic agreements. Increasingly, it's also about who controls the technology a country needs to function. I'll write about India's technological dependence separately, since that deserves a much deeper discussion on its own.

The Double Standard

And maybe that's the uncomfortable part of this whole situation. India wants access to American markets and technology while keeping its strategic relationship with Russia intact. But the American position also raises an obvious question about consistency. The United States continues to trade with Russia itself, importing billions of dollars worth of Russian goods. Yet India is being threatened with potentially enormous tariffs just for continuing to buy Russian energy. There are legitimate differences between the categories of trade and the sanctions applied to each, but that doesn't fully erase the contradiction. If trading with Russia is treated as a problem because it props up Moscow economically, the principle shouldn't suddenly shift depending on who's doing the trading. It's hard to demand that India pick a side while you're still doing business with the same country yourself.

The Balancing Act

India's foreign policy has always tried to juggle relationships with multiple powers. Russia for energy and defence, America for investment and technology, Europe for trade, China as both a neighbour and an economic power. That balancing act gets a lot harder when those relationships start pulling in opposite directions. India's decision to keep buying Russian energy is ultimately a foreign policy choice, but so is America's decision to use trade as pressure against it. Both countries have legitimate interests here. But once one side can impose enormous costs on the other through tariffs, the relationship stops being simply about free trade. It becomes a question of leverage. And if India genuinely wants strategic autonomy, it eventually has to build an economy strong enough that autonomy is more than a phrase used in foreign policy speeches.
