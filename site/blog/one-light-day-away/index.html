One Light-Day Away

Voyager 1, humanity's most distant traveller, and the extraordinary distance between us and the universe.

Long before I heard Carl Sagan speak about the Pale Blue Dot, I had heard of Voyager 1. It was one of those fascinating objects roaming through space, almost like an interstellar expedition, carrying humanity's curiosity into places we could barely imagine. But today, Voyager 1 has travelled so far that it is about to reach a milestone no human-made object has ever achieved: one light-day away from Earth. Assuming, of course, that the Egyptians didn't build the pyramids using advanced technology and launch a spacecraft before us, this is probably the first human-made object to reach this distance. (Yes, I am being sarcastic.) Voyager 1 was launched in September 1977, nearly fifty years ago, and it is now travelling at approximately 61,000 kilometres per hour. On November 18, 2026, it will reach a distance of approximately 25.9 billion kilometres from Earth. That is not merely far away. It is a distance so enormous that even light, the fastest thing in the universe, takes an entire day to travel from Earth to the spacecraft.

How Far Is One Light-Day?

We often use light-years to describe astronomical distances, but a light-day is equally fascinating when you try to comprehend it. Light travels approximately 299,792 kilometres every second. In one day, it travels roughly 25.9 billion kilometres. The Earth is approximately 150 million kilometres from the Sun, and sunlight takes around eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. Voyager 1, at one light-day away, is therefore approximately 173 times farther from Earth than the Sun is. Imagine travelling from Earth to the Sun, then doing it again, and again, and again, more than 170 times. And even that comparison barely captures the distance. A radio signal sent from Earth to Voyager 1 now takes 24 hours to arrive. If Voyager responds immediately, another 24 hours will pass before we receive the response. You could send a command today, go to sleep, wake up tomorrow knowing it has reached the spacecraft, and then wait another entire day to find out whether it worked. The spacecraft is not experiencing any dramatic change as it crosses this milestone. It is simply continuing its journey. But for us, this distance represents something extraordinary.

The Solar System Has Already Fallen Behind

Voyager 1 has already passed through the Kuiper Belt, the region beyond Neptune containing numerous icy objects, including Pluto. It crossed the heliopause in 2012, the boundary where the influence of the solar wind gives way to the interstellar medium. It is now travelling through interstellar space, although it is still gravitationally connected to the Sun and remains within the enormous region that astronomers generally associate with our solar system. The Oort Cloud, a hypothesised vast reservoir of icy bodies surrounding the solar system, lies much farther ahead. Its inner boundary may begin thousands of astronomical units from the Sun, while its outer reaches may extend tens of thousands, possibly even around a hundred thousand astronomical units away. Voyager 1 is nowhere near it yet. At its current speed, it may reach the inner Oort Cloud in several hundred years, and could take tens of thousands of years to pass through the broader region. It is astonishing to think that we launched a spacecraft nearly half a century ago, and it has not even completed its journey through the outskirts of our own solar system.

What Lies Ahead?

The universe is not an empty highway with planets and asteroids conveniently placed along Voyager's route. Interstellar space contains gas, dust and extremely sparse particles, but the distances between objects are enormous. Voyager 1 is roughly the size of a car, and while a sufficiently large object could destroy it, the probability of such a collision is extraordinarily low simply because there is so much empty space. A tiny particle travelling at enormous velocity could potentially damage its instruments, but even that is a matter of probability rather than an inevitable threat. Voyager will continue travelling through interstellar space, passing stars at distances that are difficult to imagine, without ever necessarily encountering anything significant. It may never reach another planetary system in any meaningful sense. It may simply continue drifting through the galaxy, its trajectory carrying it towards a distant region of space that humanity will never see. There is no destination waiting for it. No planned arrival. No final stop. It is perhaps the most literal example of humanity sending something into the unknown and accepting that we may never know where it ends up.

Will Anyone Ever Find It?

Voyager 1 carries the Golden Record, a carefully selected collection of sounds, images, music and greetings from Earth, intended as a message to any extraterrestrial civilisation that might someday encounter it. Carl Sagan once described the spacecraft as carrying a message from humanity, a representation of our planet and our civilisation. But will anyone ever find it? Probably not. The spacecraft is incredibly small compared with the vastness of the galaxy, and the chances of it encountering an intelligent civilisation are practically impossible to calculate but likely extraordinarily low. It could travel for millions or billions of years without ever being discovered. It could eventually be destroyed by some collision, radiation or other event. Or perhaps, one day, an intelligent civilisation might find this strange metallic object carrying a record of a species that existed on a tiny planet around an ordinary star. They might listen to the sounds of our oceans, our music, our languages and our greetings. They might look at the images and realise that, somewhere in this vast universe, a species once existed that was curious enough to send a message into the darkness. And perhaps they might simply conclude, as Carl Sagan imagined, that man lived.

We Will Eventually Lose Contact

Voyager 1 is not immortal. Its power supply is slowly declining, and NASA has been shutting down instruments and systems to conserve what little energy remains. We may lose contact with it sometime in the 2030s, although the exact date depends on how long its remaining power and systems can continue functioning. One day, we will send a command and receive nothing. No signal. No response. Just silence. And yet the spacecraft will continue travelling. Long after its instruments have stopped functioning, long after its radio has fallen silent, it will continue moving through interstellar space. Perhaps even long after humanity itself has disappeared, Voyager 1 will still be out there. I wish it would just keep going. I wish it could survive the Earth, the Sun, humanity and everything that eventually happens to us. I wish that somewhere, billions of years from now, somebody finds it and discovers that we existed. We were a species that looked at the stars, wondered what lay beyond them and built a machine capable of travelling farther than anything we had ever made before. And even if nobody ever finds Voyager, even if it drifts forever without another soul knowing it exists, I think there is something beautiful about that. We sent a tiny piece of ourselves into the universe, and it is still going. Perhaps that is enough.
