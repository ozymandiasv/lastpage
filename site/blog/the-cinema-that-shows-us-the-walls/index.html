The Cinema That Shows Us the Walls

Malayalam cinema has never been particularly interested in making oppression comfortable to watch. It shows us something far more disturbing: how people learn to live inside the systems that hurt them.

A few days ago I watched Ponthan Mada, T. V. Chandran’s 1994 film, based on two short stories by C. V. Sreeraman and set in 1940s Kerala under British colonial rule. Mammootty plays Mada, a low-caste toddy tapper who climbs a palm tree every time he wants to speak to his landlord, Seema Thampuran, played by Naseeruddin Shah, because he is not permitted to enter the house. They talk through a window. Mada hanging from the tree, Thampuran leaning out. And between them, something that genuinely resembles love. That is the film’s unbearable genius. Thampuran is not presented as some conventional villain. He is perhaps the most emotionally present person in Mada’s life. He notices him, speaks to him, cares for him in the way the system allows him to. And Mada is proud. Genuinely, deeply proud to be seen by this man. The oppression is not dressed as cruelty. It is dressed as affection. And the system has convinced both of them, oppressor and oppressed alike, that this is simply the natural order of things. That gratitude for scraps is dignity. That love within a hierarchy is still love. That is far more disturbing than a villain simply being cruel because it shows us how oppression can survive without anyone in the room believing they are doing anything wrong.

The Same Year, The Other Film

The same year, Mammootty won the National Film Award for Best Actor, sharing the award with Mohanlal, with Ponthan Mada and Vidheyan among the performances recognised. In Vidheyan, directed by Adoor Gopalakrishnan, one of the great architects of Malayalam parallel cinema, Mammootty plays Patelar, a brutal feudal landlord who commits atrocities without hesitation or consequence. There is a scene, quiet and devastating, where a man whose wife has been raped by Patelar holds her close and finds a disturbing sense of pride and attachment in the very presence of the man who has violated her. It is difficult to watch because the film does not give us the easy reaction we expect. It doesn’t simply ask us to condemn the man. It asks us to understand how someone arrives at that place. How the mind, worn down by years of absolute powerlessness, begins to find identity in the very thing that destroys it. How subjugation, when it goes deep enough and long enough, stops feeling like subjugation and starts feeling like belonging. That Mammootty could inhabit both Mada and Patelar in the same year, moving from the meek devotion of one to the cold brutality of the other without a seam showing, is extraordinary. But leave the performance aside for a moment. Look at what these stories are willing to say about human beings.

A Cinema That Does Not Flinch

Think about Adoor Gopalakrishnan’s Elippathayam, G. Aravindan’s quietly unsettling body of work, and Thaniyavarthanam, directed by Sibi Malayil, where a man’s deteriorating mental health is met not with medicine but with rituals and exorcisms because his family is so deeply entrenched in superstition that they cannot recognise what is actually happening to him. The tragedy there is not simply his deterioration. It is everyone around him watching and seeing something else entirely. Then there is Celluloid, the film about J. C. Daniel, the man widely regarded as the father of Malayalam cinema. When Vigathakumaran was screened, caste entered even the physical space of the cinema hall, and the film’s history became inseparable from the discrimination faced by P. K. Rosy, a Dalit woman who played the female lead. The problem was not simply the film. It was the idea of someone from a community considered inferior occupying a space and appearing in a role that the social order believed did not belong to her. Even cinema, a dark room where strangers sit together for a couple of hours and watch the same story, could not escape the hierarchy outside it. Sit with that for a moment. A new medium that was supposed to bring people together was still carrying the same divisions into the theatre.

What Most Cinema Does Instead

Most cinema wants to comfort you. It wants heroes and villains with clear moral architecture, redemption arcs, catharsis, a final scene where something is resolved. It wants you to leave the hall feeling that the world makes sense, or at least that the good people are trying. Malayalam cinema, at its best, gives you none of that. It gives you humans inside systems. Systems so old and so thoroughly internalised that the people inside them have stopped noticing the walls. It gives you Mada on his palm tree, proud to be spoken to. It gives you a man whose sense of self has been so thoroughly destroyed by feudal power that even the person who represents that power becomes part of his identity. It gives you a family conducting rituals around a man who needed a doctor. It gives you a woman entering the history of Indian cinema only to be pushed out of it because the social order could not accept who she was. It does not editorialise. It does not tell you how to feel. It simply holds the frame open long enough for you to see what is actually there.

The Most Terrifying Part

The cruelty is not the hardest thing to look at. The hardest thing is the acceptance. The way oppression, given enough time, stops being something done to people and becomes something people carry inside themselves. The way it becomes culture, and culture becomes identity, and identity becomes something fiercely protected even when it is the very thing causing the wound. That is what I find so powerful about these films. They don’t simply show us bad people doing bad things. That would be easy. They show us ordinary human beings learning to live inside systems that should never have been normal in the first place. They show us how a person can become attached to the very hierarchy that humiliates them, how a family can protect the belief that is destroying one of its own members, and how a society can decide that someone’s presence itself is an offence. These films do not answer the question of how we got here. They just make sure you cannot pretend you did not ask it. And then they leave you with the part that is harder than any answer: the quiet, uncomfortable possibility that we are still here, just with different names for the same walls.
