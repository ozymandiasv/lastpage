The Election Cannot Belong to the Election Commission

When the people’s right to vote becomes dependent on an institution that refuses to explain its own decisions, democracy itself begins to lose its meaning.

The last two days have made the questions around the Election Commission much more serious. The Supreme Court has now made it clear that it did not approve the modification to Form 6, despite the Election Commission previously saying that the declaration attached to Form 6 had been upheld by the Court. The Commission has since offered another explanation, saying that Form 6 itself was not changed and that an additional declaration was attached during SIR. Whatever the legal distinction eventually turns out to be, the public should not have to watch the Supreme Court and the constitutional authority responsible for elections appear to contradict each other over something as fundamental as voter registration. And when an electoral exercise has involved the removal of crores of names from rolls, the burden of explanation becomes enormous. This is no longer something that can be brushed aside with press statements. Gyanesh Kumar has to answer.

What Exactly Are We Giving the ECI the Power to Do?

Form 7 is not some obscure administrative document. It is the form through which an objection can be made against the inclusion of a person’s name or a request can be made to delete a name from an electoral roll, including on grounds such as death, permanent shifting, duplication or lack of citizenship. The problem is not that such a mechanism exists; obviously, dead people and duplicate entries should be removed. The problem is what happens when the mechanism can potentially be weaponised. We have already seen allegations in Karnataka involving bulk Form 7 applications targeting voters, including allegations involving Muslim voters and BJP-linked individuals. The Election Commission itself has previously acknowledged a Karnataka case where thousands of Form 7 applications were found to be incorrect. That should tell us exactly why every deletion must be traceable, challengeable and independently verifiable. A voter possessing a voter ID should not suddenly discover that the state has erased their political existence without a transparent explanation and an effective opportunity to challenge it. And this is why SIR needs much more scrutiny than it is receiving. If the purpose is genuinely to identify dead voters, shifted voters and duplicates, then go to the door. Verify the person. Find the dead. Find the people who have moved. Correct the roll. But do not build an electoral system where enormous databases can be altered without every individual being able to see what information the government holds about them, why their name has been retained or removed, and who made that decision. Electoral data should not be treated like a mysterious government database that citizens are expected to trust blindly. We trust government records precisely because they are supposed to be accountable to us. And if private companies or opaque technological systems are involved in maintaining or processing voter information, that deserves scrutiny too. The citizen must remain the owner of the democratic record, not merely a subject inside someone else’s database.

Power Must Ultimately Belong to the People

There is another issue that deserves far more attention: the statutory immunity given to the CEC and Election Commissioners under Section 16 of the 2023 law. It prevents courts from entertaining or continuing civil or criminal proceedings for acts, things or words done in the course of their official duties. Such protection may have been presented as necessary to protect the independence of the Commission, but independence cannot mean immunity from accountability. If a constitutional authority is given enormous power over who gets to participate in an election, there must also be a meaningful legal mechanism to investigate and prosecute genuine wrongdoing. Otherwise, the institution becomes both extraordinarily powerful and extraordinarily difficult to hold accountable. That provision should face constitutional scrutiny because a law cannot place an executive statutory shield above the basic principles of equality before law, judicial review and constitutional accountability. If the provision is found to cross those limits, it should be struck down. I am not saying that every allegation against the Election Commission has been proved. They have not. But that is precisely why there must be an independent, transparent investigation instead of an endless battle of statements. If Gyanesh Kumar is found to have deliberately or unlawfully manipulated electoral processes, then resignation should not be the end of the matter; criminal accountability must follow where the law permits it. And if the allegations are false, the same transparent investigation should establish that too. What cannot continue is an election process in which citizens are expected to simply trust the institution while the institution refuses to provide answers that satisfy even basic public scrutiny. The Election Commission does not own our elections. The government does not own our elections. Political parties do not own our elections. We, the people, do. The power to vote is the power to decide who governs us, and no institution should ever become so powerful that it can quietly decide who gets to exercise that power.
