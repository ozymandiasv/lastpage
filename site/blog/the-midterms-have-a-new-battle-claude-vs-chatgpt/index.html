The Midterms Have a New Battle: Claude vs ChatGPT

Behind America’s political campaigns, two AI philosophies are fighting for influence. One wants stronger safeguards, the other fears that regulation could slow the future. And ordinary people are caught between them.

A couple of days ago, I wrote about the American midterm elections and why the results could become a serious problem for Donald Trump. But there is another battle unfolding alongside the usual political contest, and it involves two names most of us probably associate with chatbots rather than American politics: Claude and ChatGPT. The AI industry has entered the election campaign with enormous amounts of money, using political action committees to support candidates who favour their preferred approach to regulating artificial intelligence. Reports suggest that AI-linked political groups have raised around $100 million and spent nearly $60 million on election-related campaigns. What makes this particularly interesting is that the disagreement is not simply between two competing companies trying to sell better products. It is a disagreement about how much freedom we should give a technology that could fundamentally change society. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, generally favours stronger safeguards and regulation, while the OpenAI-aligned political network has pushed for a lighter regulatory environment and faster development. These are not literally Claude and ChatGPT contesting congressional seats, but their competing philosophies have found their way into American electoral politics. And suddenly, the midterms have become another battleground in the future of AI.

Freedom to Experiment, or the Freedom to Cause Harm?

I find this ideological divide difficult to support completely from either side. I understand why Anthropic is concerned about AI safety. We are developing systems that can write software, analyse enormous amounts of information, assist with scientific research and increasingly perform tasks with very little human supervision. Giving companies unrestricted freedom to experiment with such powerful technology means that ordinary people could become unwilling participants in experiments whose consequences we do not fully understand. We have already seen concerns about misinformation, job displacement, surveillance, cybersecurity and the use of AI in military applications. Waiting for something catastrophic to happen before introducing safeguards would be irresponsible. At the same time, I understand the argument against excessive regulation. If governments impose restrictions that are too broad, expensive or complicated, smaller research teams may struggle to compete, useful applications may remain undeveloped, and the technology could become concentrated in the hands of a few companies that can afford compliance. We might end up protecting society from certain risks while also preventing it from benefiting from medical discoveries, scientific breakthroughs and other possibilities that AI could unlock. The freedom to test and develop technology is important, but that freedom cannot mean treating people as disposable test subjects. Equally, regulation should not become a convenient way for established companies to prevent competition. Neither unrestricted experimentation nor blanket restrictions offer a satisfactory answer.

Claude May Look More Ethical, But What Happens Next?

Claude often appears to take the more cautious approach, and I understand why people find Anthropic’s philosophy reassuring. Its emphasis on safety, model evaluations and responsible deployment feels particularly important when we are dealing with technology that is advancing faster than our institutions can understand or regulate. But I am reluctant to declare one company ethical and another irresponsible. Companies have shareholders, investors, commercial ambitions and enormous financial incentives. Even an organisation that begins with strong principles may find those principles tested when competition intensifies and billions of dollars are at stake. Anthropic itself operates in a competitive industry, and its safety commitments will have to survive that pressure. OpenAI’s argument that excessive regulation could slow innovation also deserves consideration, but innovation cannot be measured simply by how quickly we release increasingly powerful models. What concerns me is that the people who will experience the consequences of these decisions have very little influence over them. We are allowing a handful of technology companies, wealthy investors and politicians to shape a future that will affect almost everyone. Perhaps the most important question is not whether Claude or ChatGPT wins this political battle, but whether we can build rules that protect people without suffocating research and competition. AI is dangerous precisely because its potential is so enormous, and we still do not know where its limits are. I would rather see a world where we proceed carefully than one where we discover the consequences after the damage is done. But I also do not want a future in which fear becomes an excuse to underutilise one of the most transformative technologies humanity has developed. Finding that balance may prove considerably harder than building the technology itself.
