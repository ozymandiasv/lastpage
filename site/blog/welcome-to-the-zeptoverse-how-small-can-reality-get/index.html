Welcome to the Zeptoverse: How Small Can Reality Get?

From particles a trillionth of a billionth of a metre across to the mysteries of dark matter and the origins of our universe.

We spend much of our lives looking at things that are either enormous or microscopic, but I wonder how often we actually stop to think about the scale of the universe. We can look at galaxies millions of light-years away, examine cells under microscopes and even study individual atoms. But what happens when we go smaller than an atom, smaller than its nucleus, smaller than the particles that make up that nucleus? This is where things get particularly interesting. On 3 November 2026, scientists working at CERN's Large Hadron Collider announced the first experimental evidence of an extraordinarily rare particle decay after more than four decades of searching. The result has brought physicists closer to exploring something they call the Zeptoverse, a realm of incredibly small distances where unknown particles and fundamental forces might be hiding. A zeptometre is \(10^{-21}\) metres, or one trillionth of a billionth of a metre. To put that into perspective, an ordinary atom is roughly 100 billion times larger than a zeptometre. Imagine taking something already invisible to the naked eye, shrinking it another hundred billion times, and then trying to investigate what happens there. That is the scale we are talking about. And somehow, we are beginning to explore it.

How Do You Investigate Something You Cannot See?

The interesting part is that scientists aren't actually looking at individual objects at zeptometre distances through some extraordinarily powerful microscope. They are using mathematics, particle collisions and the behaviour of particles to investigate what might exist at these scales. At CERN, the Large Hadron Collider accelerates particles to enormous energies and smashes them together. These collisions produce short-lived particles that quickly decay into other particles. The LHCb experiment studies particles containing beauty quarks, which are heavier relatives of the up and down quarks that make up ordinary matter. The recent result involved a neutral beauty meson decaying into two muons, which are heavier relatives of electrons. This process is extraordinarily rare, expected to happen roughly once in every ten billion beauty particles. By combining years of observations and using advanced machine-learning techniques to separate the signal from background noise, researchers obtained evidence with a statistical significance of 3.2 sigma. This is an important milestone, although it is not yet a confirmed discovery by the usual five-sigma standard. More importantly, the experiment doesn't directly photograph the zeptoverse. Instead, scientists study how unknown particles might influence the behaviour of particles they can already observe. Think of it as trying to understand something invisible by examining the effects it leaves behind.

Beyond the Zeptoverse: The Planck Length

And if you think a zeptometre is difficult to imagine, we haven't even reached the most extreme scale in this discussion. There is something called the Planck length, approximately \(1.6 \times 10^{-35}\) metres. Let that number sink in for a moment. It is around 100 trillion times smaller than a zeptometre. Even the distances we are beginning to investigate through the Zeptoverse are enormous compared with the Planck length. At this scale, our current understanding of physics is expected to become incomplete because quantum mechanics and general relativity, our two extraordinarily successful descriptions of nature, do not fit together into a complete theory of quantum gravity. Scientists suspect that understanding this scale could help explain what spacetime itself is made of, how gravity behaves at the quantum level and what happened during the earliest moments of the universe. However, there is an important distinction: the Planck length is not an experimentally established smallest possible unit of space. It is a scale at which our existing theories suggest that new physics becomes necessary. We don't know whether space is actually divided into tiny units or whether something entirely different happens there. We haven't experimentally explored anything close to this scale. In fact, the gap between what we can investigate today and the Planck length is almost absurdly enormous. We are still trying to understand the staircase before we even reach the basement.

The Higgs Boson and the Mystery of Mass

You might remember the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012, another major achievement at CERN. To understand why it matters here, we need to understand the Higgs field. Physicists believe that space is filled with this field, and certain fundamental particles interact with it. The strength of that interaction determines their mass. The Higgs boson is the associated particle of this field, and its discovery confirmed a crucial part of the Standard Model of particle physics. But here's something interesting: the Higgs mechanism doesn't explain all the mass we see around us. Most of the mass of ordinary matter, including protons and neutrons, comes from the energy associated with the strong nuclear force holding their constituent quarks and gluons together. So where does the Zeptoverse enter this discussion? Scientists are investigating whether undiscovered particles might interact with the Higgs field. One possibility is known as the Higgs portal, a hypothetical connection through which dark matter particles could interact with ordinary matter via the Higgs boson. If such interactions exist, studying the Higgs boson and other particle processes could help reveal them. We haven't found evidence establishing this connection yet, but it is one of the reasons physicists are so interested in exploring physics beyond the Standard Model.

Dark Matter: The Universe We Cannot See

Now comes perhaps the biggest mystery of all. When astronomers observe galaxies, they notice something strange. Stars on the outer edges of galaxies move much faster than we would expect if the visible matter alone were responsible for their gravitational attraction. Galaxy clusters and gravitational lensing observations provide further evidence that there is considerably more matter in the universe than we can see. Scientists call this missing component dark matter. It doesn't appear to emit or absorb light in the way ordinary matter does, but its gravitational influence is measurable. According to our current cosmological model, ordinary matter accounts for approximately 5% of the universe's total mass-energy content, dark matter around 27%, and dark energy approximately 68%. We have yet to identify what dark matter actually consists of. It could be made of particles that interact extremely weakly with ordinary matter, or something we haven't even considered. Some theoretical models suggest that these particles could interact with the Higgs field, while other possibilities involve entirely different particles and forces. This is why investigating rare particle decays at CERN is important. Unknown particles could influence these decays even if they are too massive to produce directly in our current experiments. If future measurements reveal differences between experimental observations and the Standard Model's predictions, they could provide clues about new physics. The recent Zeptoverse result, however, agrees with the Standard Model. It has not discovered dark matter or a new fundamental force. It has given scientists another powerful way to investigate where those discoveries might be hiding.

We Know So Little, And That's The Interesting Part

What fascinates me about all this is the contrast between how much humanity has achieved and how much remains unexplained. We have discovered the Higgs boson, identified dozens of fundamental particles and developed mathematical theories capable of predicting their behaviour with extraordinary accuracy. We have built a machine several kilometres underground that recreates conditions in which particles collide at energies we could never encounter in everyday life. Yet we still don't know what dark matter is, what dark energy actually represents, how gravity fits into quantum mechanics or what spacetime looks like at the Planck scale. The Zeptoverse isn't a discovery of a new universe, nor have scientists suddenly found an entirely new set of particles. It is an opportunity to investigate nature at distances we have barely begun to explore. And perhaps that's the most exciting part. Every time we improve our instruments, we discover another layer of questions waiting for us. We once thought atoms were indivisible. Then we discovered electrons, protons and neutrons. We eventually discovered that protons and neutrons contain quarks. Now we are trying to understand what might be happening at even smaller scales. We might not find what we expect, and we might have to rewrite parts of what we currently believe. But that's science. The universe doesn't owe us an explanation simply because we have finally learned how to ask better questions. Peace.
