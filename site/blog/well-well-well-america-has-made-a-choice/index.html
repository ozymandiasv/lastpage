Well, Well, Well. America Has Made a Choice

The 2026 midterms, a Democratic comeback, and a reminder that even the most powerful presidents need voters.

Well, well, well. Look who has decided to vote smartly! America has made a rather interesting choice this time. The Democrats have taken control of both the House of Representatives and the Senate in the 2026 midterm elections, delivering a significant blow to Donald Trump and his administration. Now, I wouldn't call this the end of Trump, nor would I pretend that America has suddenly become a completely different country overnight. But it is certainly a message, and quite a loud one. Two years ago, Trump returned to the White House with a mandate from voters who believed he could fix the economy, control immigration and restore America's standing. Now, halfway through his term, those same voters have handed his opposition control of Congress. Democracy has a funny way of reminding politicians that the people who put them in power can also take away their political comfort.

A President Who Has Lost His Congress

The importance of this result goes beyond the number of seats won. The United States Congress has two chambers: the House of Representatives and the Senate. The House controls legislation involving taxation and government spending, while the Senate has enormous influence over appointments, legislation and foreign policy matters. With Democrats controlling both, Trump will find it considerably harder to push his agenda through Congress. They can block legislation, scrutinise his administration, investigate government decisions and challenge his nominations. This doesn't mean Democrats can simply reverse everything Trump has done. The President still has executive powers and can veto legislation. But the political landscape has changed dramatically. Trump spent much of his political career presenting himself as someone who could get things done because he wasn't constrained by the usual political establishment. Well, the establishment has just changed its seating arrangement.

The Economy, the War and the Trump Effect

There are several reasons behind this result, but the cost of living appears to have been one of the biggest concerns. Voters who expected economic relief are still dealing with rising costs, while the unpopular war with Iran has added another layer of frustration. Democrats also managed to win in places that were traditionally considered safe Republican territory. Kansas elected a Democratic senator for the first time in nearly a century, and Texas and South Carolina, states Trump carried comfortably in 2024, also saw major Democratic victories. This is particularly significant because it suggests that the result wasn't simply Democrats winning back their usual supporters. They managed to attract voters in places where Republicans had previously enjoyed a considerable advantage. Trump's endorsements and campaigning couldn't prevent several Republican candidates from losing either. Perhaps the biggest lesson here is that political loyalty has limits. You can build a movement around a personality, but eventually people still expect their lives to improve.

A Warning, Not a Revolution

I wouldn't mistake this election for an unconditional endorsement of the Democratic Party. American voters have their own frustrations with Democrats, and winning an election is considerably easier than governing a country. The Democrats now have an opportunity to investigate the administration, challenge its policies and offer an alternative. What they do with that opportunity will matter just as much as the victory itself. But for now, this is a significant moment in American politics. Trump wanted voters to treat these midterms as a referendum on his presidency, and in many ways, they did. The result is a Congress that will no longer simply follow his lead. Perhaps the most important reminder is that elections aren't coronations. No matter how powerful a president believes himself to be, there is always another election, another ballot and another opportunity for people to say they've had enough. And sometimes, they do.

And there will be consequences

The consequence is where the election gets particularly interesting. A Democratic-controlled Congress gives the opposition something much more powerful than the ability to criticise Trump; the ability to investigate him and potentially initiate impeachment proceedings. The House can summon witnesses, demand documents, investigate decisions made by the administration and examine possible conflicts of interest. Democrats have also not ruled out a third impeachment of Trump. And yes, this is constitutionally possible. The House of Representatives can impeach a president with a simple majority vote. But here’s the important distinction: impeachment does not automatically remove a president from office. It is essentially the formal charging of the president with serious wrongdoing. The Senate then conducts a trial, and conviction requires a two-thirds majority of senators present. Only then can the president be removed. Trump has already been impeached twice, in 2019 and 2021, but was acquitted by the Senate both times. Interestingly, I learnt something about the Indian system while looking into this. We use the word impeachment in India too, but the process is quite different. In India, impeachment of the President is specifically for violation of the Constitution. Either House of Parliament can initiate it, but the resolution must pass with a two-thirds majority of the total membership of that House, followed by the same requirement in the other House. It is not the American arrangement of one chamber bringing charges and the other conducting a trial with a two-thirds conviction threshold. The removal of Indian judges is another process altogether, although it is commonly described as impeachment. In the US, meanwhile, Democrats now control both chambers, but they still don’t have the numbers to remove Trump by themselves. With a Senate majority of around 54, they would need substantial Republican support to reach the required two-thirds. So, can this election pull Trump down? Potentially, yes. Automatically, absolutely not. The more immediate consequence is that his administration will face investigations, resistance and much closer scrutiny. The possibility of a third impeachment is real; removal is a much higher hurdle.
