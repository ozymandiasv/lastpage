What Does It Mean to Be a Developing Nation When Our Children Are Hungry?

Three in every ten Indian children under five suffer from stunted growth. What does that say about the country we are building?

I came across a report on the Global Hunger Index, and what stayed with me was not India's rank, but a number I could hardly believe: nearly three in every ten Indian children under five suffer from stunting, a condition in which chronic undernutrition impairs their growth and development. Thirty per cent. Three children out of every ten. I live in a city, surrounded by schools, restaurants, supermarkets and the ordinary comforts of urban life. Perhaps that has made it easier for me to remain unaware of the scale of deprivation that exists beyond what I see every day. I knew poverty existed. I knew children went hungry. But knowing something in the abstract and confronting its scale are two entirely different things. Somewhere in this country, a child is growing up without the nutrition necessary for their body and brain to develop properly, while the rest of us go about our lives as though this is an unfortunate but distant reality. That child did not choose their circumstances. They did not choose their parents' income, the village they were born in or the opportunities available to them. Yet the consequences will follow them for years, perhaps for the rest of their lives. I cannot wrap my head around the fact that this is happening on such a scale in a country that speaks so confidently about its rise, its ambitions and its future.

A Childhood Should Not Begin With Deprivation

What makes this particularly painful is that we are not talking about an impossible problem. We are talking about food, nutrition, functioning schools and the basic conditions required for a child to have a fair chance at life. We have government schemes, school meals and public institutions meant to protect the most vulnerable, yet we continue to confront the consequences of deprivation on a staggering scale. Reports of government schools shutting down, children being denied access to education, and meals being reduced to the cheapest possible provisions should make us question our priorities. A school meal might be the most nutritious food a child eats that day. For some, it may be the only dependable meal they can count on. What does it say about us if even that becomes an afterthought? A school is not merely a building where children learn to read and write. It can be a source of nourishment, safety, opportunity and hope. When schools disappear or their essential services fail, the damage extends far beyond the classroom. We cannot expect children to concentrate, learn and build a future when their bodies are struggling with hunger. Education without nutrition is an incomplete promise, and nutrition without access to education leaves another promise unfulfilled.

Politics Without Priorities

I find it difficult to look at Indian politics without feeling angry about the distance between the things we argue over and the things that should matter most. Political parties fight over power, leaders cultivate their images, and public debate is consumed by religious identities, historical grievances, ideological battles and the endless competition to prove who loves the country more. Meanwhile, children are growing up without the nutrition they need to develop. What greater national failure can there be than allowing a child's circumstances at birth to determine whether they receive the food, healthcare and education necessary to live a healthy life? I am not suggesting that hunger has a single cause or that every failure can be blamed on one government. Poverty, sanitation, maternal health, food security and access to public services are interconnected problems that require sustained work. But that is precisely the point: these problems demand political attention beyond election cycles and publicity campaigns. A country cannot measure its progress only by the wealth it creates; it must also ask whether its children are getting enough to eat. If political power does not translate into better lives for those who need help most, what exactly are we celebrating?

Their Circumstances Are Not Their Fault

If you have been fortunate enough to afford your children's education, feed them properly and give them the opportunities you never had, please remember that your experience is not everyone's reality. Somewhere, three out of every ten children under five are facing the consequences of chronic undernutrition. They are not statistics detached from our lives. They are children who deserve the same chance to grow, learn, dream and discover what they might become. Their hunger is not their failure, and their poverty is not a reflection of their worth. We need a better country, not merely a richer one; a country where a child's future is not compromised before they are old enough to understand what poverty means. Patriotism cannot be limited to flags, slogans and declarations of national pride. It must include the willingness to confront the suffering that exists within our own borders, even when it makes us uncomfortable. I do not know what it will take for us to treat this crisis with the urgency it deserves, but I know that we should be ashamed if we can argue endlessly about the greatness of our nation while accepting that millions of its children are growing up without adequate nutrition. A country that cannot ensure its children are properly fed has no business being satisfied with its own progress.
