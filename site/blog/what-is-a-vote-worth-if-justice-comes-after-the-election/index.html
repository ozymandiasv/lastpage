What Is a Vote Worth If Justice Comes After the Election?

One line from the Chief Justice, and why it misses what a vote actually means to the person casting it.

On September 28, the Supreme Court was asked to advance the hearing of a West Bengal Special Intensive Revision matter involving more than 18,000 pending appeals from voters whose names had been affected by the electoral-roll revision. The reason for the urgency was straightforward: the Rejinagar and Nandigram by-elections are scheduled for October 6, and the lawyers argued that some of these voters had already been prevented from voting once and could potentially be prevented from voting again. The bench headed by Chief Justice Surya Kant declined to advance the hearing and said the matter would be heard on October 5, one day before polling. During the exchange, the CJI remarked, “These are just bye-elections.” That sentence bothers me, not because a by-election is constitutionally identical to a general election, but because a vote does not become less important simply because the election is called a by-election. For the individual whose name has been removed or whose appeal remains unresolved, this isn’t a small election. It may be the only opportunity that person has to participate in that particular constituency’s election. If the court hears the matter on October 5 and polling takes place on October 6, there is an obvious question: what meaningful remedy can be provided to somebody who is finally found to have been wrongly excluded, but only after the opportunity to vote has effectively passed?

Ashby v White and the Problem of a Remedy That Comes Too Late

There is a remarkably old case that makes this problem even more interesting. In Ashby v White (1703), Matthew Ashby claimed that a returning officer had wrongfully prevented him from voting in an election. The jury initially awarded him £5 in damages. The King’s Bench majority rejected the action, but Chief Justice John Holt dissented. His reasoning became famous: if someone possesses a legal right, there must be a means of vindicating that right when it is violated. More importantly, Holt recognised the peculiar nature of a lost vote: once the election has happened, the vote itself cannot simply be returned to the voter. The House of Lords eventually reversed the King’s Bench decision and held that Ashby had a cause of action. The historical circumstances are obviously very different from modern India, so Ashby should not be treated as an Indian legal precedent. But the principle raises an uncomfortable question that is still relevant: is a legal right meaningful if the remedy arrives after the moment in which that right could actually be exercised? A voter who is wrongly prevented from voting on October 6 cannot be given the ballot back on October 10. The election has happened. The political choice has been made. Compensation, a declaration or a later correction may acknowledge the wrong, but none of them can recreate the lost opportunity.

India Has Recognised the Importance of the Act of Voting

Indian constitutional law makes this distinction particularly interesting. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the right to vote itself is not a fundamental right in the conventional sense. In Lily Thomas v Union of India, the Court described voting as a statutory right governed by election law. But later cases have drawn an important distinction between the legal right to vote and the freedom of voting. In People’s Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India, Justice P.V. Reddi explained that when a voter actually casts a vote, that act constitutes an expression of opinion and preference protected by Article 19(1)(a). The Court has also recognised a voter’s fundamental right to know relevant information about candidates as part of meaningful electoral choice. That distinction matters here. We shouldn’t inaccurately say that the Supreme Court has declared an absolute fundamental right to vote that cannot be regulated. It hasn’t. But Indian constitutional jurisprudence does recognise that elections are not merely administrative exercises and that the act of voting has a constitutional dimension through freedom of expression and democratic participation. The Supreme Court has also described secrecy of voting as fundamental to free and fair elections. So perhaps the real issue before the Court isn’t simply, “Is this only a by-election?” The deeper question is: what happens when the machinery responsible for determining who gets to participate in an election cannot resolve that person’s claim before the election takes place?

A Remedy After the Poll Is Not the Same as a Remedy Before It

This is where I think the Court’s timing deserves scrutiny. The Supreme Court itself has recognised, in another West Bengal context, that delay around electoral disputes can create serious practical problems. Just days earlier, while dealing with the dispute over the Trinamool Congress name and symbol, the Court pressed the Election Commission for a reasonable and expeditious timeline, specifically noting the importance of resolving the issue in the context of the approaching by-elections. That makes the October 5 hearing particularly striking. The Court may ultimately have perfectly valid procedural reasons for refusing an earlier hearing, and we should not assume from one remark that the Court is indifferent to voting rights. But the practical consequence remains: if a voter wins their appeal on October 5, they may potentially be restored in time; if the matter takes longer, the legal victory may arrive after the electoral opportunity has disappeared. And that is where Ashby v White becomes relevant—not as binding Indian law, but as a reminder from more than three centuries ago that a right which cannot be effectively exercised because of an official error raises a problem that cannot always be solved by a remedy delivered later.

An Election Is Not “Just” an Election

I don’t think the answer is to demand that courts interfere with every election process whenever there is a dispute. Elections need certainty too. Endless litigation can itself undermine democracy. The Election Commission and courts have to balance competing considerations, and the legal system has established procedures for electoral-roll disputes and election petitions. But there is something worth questioning here. For a voter, there is no such thing as “just a by-election.” There is only an election in which they either get to participate or they don’t. A citizen does not experience democracy as an abstract constitutional principle. They experience it when their name appears on the electoral roll, when they walk into a polling station, when their identity is verified and when they are finally allowed to make that choice behind the voting compartment. If the system prevents them from reaching that final moment and only later tells them that they were entitled to participate, something important has already been lost. The question, therefore, is not whether the Supreme Court should favour one political party or another. It is whether electoral justice has to arrive before the vote becomes impossible, rather than merely proving afterwards that the voter was right. Because once October 6 passes, nobody can give that particular voter their October 6 vote back. And that may be the most important lesson hiding inside a seemingly small by-election.
