When the Charges Do Not Match the Accusation

How a missed statutory deadline in a terror case reveals more about the system than about the accused.

There is something uncomfortable about the way words like terrorist, terror conspiracy and national security are used by the state. These are not ordinary accusations, and I find it difficult to read them as just another part of a criminal investigation. Once such words are attached to someone’s name, the consequences begin long before a court determines guilt. Liberty can be taken away, reputations can be destroyed and years can pass while an investigation continues. That is precisely why I think the more extraordinary the accusation, the more carefully the machinery behind it should be examined. The recent case involving American national Matthew Aaron VanDyke made me stop and look at that machinery. VanDyke and six Ukrainian nationals were arrested by the National Investigation Agency in March over allegations that they had conspired to carry out terrorist activities and provide drone-warfare training to Myanmar-based armed groups. The original case involved the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, or UAPA. But when the 180-day period for completing the investigation expired on September 8, the NIA filed a chargesheet invoking Sections 21 and 23 of the Immigration and Foreigners Act, 2025, while keeping the UAPA investigation open.

What Exactly Is a Technicality?

The word technicality is often used as though it describes some annoying loophole that allowed a guilty person to escape justice. I have never understood that way of looking at it. If a deadline is written into the law, then respecting that deadline is not a technicality; it is part of respecting the law. When the state is given extraordinary powers to investigate and detain people under an extraordinary law, there have to be limits on those powers as well. Otherwise an investigation can quietly become an indefinite period of incarceration. VanDyke did not walk free because a court examined the terrorism allegations and declared them false. He received default bail because the statutory period for completing the investigation had expired without the relevant charges being included in the chargesheet. A Delhi court described the chargesheet as “piecemeal” because it dealt with the immigration offences while the UAPA investigation remained incomplete. The court did not acquit him, and the NIA has maintained that its investigation is continuing. That distinction matters to me because it leaves behind a rather uncomfortable question. If the original case was serious enough to involve UAPA allegations, why was the chargesheet filed within the statutory deadline limited to immigration offences while the UAPA investigation remained unfinished? There may be legitimate reasons. Complex investigations take time, evidence may still be collected and the law permits further investigation. None of this automatically means that the original allegations were fabricated or that the NIA acted improperly. But the seriousness of the accusation makes the question worth asking. When the state tells the public that someone is connected to terrorism, I think the public is entitled to understand what happened when the legal deadline arrived and why the most serious allegations were not part of the chargesheet filed at that point.

The Accusation Cannot Become the Conviction

This becomes even more important when looking at the broader operation of UAPA. The law is designed to deal with serious threats, but its stringent provisions can have consequences that extend for years before a trial reaches its conclusion. A recent Indian Express report from Maharashtra, for example, found 31 pending UAPA cases in one court in Gadchiroli, with many unresolved for more than five years. The restrictive bail provisions can mean that accused people remain in prison for long periods while their trials are still progressing slowly. The Umar Khalid case illustrates the same tension at a national level. Khalid has been in custody since September 2020 in the Delhi riots larger-conspiracy case, and the Supreme Court denied him bail in January 2026. Later, a separate Supreme Court bench referred broader questions concerning bail under special statutes, prolonged incarceration and Article 21 to a larger bench. I don’t think these cases should be casually equated. The allegations, evidence and circumstances are different. But I do think they raise the same basic question about the relationship between accusation and punishment. Someone being accused of terrorism does not make them a terrorist, just as someone receiving bail does not make them innocent. That sounds obvious, but in public discussion we often forget it. The accusation becomes the identity, and the trial becomes something that comes afterwards. It should be the other way around. An accusation is supposed to be tested through evidence, procedure and judicial scrutiny. It should never become a substitute for any of them.

Extraordinary Powers Need Extraordinary Care

There is an obvious reason governments need strong laws against terrorism. Genuine terrorist conspiracies cannot be treated like ordinary criminal offences, and investigators need sufficient time and powers to uncover complicated networks. I have no difficulty accepting that. But the other side of that argument is just as important to me: the more power the state receives, the more important the safeguards become. If a person can remain imprisoned for years because a case is classified under a stringent anti-terror law, then the investigation cannot be treated casually. If the state misses a statutory deadline, that cannot simply be dismissed as an unfortunate procedural inconvenience. And if the evidence required for the most serious allegations has not yet been assembled, that fact matters too. The VanDyke case is therefore less interesting to me as a question of whether one particular accused person should be considered innocent or guilty and more interesting as a question about the machinery of criminal justice itself. How much power should the state have to hold someone on the basis of an allegation before that allegation is properly supported by evidence? How long should an investigation be allowed to continue before the accused receives the protections that the law provides? And when extraordinary legislation is used, should we not expect extraordinary care in following every safeguard that comes with it? Because the rule of law is not really tested when the accused is popular, sympathetic or obviously innocent. It is tested when the accusation is frightening, the state has enormous power, and following the rules becomes inconvenient.
