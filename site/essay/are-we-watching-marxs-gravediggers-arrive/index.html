Are We Watching Marx's Gravediggers Arrive?

Automation, monopolies, artificial intelligence and the contradictions Marx believed capitalism could never escape.

Every time I read Marx, I find myself wondering how much of what he described is still unfolding around us. His predictions have not all come true, and history certainly hasn't followed the neat revolutionary timeline some Marxists once imagined. But his analysis of capitalism, particularly its tendency to expand, concentrate wealth, replace labour with machinery and periodically plunge into crises, remains remarkably relevant. Look around today. We have massive layoffs, automation, growing corporate monopolies, billionaires accumulating extraordinary wealth and governments increasingly comfortable protecting the interests of powerful businesses. What interests me is that Marx wasn't simply predicting that capitalism would produce rich people. He was examining how the system itself would develop. In The Communist Manifesto, published in 1848, Marx and Engels described how capitalism would transform societies, dissolve older economic arrangements and expand beyond national boundaries. The bourgeoisie, they argued, would create a world market, connecting distant economies through trade, production and capital. They also recognised the importance of the modern state and the centralisation of political and economic power. Capitalism was never going to remain confined to individual towns or isolated national markets. Its very nature demanded expansion, competition and the constant search for new markets. When I look at today's global supply chains, multinational corporations and financial markets, I find that particular observation difficult to dismiss.

The Machinery of Capitalism

One of Marx's most important arguments concerned machinery and the displacement of workers. In Capital, particularly Volume I, he explained how technological development allows capitalists to increase productivity while reducing their dependence on human labour relative to the scale of production. This creates what he called the industrial reserve army: people who are unemployed, underemployed or available to be absorbed whenever businesses need additional labour. Their existence also places pressure on employed workers because there is always someone else competing for their jobs. Marx understood that machinery could make society extraordinarily productive, but under capitalism, the benefits of that productivity would not automatically be distributed among everyone. The ownership of machinery and the means of production mattered. This is where computers changed the game. Automation moved beyond manufacturing and began transforming administration, accounting, communication, logistics and countless other occupations. Artificial intelligence could take this much further because it is beginning to automate tasks that previously required human cognitive labour. We are no longer discussing only machines replacing physical effort; we are discussing software capable of writing, analysing, coding, designing and processing information. That doesn't mean every job will disappear, but it does raise a fundamental question: if society can produce more with fewer workers, who gets to enjoy the benefits of that increased productivity?

From Competition to Concentration

Marx also examined how competition could gradually produce its opposite. Capital accumulates, successful businesses expand, weaker competitors are absorbed or eliminated, and ownership becomes increasingly concentrated. He distinguished between the concentration of capital through accumulation and the centralisation of capital through mergers, acquisitions and the consolidation of existing businesses. This is an important foundation for understanding modern monopolies and oligopolies. However, the more developed theories of monopoly capitalism came later, particularly through thinkers such as Rudolf Hilferding and Vladimir Lenin. Look at the technology industry today. A relatively small number of companies control enormous portions of cloud infrastructure, digital advertising, operating systems, online commerce and AI computing. These companies don't merely sell products; they increasingly control the infrastructure upon which other businesses depend. This is where crony capitalism becomes particularly interesting. The ideal of a competitive free market starts looking rather different when the largest corporations can influence regulations, secure favourable contracts, lobby governments and use their financial power to protect their positions. Competition remains the language, but concentration increasingly becomes the reality. And the more capital accumulates in fewer hands, the greater the question of who actually controls the productive capacity of society.

The Computer Was a Beginning. AI Could Be a Turning Point.

I think computers marked one of the most significant turning points in this process, but artificial intelligence could introduce another. The industrial revolution mechanised physical labour; computers mechanised information processing; AI is beginning to mechanise portions of intellectual labour itself. Consider the implications. A company that once required hundreds of employees for customer support, documentation, research or routine software development might increasingly accomplish parts of that work with a much smaller workforce. Even if new occupations emerge, there is no guarantee that the people displaced will possess the skills, financial resources or time required to transition into them. Meanwhile, the companies owning the computing infrastructure, intellectual property and platforms could capture a disproportionate share of the economic benefits. This is not necessarily an argument against automation. Why should humanity reject technology that can reduce the amount of labour required to produce goods and services? The problem is the economic arrangement through which those benefits are distributed. If machines make production cheaper and more efficient, theoretically we could work fewer hours, enjoy better living standards and spend more time pursuing things beyond employment. But under a system where access to basic necessities depends on selling one's labour, becoming less necessary to the production process can become a threat to one's livelihood. That contradiction is precisely what makes the AI transition so important.

Who Are the Gravediggers?

Marx famously wrote that capitalism would produce its own gravediggers. He believed that industrial capitalism would bring workers together, concentrate them within large production systems and create the conditions for collective organisation. The very development of capitalism would therefore strengthen the class whose interests fundamentally contradicted those of the capitalist class. But I think we should ask what that looks like today. Workers are increasingly fragmented across contractors, platforms, temporary employment and remote workplaces. Some work from offices, some from factories, some from their homes and others spend entire days on the streets delivering food, transporting passengers or completing tasks through digital platforms. They may never meet the people performing similar work for the same corporation. That makes collective organisation considerably more complicated. Yet economic insecurity, layoffs, stagnant wages and deteriorating working conditions continue to create common grievances. The wealthy have also developed ways of managing these tensions through welfare programmes, temporary financial assistance, consumer credit, subsidies and political concessions. There is nothing inherently wrong with social protection, of course. But if these measures merely provide enough relief to keep people dependent while leaving the underlying distribution of economic power untouched, how sustainable is that arrangement? How long can a system keep asking people to accept insecurity while presenting extraordinary wealth at the other end as the natural reward for success?

How Long Can the Contradiction Last?

I don't know whether we are approaching the kind of revolutionary transformation Marx anticipated. History has repeatedly shown that capitalism can adapt, absorb criticism, introduce reforms and survive crises that once appeared existential. Workers have won rights, welfare systems have expanded in many countries, and democratic institutions have created mechanisms for redistributing some of the wealth generated by capitalism. We should not ignore those developments or assume that economic contradictions automatically produce revolutions. But I do think the questions Marx raised are becoming increasingly difficult to avoid. If AI and automation make human labour less necessary, why should access to food, housing, healthcare and a dignified life remain so dependent on employment? If a handful of corporations own the infrastructure responsible for an increasing share of economic production, what happens to everyone else? Perhaps the wealthy will continue offering enough concessions to manage public anger. Perhaps governments will introduce new forms of social protection. Or perhaps the contradictions will eventually become too difficult to contain. Marx's gravedigger metaphor was never simply about poor people becoming angry. It was about a system creating the social conditions that could eventually challenge its own foundations. And when I look at the direction in which technology, wealth and ownership are moving, I cannot help wondering whether we are beginning to see those conditions take shape.
