Gandhi: A Man India Needed

To understand a historical figure is to see both what he gave us and where he was wrong.

Another Gandhi Jayanti, and somehow we seem to be losing the ability to speak about Gandhi as a historical human being rather than as either a saint or a villain. I have immense respect for Gandhi, even though I have plenty of reasons to disagree with him and would certainly not call myself his biggest fan. But I cannot look at his flaws and use them to erase what he contributed. What bothers me even more is watching young people confidently blame Gandhi for things they have barely studied, repeating claims about him without really understanding the circumstances in which he lived or the choices he made. Perhaps political movements have played their part in reshaping his reputation, and perhaps there is also something fashionable about blaming a historical figure simply because it feels rebellious or intellectually convenient. But Gandhi was a man, and India needed that man at that particular moment for particular reasons.

The Man Who Made Resistance Mass Politics

Gandhi did not invent civil disobedience. Henry David Thoreau had written about it long before Gandhi, and resistance to colonial rule in India existed before Gandhi became its central figure. But Gandhi did something extraordinary with the idea. He turned resistance into a mass political method. Satyagraha, non-cooperation, boycotts, marches and civil disobedience were not simply theories discussed among intellectuals; they became things ordinary people could participate in. The power of Gandhi’s politics came partly from this ability to take an idea and make it understandable to millions. He gave people a way to resist without requiring them to become soldiers, politicians or revolutionaries in the traditional sense. The British were not defeated by Gandhi alone, and it would be historically dishonest to pretend otherwise. The World Wars, Britain’s declining economic and political power, international decolonisation and the work of countless Indian leaders all played enormous roles. But those circumstances might have produced a very different outcome without a mass Indian movement capable of turning independence into a collective demand.

The Unity Was Something Different

When I say Gandhi united India, I do not mean the political integration of the princely states that we associate with Sardar Patel. I mean something much broader: the creation of a feeling that independence was not the concern of an educated political class alone. Farmers, workers, merchants, students, women, industrialists, lawyers and people from vastly different social backgrounds could all see themselves as participants in the same struggle. That unity was never perfect, and India remained deeply divided by caste, religion, language, class and region. Gandhi certainly did not erase those divisions. But he helped create a political movement in which those differences could temporarily exist alongside a larger idea of belonging to one country. That is an enormous achievement. Independence was not simply negotiated in rooms between British officials and Indian leaders; it became something millions of Indians had participated in demanding.

Why I Still Disagree With Gandhi

But respect does not require worship, and this is where my own discomfort with Gandhi begins. His approach to caste remains one of the strongest reasons I cannot simply celebrate everything he represented. Gandhi opposed untouchability and worked extensively against the practice, but his understanding of caste was not the same as the more radical annihilation of caste demanded by B. R. Ambedkar. His use of the word “Harijan” is particularly uncomfortable to me. It was intended as a term of dignity, but renaming an oppressed community does not necessarily challenge the social structure that created the oppression in the first place. Gandhi often tried to reform and morally persuade society where Ambedkar demanded a much deeper transformation of the caste system itself. Their disagreements were not minor differences of personality; they represented fundamentally different ways of understanding social justice. And then there are Gandhi’s personal experiments and some of the deeply uncomfortable aspects of his life that I do not feel any need to defend simply because he became a national icon. Historical figures should be examined, not protected from criticism.

A Great Man Does Not Have to Be a Perfect Man

Perhaps this is what we have forgotten. Gandhi does not have to be perfect for his contribution to matter. He can be criticised for his views on caste, questioned over his political decisions, challenged over his contradictions and examined for the uncomfortable parts of his personal life. None of that requires us to pretend that his contribution to India’s freedom struggle did not exist. Gandhi did not single-handedly free India. He did not invent nationalism, resistance or civil disobedience. He was one among many people who shaped the struggle, and there were moments when other leaders had ideas that were far more convincing than his. But he became one of the most consequential figures in the process because he understood how to transform political resistance into mass participation. I see Gandhi as a larger good within a deeply complicated human being, which is perhaps how we should see most historical figures. We do not need to turn people into saints before we are allowed to respect them. Gandhi was not a saint. He was not always right. He carried prejudices and contradictions that deserve serious criticism. But he was also a man who appeared at a moment when India needed someone capable of taking the idea of independence beyond political circles and placing it in the hands of ordinary people. That cannot simply be erased because we have discovered that Gandhi was flawed. He can be criticised. He should be questioned. But he cannot be ignored. And for everything he did, Gandhi remains one of the most consequential human beings in India’s modern history.
