If Time Can Slow Down, What Exactly Is Time?

The more I learn about time, the less certain I become about what it actually is.

I am an arts student. I was never particularly curious about astrophysics until I watched Interstellar some twelve years ago. I remember being fascinated by the film without fully understanding what was happening. I usually try to learn a little about the science behind a film before watching it, but Einstein’s theories of relativity were not something I could understand overnight. Still, the film sent me down a rabbit hole that eventually led to a question I had never seriously considered: what exactly is time? Until then, time had been seconds, minutes, hours, days and months. We wake up, the clock moves, the day passes, and tomorrow arrives. There seems to be very little to question. But physics has shown that time does not behave quite the way we experience it. It can pass at different rates for different observers, and there is no universal present moment that everyone in the universe must agree on. We have become remarkably good at measuring time. Whether we understand what it actually is remains a different question.

Time Is Relative, Not Universal

Einstein’s special theory of relativity, published in 1905, changed the way physicists understood time. One of its consequences is time dilation: observers moving relative to one another can measure different amounts of elapsed time. The difference becomes especially significant at speeds approaching the speed of light. If someone travelled through space at an extremely high speed and eventually returned to Earth, less time could have passed for that traveller than for the people who stayed behind. The traveller would not simply feel as though time had moved more slowly. Their clock would genuinely record less elapsed time. This has been confirmed through experiments involving precise clocks and fast-moving particles. General relativity adds another complication: gravity affects the passage of time, too. A clock closer to a massive object ticks more slowly than one farther away, when their rates are compared under the appropriate conditions. GPS satellites have to account for both motion and gravity because their clocks do not tick at exactly the same rate as clocks on Earth’s surface. Without these corrections, satellite navigation would become increasingly inaccurate. I had always imagined time as something like a river flowing at the same speed everywhere. Relativity makes that picture difficult to maintain. The time measured by a clock depends on its journey through spacetime, a concept that combines the three dimensions of space with time. Physicists call the time measured along a particular path proper time. Two clocks can begin together, follow different paths and reunite having recorded different amounts of time. This does not mean that time is imaginary or that we can experience it however we please. Relativity gives us precise mathematical rules for predicting what different clocks will measure. What it does away with is the idea of one universal clock against which every other clock must be judged. Which brings me to something I find harder to picture than time dilation itself. If there is no universal clock, is there at least a universal present?

Is There Such a Thing as Now?

In everyday life, we rarely question whether two distant events are happening at the same time. If I am sitting in India while someone else is in France, I assume we are both experiencing the same present. Our clocks can be synchronised, and for ordinary purposes, that assumption works perfectly well. Special relativity complicates it. Observers moving relative to one another can disagree about whether two distant events happened simultaneously. This is not simply because light takes time to travel between them. Even after accounting for the speed of light and synchronising clocks according to the rules of relativity, their measurements of distant simultaneity can differ. The Andromeda paradox, popularised by physicist Roger Penrose, offers an unsettling illustration. Two people walking past each other on Earth at slightly different velocities can assign different distant events in the Andromeda galaxy to what they consider the present. The difference in their own motion is tiny, but because Andromeda is millions of light-years away, the corresponding difference in the distant events they regard as simultaneous can be enormous. Neither person can see the future, and nothing in Andromeda changes because someone has walked past someone else. The point is that relativity does not give us a single, observer-independent division of the entire universe into past, present and future. Different observers can divide spacetime into different sets of simultaneous events. This raises a question that sounds almost absurd until you start taking the physics seriously: if two observers disagree about what is happening now in a distant galaxy, what does now mean on a cosmic scale? One interpretation, known as the block universe, treats past, present and future events as parts of a four-dimensional spacetime structure rather than imagining the universe as something that moves through time from one moment to the next. But relativity does not prove that every future event already exists in exactly the same sense as the present. The block universe is an interpretation of the physics, not an experimentally established fact. I still struggle with the idea that the present might not be a universal feature of reality. Perhaps it belongs more to our experience of the world than to the universe as a whole. We live moment by moment, so naturally we imagine that everything else must do the same. Physics gives us reason to question that assumption.

Why Does Time Move Forward?

There is another problem that relativity does not answer: why do we experience time in one direction? We remember yesterday but have no memories of tomorrow. Ice melts in a glass of water, but we do not see the melted water spontaneously freeze while drawing heat from its surroundings. A glass falls and shatters. We never watch the pieces gather themselves back into a glass and jump onto the table. Many familiar microscopic laws of physics do not strongly distinguish between processes running forwards and backwards in time. Yet ordinary life has a clear direction. One of the main ways physicists understand this is through entropy. Entropy is related to the number of microscopic arrangements that can produce a particular macroscopic state. The second law of thermodynamics tells us that the total entropy of an isolated system does not decrease in ordinary macroscopic processes. States with higher entropy generally correspond to many more possible microscopic arrangements. This helps explain why heat spreads from hot objects to colder surroundings and why broken eggs do not spontaneously reassemble. The direction associated with increasing entropy is called the thermodynamic arrow of time. But there is still a question behind it: why did the universe begin in a state that allowed entropy to increase for billions of years? The early universe was extremely hot and dense, yet remarkably smooth on large scales. Understanding why its initial conditions were so special, particularly when gravity is taken into account, remains an important problem in cosmology. Our experience of time is connected to this physical direction. Memories are records formed through physical processes, and we accumulate records of earlier events rather than future ones. But entropy alone does not explain every aspect of our subjective experience of time. The physical direction of processes and the feeling that time is flowing are related, but they are not the same question. So we have an explanation for much of the direction we observe in everyday life, but the deeper question of why the universe began with the conditions that made this direction possible is still open.

Can We Travel Through Time?

Travelling into the future is not science fiction in the way it is often portrayed. Relativity allows it. If I travelled at a speed close to that of light and returned to Earth, less time could have passed for me than for the people who stayed behind. Gravitational time dilation can produce differences in elapsed time, too. These are consequences of established physics, not merely possibilities invented for films. Travelling into the past is another matter. Some mathematical solutions to general relativity contain structures called closed timelike curves, which would allow a path through spacetime to return to an event in its own past. But a mathematical solution does not necessarily describe something nature can actually produce. Some proposals require exotic physical conditions, and we have no demonstrated method for building a machine that takes a person backwards in time. What interests me about the idea is what happens next. Suppose I could travel to the past and interfere with an event. Would the future change? If I prevented my parents from meeting, would I erase the circumstances that led to my own journey? And if I somehow managed to change the future, what would happen to the version of history I had travelled from? One possibility often explored in science fiction is a single timeline that changes when someone interferes with the past. Another is that the timeline somehow remains self-consistent, meaning that whatever the traveller does was already part of history. A third possibility is that changing the past creates, or leads to, an alternate reality with a different future. In that version, the traveller might alter events in another timeline without erasing the one they came from. These ideas offer ways of thinking about time-travel paradoxes, but none has been established as the way our universe works. We do not know whether alternate timelines of this kind exist, or whether travelling into the past is physically possible in the first place. Quantum mechanics often enters this discussion through the many-worlds interpretation, associated with physicist Hugh Everett. It describes quantum evolution in terms of a universal quantum state with components corresponding to different measurement outcomes. It is a serious interpretation of quantum theory, but it does not establish that every decision we make creates another universe containing a different version of us. Nor does it provide a known way to travel between such worlds. Still, I find myself thinking about the choices that have shaped my own life. What if I had studied something else, taken a different road or made a decision I still occasionally regret? Would changing one of those moments change everything that followed? In ordinary life, these are alternative possibilities, not evidence of parallel universes. A different choice would lead to different consequences, but that does not mean every possible outcome exists somewhere as a separate physical reality. Perhaps the appeal of time travel is partly that it promises something we rarely get in life: the chance to find out what would have happened if we had done things differently. We can revisit old decisions in our minds, but we cannot simply return to the moment and try again. Whether the universe permits such a thing is a scientific question. Wanting to do it is a very human one.

What If Time Is Not Fundamental?

This is where I get stuck in a different way. Physics gives us extraordinarily accurate ways of measuring time and predicting how clocks behave. Yet when physicists try to combine general relativity with quantum mechanics, time becomes one of the difficult problems. General relativity treats spacetime as a dynamic structure that is affected by matter and energy. In standard quantum mechanics, time is usually treated as an external parameter describing how a quantum state evolves. The two theories handle time differently, and reconciling them is part of the challenge of developing a theory of quantum gravity. Some approaches suggest that time, as we understand it, might emerge from something more fundamental. Others retain time in some form. There is no settled answer, and science has not established that time is an illusion. But the difficulty of bringing our best theories together suggests that our understanding of time may still be incomplete. The questions that follow are difficult to separate from philosophy. What does it mean for the past to exist if we can access it only through memories, records and other physical traces? Is the future real in some sense, or is it a collection of possibilities that have not yet become actual events? Physics can help us examine these questions, but it does not automatically settle every philosophical interpretation we attach to its equations. Then there is the simulation hypothesis: the possibility that our experienced universe is generated by some underlying computational system. It is an interesting thought experiment, but there is currently no compelling empirical evidence that we live inside a simulation. The fact that I can create a fictional universe in a novel does not establish that our own universe was created in the same way. My characters may have histories and relationships within the story, but that does not make them conscious beings living in another physical reality. I suppose this is where it becomes easy to get carried away. Once we realise that the universe behaves in ways our intuition cannot easily explain, almost any possibility can begin to sound plausible. But imagining something and finding evidence for it are two different things. I can wonder whether time emerges from something deeper without claiming that science has already answered the question.

What Do We Actually Know About Time?

I started thinking about time because of Interstellar. I wanted to understand how an hour on one planet could correspond to years passing elsewhere. That simple question led me to relativity, the problem of simultaneity, entropy and the possibility that time might not be fundamental in the way we assume. I now understand why clocks can disagree, why the universe does not necessarily have a single present moment shared by every observer, and why the direction of time is connected to the second law of thermodynamics. I also understand that travelling into the future is permitted by known physics, while travelling into the past remains speculative. Those are real answers, even if they do not resolve the larger mystery. I am still an arts student trying to make sense of physics, and I know that understanding the broad ideas is not the same as mastering the mathematics behind them. But I do not think a question becomes uninteresting simply because I cannot yet follow every equation involved. Time is something we use every day without giving it much thought. We measure it with extraordinary precision, rely on it for satellite navigation, and use physical theories involving it to predict the behaviour of the universe. Yet we still debate what it means for time to flow, why it has a direction and whether it is a fundamental part of reality. I began with a film about time moving differently on another planet. I ended up questioning what I had always assumed the present to be. I still do not know what time ultimately is. What has changed is that I no longer assume the answer must be as straightforward as the ticking of a clock.
