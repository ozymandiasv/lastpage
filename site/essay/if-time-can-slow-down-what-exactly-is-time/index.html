If Time Can Slow Down, What Exactly Is Time?

The more I learn about time, the less certain I become about what it actually is.

I am an arts student. I was never curious about astrophysics until I saw Interstellar some 12 years ago. I remember watching the movie in awe and not really being able to comprehend what was happening around the concept. I mean, before watching any movie I would learn a bit about the details I should know, but the Theory of Relativity was a bit too much to learn overnight. But then that movie taught me to look deeper, deeper into the General and Special Theories of Relativity, and that opened up a whole new concept called time to me. And that changed a lot. I had heard somewhere that one minute for God is a thousand years on Earth. Oh well, that could relatively make sense, and it also made me wonder what exactly is time. Before that, time was just hours, seconds, days and months to me. You wake up, the clock moves, the day passes, you sleep, and tomorrow comes. It is probably the most familiar thing in our lives, and yet we rarely question it. We question where the universe came from, what happens inside a black hole, whether aliens exist and whether God exists, but time somehow gets a free pass. We simply assume that it is there, moving forward, carrying everything along with it. Then I found out that Einstein had already made that assumption considerably less comfortable.

There Is No Universal Now

The strange thing about relativity is not simply that time can slow down. It is that time is not necessarily the same for everyone. Depending on how fast you are moving and the gravitational field you are in, you can experience a different amount of time from someone else. This is not science fiction. It has been measured. So two people can start with clocks that agree and later discover that their clocks do not show exactly the same amount of elapsed time. The clocks are not broken. They have simply experienced different paths through spacetime. That alone is strange, but what I find even more difficult to accept is the idea that there may be no universal “now”. The Andromeda paradox is one way of thinking about this. Two observers moving differently can disagree about which distant events are happening at the same time. We normally assume that somewhere in the universe, there is a present moment that everyone shares. I am here, someone in another galaxy is there, but surely we are both living in the same now. Apparently, the universe does not owe us that certainty. And if there is no universal now, then what exactly is the present? Is the present something that exists in the universe, or is it simply the place from which I happen to experience it? This is where physics starts becoming philosophy for me. If two people can experience different amounts of time and disagree about what is happening simultaneously, perhaps time is not this universal thing we imagined it to be. Maybe there is no giant clock somewhere keeping everything in order. Maybe what we call time is deeply connected to our position, our movement and our experience of the universe. We have always spoken about the past, present and future as though they were three clearly separated places. The past is behind us, the present is where we are, and the future is somewhere ahead. But spacetime makes that picture much less obvious. Maybe the universe does not experience time the way we do. Maybe the universe simply exists, while we experience events one after another because that is the only way a conscious being can experience them. And if that is true, then perhaps the feeling that time is moving is not necessarily something the universe is doing. Perhaps it is something happening inside us.

What Happens to the Future?

Then there is the future, which is where things get even more interesting. Relativity gives us a scientifically established way of travelling into the future through time dilation. If you travel fast enough, time can pass differently for you compared with someone who remains behind. Going backwards is a completely different matter. Physics has not shown us that we can simply build a machine and travel into yesterday, although certain solutions in relativity have led physicists to explore the possibility of closed time-like curves and other strange ideas. And the moment we allow the possibility of going backwards, we immediately create problems that sound like they belong in a science-fiction movie. What happens if I go back and change something that prevents me from ever going back in the first place? Which version of events is then the real one? Maybe there would be another timeline. Maybe the universe somehow prevents the contradiction. Maybe travelling backwards is simply impossible. We do not know. Quantum physics makes the imagination run even further, but this is also where I think we should be careful. There are interpretations of quantum mechanics that allow us to think about branching realities, but science has not established that every decision we make creates another universe. Still, I cannot help thinking about it. What if every possibility that could have happened somehow exists somewhere? What if there is another version of me who made a completely different decision five minutes ago? What if the life I did not choose is being lived somewhere else? I know this is moving from physics into speculation, but perhaps that is exactly what makes the question interesting. Even this blog post can become a ridiculous little thought experiment. What if I do not post it? Obviously, the universe will not collapse and another dimension will not suddenly open in my bedroom. But a different chain of events will follow. Someone will not read it. Perhaps a conversation that would have happened because of it will never happen. Maybe I will spend the next hour doing something else, which leads to another decision, which leads to another one. This is not quantum mechanics; it is simply cause and effect. But it makes me realise how little of our lives we actually control. We imagine our lives as one continuous story, but every moment contains choices and possibilities that disappear as soon as we choose one. We remember the life we lived, but what about all the lives that could have been lived? The person I might have become if I had taken a different road, studied something else, met someone else, stayed somewhere else or simply said yes instead of no? Those possibilities are not part of my memory because they never happened. Yet they existed, at least for a moment, as possibilities. Maybe this is why we are so fascinated by time travel. Perhaps we are not really fascinated by time itself. Perhaps we are fascinated by regret.

What If Time Isn't Reality?

And then I end up at a much bigger question. What if time is not reality in the way we think it is? Maybe time is fundamental and we simply do not understand it yet. Maybe past, present and future are all part of some larger structure and our consciousness gives us the feeling of moving through them. Maybe time emerges from something deeper that we have not discovered. Or maybe the universe is not anything like the picture we have created in our heads. Once you start questioning time, it becomes surprisingly easy to start questioning everything else. If there is no universal present, what exactly does it mean for something to exist now? If the past has happened but is no longer physically accessible to us, in what sense does it still exist? If the future has not happened, is it real or merely possible? And if our experience of reality depends so heavily on how our minds arrange events into a sequence, how much of what we call reality is actually the universe and how much of it is our interpretation of the universe? This is probably where I leave science and enter pure speculation, but I have always found that the most interesting questions begin there. What if we are living inside a simulation? What if our universe is some unimaginably complicated program running somewhere and we simply have no way of knowing what exists outside it? What if shutting down that hypothetical computer would be the end of everything we know? I know there is no evidence that this is true, but the thought itself tells us something interesting about how uncertain our understanding of existence really is. I sometimes even wonder about writing. If I create a fictional world, give it people, histories, relationships and events, then inside that story there is a reality that did not exist before I created it. Obviously, my fictional characters are not conscious beings living in another universe. But the idea makes me wonder about what we actually mean when we call something real. Perhaps reality is not as simple as something being there or not being there. Perhaps there are layers to existence that we are simply not capable of experiencing. I started thinking about time because of Interstellar. I wanted to understand why an hour on one planet could mean years somewhere else, and somewhere along the way I ended up questioning the entire idea of time. Maybe that is what fascinates me about it. Time is something every human being experiences, yet none of us can step outside it and look at it objectively. We cannot pause it, go back and examine it, or stand outside the universe and see where the past ends and the future begins. We simply exist inside it. We remember what has happened, experience what we call the present, and imagine what might happen next. Perhaps time is a physical property of the universe. Perhaps it is something deeper. Perhaps it is partly a construction of consciousness. I do not know. But if Einstein was right that time is not universal, if there is no single “now” shared by the entire universe, and if physics itself still struggles to reconcile time with our deepest theories of reality, then maybe the question was never really “How does time work?” Maybe the more uncomfortable question is: what exactly is this thing we have been calling time all our lives?
