Poverty Is Not Just Multiplication

When we reduce poverty to the number of children poor people have, we stop asking why poverty exists in the first place.

Whenever poverty comes up in India, one of the first arguments I see is that poor people have too many children. Two poor people marry, four poor children are born, and poverty continues. It is a convenient explanation because it gives us someone to blame. We don't have to discuss unemployment, unequal access to education, landlessness, low wages, poor healthcare or the concentration of wealth. We can simply point at a poor family and say they should have had fewer children. But I think we need to look at poverty differently. A child born into poverty is not the cause of poverty. They are someone who has inherited the circumstances of it. Their parents did not create every condition that keeps them poor, and their children will not automatically escape those conditions simply because they are born into a smaller family. Population matters, of course, but poverty is not just multiplication. It is also about the opportunities available to people, the value of their labour, the quality of public services and whether they have any realistic chance of improving their lives. If we want to understand why poverty persists, we have to look beyond the number of people being born and ask why so many people remain poor after they are born.

The Child Is Not the Cause of Poverty

A child born into a wealthy family inherits a completely different set of opportunities from a child born into a landless labourer's household. Both may be equally intelligent, equally curious and equally capable, but their starting points are radically different. One may have access to private education, nutrition, computers, coaching and a network of people who can help them find opportunities. The other may have to walk several kilometres to school, work alongside their parents, or abandon education altogether because the family needs another pair of hands. And then, years later, when the second child remains poor, we wonder whether their parents should have had fewer children. How convenient it is to blame the number of people born into poverty rather than examine the conditions that prevent them from escaping it. Children do not decide whether their parents have secure employment, whether their family owns land, whether their village has a functioning school, whether their mother can access healthcare or whether their household has enough money to survive a bad month. They are born into a system that has already determined much of what will be available to them. We should be asking why that system offers so little to so many people.

Why Do Poor Families Have More Children?

This is a question worth asking, but it requires more than contempt for poor people. Fertility is influenced by education, access to contraception, healthcare, economic security, women's autonomy and the circumstances in which families live. In communities where infant and child mortality has historically been high, families may have more children because they fear losing some of them. Where children are expected to contribute to household labour or support their parents in old age, having more children may be seen as a form of security. Where women have limited access to education, employment or reproductive healthcare, their ability to decide when and how many children to have may be restricted. None of this means that every poor family has five children for the same reason, or that having more children is always economically wise. It means that reproductive choices are shaped by the conditions people live in. If we genuinely want smaller families, we should be investing in education, healthcare, contraception, women's independence and economic security. We should create conditions in which people can confidently decide to have fewer children because they know those children will survive, receive an education and have a future. There is a reason fertility tends to decline as societies become healthier, more educated and economically secure. It is not because poor people suddenly become morally superior when they become rich. It is because their circumstances change.

The Poverty That Reproduces Itself

The real problem is that poverty is inherited through circumstances, not merely through birth. A child born into poverty may suffer from inadequate nutrition, poor healthcare and limited access to quality education. These conditions affect their physical development, learning ability and future opportunities. If they leave school early, they may enter low-paid informal employment, where there is little job security, no meaningful social protection and almost no opportunity to accumulate wealth. They may spend their entire adult life working without ever being able to save enough to escape the conditions in which they were raised. Their children then inherit the same disadvantages. This is how poverty reproduces itself. It is not simply that poor people have children who are also poor. It is that the structures around them make it extraordinarily difficult for those children to become anything else. Caste discrimination, unequal access to land, poor public infrastructure, insecure employment and the concentration of economic opportunities in certain regions all contribute to this cycle. We cannot expect people to escape poverty through individual effort alone when the opportunities available to them are so unequal. Hard work matters, but hard work is not a substitute for opportunity. A person can work twelve hours a day and remain poor if their labour is undervalued, their employment is insecure and they have no assets to fall back on.

Who Is Actually Taking Away the Middle Class's Money?

Yesterday, I had a conversation on a post that made this issue particularly clear. The post argued that poverty in India is not reducing because two poor people get married and four poor children are born. One person in the thread said they earned around ₹22–24 lakh a year and questioned why they should pay for the children of poor people. They were willing to support education and healthcare, but not what they considered unnecessary freebies. The frustration is understandable. When someone works hard, pays taxes and struggles to maintain their standard of living, it is natural to question where that money goes. But the conversation quickly turned into something much more disturbing: the suggestion that poor people should undergo sterilisation after having two children, followed by the declaration that their children were not the taxpayer's problem. I replied that the question should not be why poor people have five children, but why they are poor, why they remain uneducated and why they are trapped in the same cycle generation after generation. The taxpayer has every right to demand accountability, efficient public services and a government that uses their money responsibly. But I find it interesting how quickly the anger of the middle class is directed towards poor families receiving assistance, while the enormous financial benefits extended to wealthy individuals and corporations often receive considerably less attention. We hear arguments about free food, free education, healthcare subsidies and welfare schemes, but what about tax concessions, incentives, subsidies and other forms of support provided to large businesses? What about the concentration of wealth and the increasing pressure on workers to do the work of two or three people because companies are unwilling to hire enough employees? I am not suggesting that every subsidy to a corporation is wrong or that every welfare scheme is justified. The point is that we should scrutinise both. If a government spends public money, we should ask who benefits, what public value is created and whether the money is being used effectively. It is rather strange that a poor family receiving assistance for food or education is often treated as a burden on the taxpayer, while the financial privileges of the wealthy are discussed as necessary for economic growth.

The Poor Are Not Separate From Us

The person who replied to me argued that unless the poor are uplifted, the middle class itself may eventually suffer. I agree with that, but I would go further. The poor are not some separate population whose existence has no connection to our own lives. They are our workers, consumers, neighbours and fellow citizens. They build our homes, clean our streets, deliver our food, work in factories, maintain our infrastructure and provide services that make our daily lives possible. An economy cannot remain healthy if a large section of its population is denied the opportunity to become educated, skilled and economically secure. When people lack purchasing power, businesses lose potential customers. When workers lack skills, productivity suffers. When young people cannot find meaningful employment, the demographic dividend we keep talking about becomes a demographic burden. And when people are pushed into desperation, social instability affects everyone, including the middle class that believes it can isolate itself from poverty. We are already living in a country where unemployment, insecure work and rising living costs are affecting families across economic categories. The middle class is not wealthy enough to ignore the conditions of the working class indefinitely. A society in which millions struggle to survive cannot offer lasting security to the people who happen to be doing slightly better.

I am not saying that every poor person makes the right decision, that every family should have as many children as they want without considering their circumstances, or that every welfare scheme is automatically good. Personal responsibility matters, and governments must ensure that public assistance reaches people who need it and produces meaningful results. But we cannot build a serious discussion about poverty around the idea that poor people are reproducing too much and therefore deserve less sympathy. If a child is born into poverty, that child has not committed an economic offence. They are not responsible for the conditions of their birth, and they should not have to earn the right to food, education or healthcare by proving that their parents made the correct reproductive choices. We should be asking why a country with so much wealth, talent and economic potential continues to leave so many people behind. Why are public schools failing? Why is healthcare unaffordable for so many? Why is employment so insecure? Why do people work for decades without accumulating assets? Why do we keep treating poverty as an individual failure when it is so clearly shaped by collective circumstances? We can argue about population, fertility and welfare, but unless we address the conditions that reproduce poverty, we will simply keep blaming the next generation for inheriting the same problems. The five children are not the whole problem. The problem is the world waiting for them when they are born.
