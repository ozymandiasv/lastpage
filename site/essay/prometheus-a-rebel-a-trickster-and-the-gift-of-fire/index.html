Prometheus: A Rebel, a Trickster and the Gift of Fire

How a character I discovered through Frankenstein became a symbol of everything I admire about curiosity, knowledge and defiance.

I recently came across Prometheus, and I must admit that Greek mythology has never been something I explored extensively. My familiarity was mostly limited to Homer's Iliad and Odyssey. It was when I read Mary Shelley's Frankenstein that I became fascinated by this particular figure. The subtitle of the novel, The Modern Prometheus, made me curious about the original myth, and the more I read, the more I found myself drawn to him. Prometheus was a Titan who defied Zeus, stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity. For this, he was condemned to eternal punishment, chained to a rock where an eagle would eat his liver every day, only for it to regenerate and the torment to begin again. There is something extraordinary about this story. Here was a figure willing to challenge the highest authority in existence, knowing very well what the consequences would be, simply because he believed humanity deserved something that had been kept away from it. And perhaps that is where my admiration began.

The Trickster I Choose to Believe In

There is another side to Prometheus that I find equally interesting. He wasn't simply a benevolent rebel; he was also a trickster. In Greek mythology, he deceived Zeus during the division of sacrificial portions at Mecone, using his intelligence and cunning to manipulate the arrangement in humanity's favour. He wasn't particularly concerned with respecting divine authority or following the rules established by the gods. He challenged, deceived and outsmarted them. Tricksters are fascinating characters because they exist somewhere between wisdom and mischief, morality and deception. They can be selfish, manipulative and destructive, but they can also expose the absurdity of established power. There are different ways to interpret Prometheus, and I suppose I have chosen the one I like most. I choose to believe in the Prometheus who challenged authority for the benefit of humanity, the one who used his intelligence not to preserve his own position but to give others something valuable. Perhaps that isn't the only interpretation, but it is the one I find worth admiring. Sometimes the person who breaks the rules isn't necessarily the villain. Sometimes the rules themselves deserve to be questioned.

What Does Fire Really Represent?

I don't think the story is merely about stealing fire. Fire represents something much larger: knowledge, technology, civilisation and the ability of human beings to transform their own existence. It is what allowed humanity to move beyond its natural limitations. And when I think about it, almost every major advancement in human civilisation has followed a similar pattern. We questioned what we knew, experimented with what we didn't understand and challenged limitations that once seemed permanent. From fire and agriculture to mathematics, medicine, electricity, computers and artificial intelligence, our progress has always depended on curiosity and the willingness to explore what lies beyond our existing knowledge. There is also something deeply political about the Prometheus myth. Who gets to decide what humanity is allowed to know? Why should knowledge be the exclusive possession of those who hold power? And what happens when someone decides that the boundaries imposed by authority are not worth respecting? Prometheus, in my interpretation, represents the belief that knowledge should not be withheld simply because someone powerful wishes to control it. I find that idea particularly compelling.

Why I Chose Prometheus

This is also why I decided to change my logo. I wanted something that represented more than an attractive design or an aesthetic preference. I wanted a symbol connected to an idea I genuinely admire. Prometheus represents curiosity, rebellion, knowledge and the willingness to challenge established authority. The broken Greek statue in the new logo is a visual expression of that idea. It isn't meant to represent a perfect hero or an untouchable figure. There is something beautiful about an imperfect, broken representation of someone whose ideas continue to survive. Prometheus suffered, but the fire he gave humanity was never taken back. And perhaps that is what I find most meaningful about him. You can punish someone for questioning authority, you can silence them, you can even destroy their physical form, but the knowledge and ideas they introduce can continue to exist long after them. I haven't suddenly become an expert in Greek mythology, and I certainly don't claim to understand every interpretation of Prometheus. I simply came across a character through a novel, became curious enough to explore his story and found an idea that resonated with me. I would rather identify with the trickster who gave humanity fire than the authority that wanted to keep it.
