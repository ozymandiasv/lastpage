Remember the Name, Lionel Messi

He looks like the boy next door and performs like a god

I am not sure how international football will be without him. It still echoes in my head, the way Rob Palmer introduced him after his first goal, “Remember the name, Lionel Messi, at the age of 17…” I did not watch that goal live. I was too young, and football had not yet become the thing that would keep me awake at impossible hours, make me care about cities I had never visited, clubs I had no connection with, and people who lived thousands of kilometres away from me. But I have watched that clip so many times over the years that it feels like a memory of my own. Remember the name. We did. For more than twenty years, we remembered it. At some point, Lionel Messi stopped being just the name of a footballer and became something that simply existed in the background of football itself. Like the World Cup. Like Argentina. Like Barcelona. Like that feeling you get when the ball reaches a player and you instinctively sit up because you know something might happen. I started properly following football around 2010, and Messi was already there. That is perhaps why this feels so strange to me. I do not remember a version of football that I loved without him being somewhere in it. The South African World Cup is one of those memories that has stayed with me. The vuvuzelas, the atmosphere, the strange feeling of suddenly discovering that football was so much bigger than the matches I occasionally watched. Messi was there, playing for Argentina, and although the story was not yet complete, there was already something about him that made you watch differently. It was not always about goals. That is something I think people who only look at statistics may never completely understand. Sometimes he received the ball in the middle of the pitch and nothing happened. Sometimes he lost it. Sometimes he passed it. But you watched. You always watched. Because there was always the possibility that in the next few seconds, he might do something you had genuinely never seen anyone else do.

Growing Up With Messi

Somewhere along the way, Messi became part of the timeline of my own life. Barcelona seasons came and went, Champions League nights came and went, El Clasicos came and went, and somehow there was always another Messi moment waiting somewhere. I remember spending hours watching YouTube compilations, usually with terrible video quality and dramatic music that made everything look even more important than it already was. The titles were ridiculous, something like “Lionel Messi Impossible Skills 2011 HD,” and yet I watched every second. There was the goal against Getafe that made everyone talk about Maradona. There was the Champions League final against Manchester United. There were those runs through defenders where you genuinely started feeling sorry for them. The ball seemed to belong to him in a way that made no sense. Other players dribbled brilliantly, of course, but with Messi there were moments when it felt as if the ball understood where he wanted to go before he did. I remember the endless arguments too. Messi versus Ronaldo became its own separate sport for an entire generation. You could not escape it. School, college, Facebook, Twitter, Reddit, YouTube comments, random conversations with people who barely watched football, everyone had an opinion. Sometimes I wonder how much of our teenage and young adult lives were spent defending footballers who had absolutely no idea we existed. But we did it anyway. We compared goals, trophies, assists, Champions Leagues and international performances as if we had personally been appointed to decide the greatest footballer in history. Looking back, some of those arguments were stupid, some were exhausting, and some were genuinely fun. But Messi was always there at the centre of it. Even people who hated him had to talk about him.

Argentina and the Heartbreak

Following Messi for Argentina was different. At Barcelona, especially during those great years, there was always a sense that something magical was possible. With Argentina, there was pressure. There was history. There was Maradona. I sometimes think no footballer should have to live under the shadow of another person the way Messi had to. Every game became a comparison. Every missed chance became evidence. Every defeat became another question. Can he do it for Argentina? Is he really like Maradona? Does he care enough? Is he a leader? It was exhausting even as a fan. I cannot imagine what it must have felt like for him. The 2014 World Cup is still one of those tournaments that I cannot think about without feeling something. Argentina reached the final, and suddenly the possibility was real. I remember watching that tournament with the growing feeling that perhaps this was it. Perhaps this was the moment when the story finally came together. And then Germany won. Football can be incredibly cruel without doing anything dramatic. Sometimes there is no villain. There is no great injustice that can be corrected. A moment simply goes the wrong way, and you carry it with you. Messi stood there after the final, eventually receiving the Golden Ball, and I remember how strange it felt. One of the greatest individual honours in football, and yet it looked like the most meaningless thing in the world at that moment. Then came more heartbreak. Copa America after Copa America, another final, another defeat, another moment when it felt like the story was moving further away from the ending we all hoped for. And then Messi retired from international football. I remember that feeling too. It felt impossible that this was how it would end. Not because football owed him anything, and perhaps that is important to say, but because after watching him give so much to Argentina and then repeatedly come so close, it was difficult to imagine that the final image would be him walking away after another defeat. But he came back. Of course he came back. When Everything Changed Something changed with Argentina after that. Maybe it happened slowly and we only recognised it later. Maybe Messi himself changed. Maybe the team changed around him. Perhaps everyone finally stopped asking him to become Maradona and allowed him to be Messi. The Copa America in 2021 felt different. I remember the nervousness before the final, the kind of nervousness where you cannot properly enjoy the match because your mind is already imagining every possible disaster. And then Argentina won. The feeling was not just happiness. It was relief. He had finally done it. After all those years, all those finals, all those questions, Messi had finally won a major international trophy with Argentina. I remember watching him celebrate and thinking about how long the journey had been. The boy Rob Palmer had asked us to remember had become a man who had spent almost two decades carrying expectations that would have broken most people. And then came Qatar. #Qatar Was Something Else I genuinely do not know if I will ever experience another World Cup like Qatar 2022, and boy I can argue. I know that sounds dramatic, but I do not care. It was dramatic. That entire month felt like football had decided to write the most ridiculous story imaginable and then refused to make it easy for anyone involved. Argentina lost the very first match against Saudi Arabia, and suddenly the dream seemed to be disappearing before it had properly begun. I remember that feeling. That horrible feeling of thinking, surely not like this. Not after everything. Not in his last World Cup. Then came Mexico. Poland. Australia. Argentina kept moving forward, and Messi kept doing what Messi did. The Netherlands match was one of the most insane football matches I have ever watched. Then Croatia. And suddenly Argentina were in another World Cup final. I remember the final so clearly that I do not think I will ever forget where I was, what I felt or how many times my heart nearly stopped. Argentina were in control. Then they were not. Messi scored. Mbappe scored. Messi scored again. Mbappe came back again because apparently football had decided that none of us deserved peace. Extra time felt like an entire lifetime. Then penalties. I have never liked penalties. I understand that someone has to win, but emotionally, penalties are just torture disguised as sport. And then it happened. Argentina won the World Cup. Messi lifted it.

The Point of Difference

He was beautiful. He was the point of difference. He has always been the point of difference. Unparalleled, and maybe today there will, of course, always be those who argue, always be those who debate. And the debate could rage on if you like. But as he falls in love with the object in the world that his heart most desired, it is hard to escape the supposition that he has rendered himself today, the greatest of all time. - Peter Drury

There is a moment in the commentary from that night that I keep thinking about. Peter Drury spoke about Messi as someone beautiful to watch, the player who made the difference when everything was on the line, someone without comparison, someone whose place in football stood apart from everyone around him. And perhaps that is what Messi was to so many of us. He was always the possibility of difference. When a match was going nowhere, you looked at Messi. When Argentina needed something, you looked at Messi. When football felt ordinary, there was always the chance that he would make it extraordinary again. That is what made watching him different for me. There have been great footballers, and there will be great footballers after him. I am not going to sit here and pretend football ends with Lionel Messi. It does not. New players will come, and some child somewhere is probably watching a seventeen-year-old footballer right now without knowing that he will one day become the hero of an entire generation. That is the beauty of football. It keeps moving.

But there will never be another first Messi.

There will never again be that experience of watching this particular story unfold in real time. We watched the boy become a footballer, the footballer become a superstar, the superstar become a symbol of an entire club, the symbol carry a country through years of heartbreak, and finally, after everything, become a World Cup winner. We watched all of it happen. Statistically, there will be players who surpass Messi's records, but only another god could break them all by himself. Deep down, we know there was never a Messi; and possibly never will be.

Yesterday, He Left

I knew this day would come. We all did. You cannot watch someone grow older and pretend that time has stopped for them. At some point, the conversations change. You start talking about their last World Cup. Their last Copa America. Their final appearance. You begin counting things that you never wanted to count. And still, when the moment actually arrives, there is a part of you that says, not yet. Because what is international football without Messi? I genuinely do not know. Argentina will be fine. They will continue. They have wonderful players. They will win and lose matches, perhaps win more trophies, perhaps discover another great player who will lead them into another generation. Football itself will continue exactly as it always has. The World Cup will come again. Copa America will come again. Someone else will wear the Argentina shirt, and eventually someone else will become the hero of millions of children. But since I started following football, Messi has always been there. There has always been the possibility of another Argentina match with Messi in it. Another tournament where perhaps something historic could happen. Another moment where the ball could reach his feet and twenty years of watching football had taught you to pay attention. Now that possibility is gone. And that is strange. I Had to Write This

I needed to write this for myself.

For the person who started watching football with Messi in the centre; and had no idea that this little man from Rosario would become such a permanent part of his memories. For every late night spent watching a match. For every stupid argument about Messi and Ronaldo. For every World Cup match that made me nervous despite the fact that I was sitting thousands of kilometres away from Argentina and had absolutely no logical reason to be emotionally involved. Except that football does not always need logic. I am from India. I have never been to Argentina. I do not speak Spanish. And yet, when Argentina won the World Cup in 2022, I cried.

Millions of people probably did.

That is the thing about Messi that I will find difficult to explain to people who did not live through his career. He belonged to Argentina, of course. He belonged to Rosario. He belonged to the people who followed him before the rest of us knew his name. But somehow, over the years, he also became part of the lives of people everywhere. He gave us memories that attached themselves to our own lives. And now one chapter is over. Remember the Name I think we were lucky. My generation was incredibly lucky. We did not have to hear stories about Messi from someone older. We did not have to search for old footage and wonder what it must have felt like to watch him live. We were there. We watched him. We watched the goals, the defeats, the finals, the tears, the retirement, the return, the Copa America, and finally the World Cup. We watched the whole story. Or at least enough of it to call it ours too. Years from now, someone younger might ask me what it was like to watch Lionel Messi. I do not know if I will talk about the statistics. I probably will not remember all of them anyway. Maybe I will show them videos. Maybe I will tell them to watch the 2022 World Cup final. Maybe I will talk about Barcelona and those nights when it seemed like football had become something completely different when he had the ball. But I think I will simply tell them that watching Messi felt like watching someone who could make football less ordinary. You never knew when it would happen. You just knew that it could. And perhaps that was enough. I am not sure how international football will be without him. Maybe, slowly, we will get used to it. Football always moves forward. New stories will begin. New heroes will arrive. One day, another seventeen-year-old will score a goal, and some commentator will say something that stays with us for the rest of our lives. But this time, I know the name. I knew it for almost my entire life as a football fan. Lionel Messi. And what a privilege it was to watch him.
