Reservation Was Never About Poverty

We keep debating reservation without first understanding what problem it was created to solve.

It is quite evident that not everyone understands what caste is or what caste-based reservation actually exists for. Many still like to believe that India is a country where caste discrimination has completely disappeared because they live in cities. It is a comforting perspective to have, but reality is far more complicated. The easiest way to test whether caste has disappeared is not by looking at social media or corporate offices but by looking at marriage. Even in India's biggest cities, inter-caste marriages are still relatively uncommon. Families still ask about caste before agreeing to marriages, matrimonial advertisements still proudly mention caste, and honour killings over inter-caste marriages continue to happen. If caste had truly disappeared, marriage would have been the first institution where we noticed it. It hasn't. Caste continues to shape people's lives, and because it continues to shape people's lives, it continues to shape opportunities. Whenever I speak about reservation, I notice that many people immediately start talking about poverty. I understand why. For a long time, I also thought both were the same thing. But they are not. Poverty and caste are two completely different problems. A poor upper-caste family deserves economic support just as much as anyone else. Scholarships, welfare schemes and financial assistance should absolutely exist for that. Reservation, however, was never introduced to solve poverty. It was introduced because entire communities were denied education, representation and access to positions of power for centuries. Those are two completely different conversations, yet we somehow keep mixing them into one.

The Day I Started Looking at It Differently

I remember being in school and seeing students who were financially much better off than my family paying almost nothing in fees while my parents struggled to pay the full amount. Naturally, I thought the system was unfair. I used to ask myself how someone richer than me could receive benefits while I couldn't. It genuinely felt wrong. It was only much later, after I came across Subaltern Studies and started reading more about caste, that I realised I had been asking the wrong question. I was looking only at the economic condition of the individual standing in front of me. I wasn't looking at the history that stood behind that individual. The question was never who had more money. The question was who had been systematically excluded from education and public institutions for generations.

Representation Matters

People often reduce caste to occupation, but historically caste determined far more than that. It decided who could study, who could own land, who could enter temples, who could govern, and who had to perform the most degrading occupations in society. Thankfully, India has changed enormously since then, and many of those restrictions no longer exist in law. But social structures do not disappear simply because the law changes. This is where I think representation becomes important. If caste no longer influenced opportunities, then our institutions should naturally begin reflecting the society they represent. Communities classified as Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes together make up a substantial majority of India's population. If everything had become equal decades ago, we would expect that diversity to be visible among judges, professors, bureaucrats, chief ministers, prime ministers and every other institution of power. But that has never really been the case. India has never had a Scheduled Caste Prime Minister. Narendra Modi identifies as OBC, although his community was included in Gujarat's OBC list much later, so people may have different views on how they interpret that politically. Even if we include him, only one of India's Prime Ministers has come from an OBC background and none from the Scheduled Castes. The point isn't to count individuals. The point is to ask why representation still looks so different from the society those institutions are meant to represent.

The Problem With the Merit Argument

Then comes the argument everyone eventually makes. Merit. I have always found that argument interesting because people often speak as though merit develops in isolation. Imagine two children with exactly the same intelligence. One grows up in a family where parents and grandparents are educated, someone at home knows how competitive examinations work, somebody can guide them through college admissions, interviews and career choices. The other becomes the first person in the family to even complete school. Nobody at home has experienced higher education. Nobody knows how competitive exams works or how university admissions happen. We expect both of them to compete from exactly the same starting line and then call the result merit. I don't think life has ever worked that way. That doesn't mean people from historically marginalised communities are less capable. It means many of them begin much further behind. As Dr. B.R. Ambedkar repeatedly argued, political equality means very little without social equality. You cannot expect equal outcomes from unequal starting points and then call the system fair.

Reservation Is About Representation, Not Charity

I think this is where most debates go wrong. People talk about reservation as though it is some kind of favour being done to certain communities. I don't see it that way at all. Reservation is not charity. It is not a reward. It is not meant to punish upper castes either. It exists because a democracy should not have institutions that are dominated by only a few sections of society while others remain historically absent. It is an attempt to make public institutions resemble the society they are meant to serve. Whether it has achieved that goal completely is another debate altogether, but that is the philosophy behind it.

Where I Think the System Needs Reform

None of this means reservation cannot be improved. In fact, I think it should be. The creamy layer debate is one of the most important discussions we can have. If reservation exists for upliftment, then governments must make sure its benefits continue reaching those who genuinely need them. If the same families keep receiving those benefits generation after generation while equally disadvantaged members of the same community remain left behind, then the purpose of reservation begins to weaken. Reforming reservation is not the same as opposing reservation. A system can be improved without abandoning the principle behind it.

Before We Debate, We Should Understand

Reservation was never designed to eliminate poverty. It was never meant to make everyone economically equal. It was introduced because generations of social exclusion created generations of unequal representation. We can absolutely debate how the system should evolve, whether creamy layer should be expanded, whether implementation can be improved and whether reforms are necessary. Those are healthy conversations. But before debating reservation, we should first understand the problem it was created to solve. If we cannot see that difference, perhaps we need to spend a little more time understanding the system before deciding whether it has succeeded or failed.
