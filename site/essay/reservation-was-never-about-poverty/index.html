Reservation Was Never About Poverty

Why caste still shapes opportunity, representation and the debate over reservation in modern India.

It is quite evident that not everyone understands what caste is or what caste-based reservation actually exists for. Many still like to believe that India is a country where caste discrimination has completely disappeared because they live in cities. It is a comforting perspective to have, but reality is far more complicated. The easiest way to test whether caste has disappeared is not by looking at social media or corporate offices but by looking at marriage. The India Human Development Survey found in its second round, conducted in 2011-12, that only about 5% of married women aged 15 to 49 said theirs was an inter-caste marriage, unchanged from the previous round in 2004-05. Roughly 30% of rural and 20% of urban households also said they practised untouchability. Families still ask about caste before agreeing to a marriage, and matrimonial advertisements still proudly mention it. Honour killings over inter-caste marriages continue to happen. If caste had truly disappeared, marriage would have been the first institution where we noticed it. It hasn't. Caste continues to shape people's lives, and because it continues to shape people's lives, it continues to shape opportunities. Whenever I speak about reservation, I notice that many people immediately start talking about poverty. I understand why. For a long time, I also thought both were the same thing. But they are not. Poverty and caste are two completely different problems. A poor upper-caste family deserves economic support just as much as anyone else. Scholarships, welfare schemes and financial assistance should absolutely exist for that. Reservation, however, was never introduced to solve poverty. It was introduced because entire communities were denied education, representation and access to positions of power for centuries. The state itself now treats the two separately. In 2019 Parliament created a distinct 10% quota for economically weaker sections outside the SC, ST and OBC categories, and the Supreme Court upheld it in 2022 by a 3-2 majority. Those are two separate conversations, yet we somehow keep mixing them into one.

The Day I Started Looking at It Differently

I remember being in school and seeing students who were financially much better off than my family paying almost nothing in fees while my parents struggled to pay the full amount. Naturally, I thought the system was unfair. I used to ask myself how someone richer than me could receive benefits while I couldn't. It genuinely felt wrong. It was only much later, after I came across Subaltern Studies and started reading more about caste, that I realised I had been asking the wrong question. I was looking only at the economic condition of the individual standing in front of me. I wasn't looking at the history that stood behind that individual. The question was never who had more money. The question was who had been systematically excluded from education and public institutions for generations.

Representation Matters

People often reduce caste to occupation, but historically caste determined far more than that. It decided who could study, who could own land, who could enter temples, who could govern, and who had to perform the most degrading occupations in society. Thankfully, India has changed enormously since then, and many of those restrictions no longer exist in law. But social structures do not disappear simply because the law changes. This is where I think representation becomes important. If caste no longer influenced opportunities, then our institutions should naturally begin reflecting the society they represent. Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes alone made up about a quarter of India's population in the 2011 Census, at 16.6% and 8.6%. Nobody knows the exact OBC share, because caste beyond SC and ST hasn't been counted since 1931. The Mandal Commission extrapolated from those old figures and put it at 52%, and Bihar's 2023 caste survey found that OBCs and EBCs together made up 63% of that state. Census 2027 will finally record caste again, with the main enumeration due in February 2027, so this argument may soon rest on real numbers instead of estimates. If everything had become equal decades ago, we would expect that diversity to be visible among judges, professors, bureaucrats, chief ministers, prime ministers and every other institution of power. But that has never really been the case. Take the High Courts. A Law Ministry reply in the Rajya Sabha in March 2025 said that of the 715 High Court judges appointed since 2018, 551 (77%) came from the general category, against 22 SC, 16 ST, 89 OBC and 37 from religious minorities. The Constitution provides no reservation for judicial appointments, so those numbers show what a system without one looks like. At the very top, India has never had a Scheduled Caste Prime Minister. Narendra Modi identifies as OBC, although his community was included in Gujarat's OBC list much later, so people read that differently, and H. D. Deve Gowda has long said that he was the first OBC Prime Minister. Either way, the count is tiny. The point isn't to count individuals. The point is to ask why representation still looks so different from the society those institutions are meant to represent.

The Problem With the Merit Argument

Then comes the argument everyone eventually makes. Merit. I have always found that argument interesting because people often speak as though merit develops in isolation. Imagine two children with exactly the same intelligence. One grows up in a family where parents and grandparents are educated, someone at home knows how competitive examinations work, somebody can guide them through college admissions, interviews and career choices. The other becomes the first person in the family to even complete school. Nobody at home has experienced higher education. Nobody knows how competitive exams work or how university admissions happen. We expect both of them to compete from exactly the same starting line and then call the result merit. I don't think life has ever worked that way. That doesn't mean people from historically marginalised communities are less capable. It means many of them begin much further behind. As Dr. B.R. Ambedkar repeatedly argued, political equality means very little without social equality. In his last speech to the Constituent Assembly in 1949, he warned that India was about to enter a life of contradictions, with equality in politics and inequality in social and economic life. You cannot expect equal outcomes from unequal starting points and then call the system fair.

Reservation Is About Representation, Not Charity

I think this is where most debates go wrong. People talk about reservation as though it is some kind of favour being done to certain communities. I don't see it that way at all. Reservation is not charity. It is not a reward. It is not meant to punish upper castes either. It exists because a democracy should not have institutions that are dominated by only a few sections of society while others remain historically absent. It is an attempt to make public institutions resemble the society they are meant to serve. It isn't a modern political invention either. Article 16(4) sat in the original Constitution and Article 15(4) was added through the First Amendment in 1951. The Mandal Commission recommended a 27% OBC quota in 1980. It took the government until 1990 to announce it and the Supreme Court until 1992 to uphold it in the Indra Sawhney case, which also capped total reservation at 50%. Whether reservation has achieved its goal completely is another debate altogether, but that is the philosophy behind it.

Where I Think the System Needs Reform

None of this means reservation cannot be improved. In fact, I think it should be. The creamy layer debate is one of the most important discussions we can have. The Supreme Court introduced the idea in the Indra Sawhney judgment to keep the socially advanced out of OBC reservation, and the income ceiling has moved from 1 lakh rupees in 1993 to 8 lakh in 2017, where it has stayed since. The argument runs in both directions. If the same families keep receiving benefits generation after generation while equally disadvantaged members of their community are left behind, the purpose of reservation begins to weaken. But a parliamentary committee said in August 2025 that revising the ceiling was the need of the hour, because a limit that low shuts out many OBC families who still need the benefit, and the Social Justice Ministry told it that no revision was under consideration. The courts are pulling at the same thread. In March 2026 the Supreme Court held that parental income alone cannot decide creamy layer status and that the nature of the parents' employment has to count as well. In its 2024 ruling that allowed states to sub-classify reserved categories, four of the seven judges argued that the creamy layer should apply to SCs and STs too. There is also a plainer failure, which is implementation. As of April 2024, 6,988 posts reserved for SC, ST and OBC candidates were vacant across central universities, and 2,648 of them were teaching posts. A quota that isn't filled doesn't do the work it was created for. Reforming reservation is not the same as opposing reservation. A system can be improved without abandoning the principle behind it.

Before We Debate, We Should Understand

We can certainly debate how the system should evolve, whether the creamy layer should be expanded, whether implementation can be improved and whether reforms are necessary. Those are healthy conversations. But before debating reservation, we should first understand the problem it was created to solve. If we cannot see the difference between poverty and exclusion, perhaps we need to spend a little more time understanding the system before deciding whether it has succeeded or failed.
