The Big Ms: Two Schools of Acting, One Extraordinary Legacy

For half a century, Malayalam cinema has given us two giants who have not only shaped our cinema but have become part of our lives. Mammootty and Mohanlal, two different approaches to acting, two extraordinary careers, and perhaps the greatest rivalry that never needed to become a rivalry.

All of a sudden, it is Mammootty versus Mohanlal everywhere. I suppose it has something to do with Mammootty's recent release Om, with Dhanush in the lead, and the much-celebrated ₹100 crore box-office milestone. For years, it was a running meme that despite being such a brilliant actor, Mammootty had never had a ₹100 crore film. I honestly couldn't care less whether he had one or not. I have always considered him among the greatest actors I have ever seen, and a box-office number was never going to change that. But this sudden comparison made me realise something: I have written about so many things, yet I haven't written about the Big Ms. Every Malayali has been comparing these two for the past five decades. Half a century! We do it religiously, and I suppose we will continue doing it until the end of time. It is almost a ritual at this point. Someone brings up a new Mammootty performance, someone else responds with a Mohanlal classic, and before you know it, we are debating the same question all over again. I am not particularly familiar with every film industry in the world, so I won't pretend to know where they would rank among every actor who has ever lived. But I am ambitious enough to say that these two giants could easily stand among the finest actors anywhere. Like Al Pacino and Robert De Niro, they represent two different schools of acting, two distinct personalities and two entirely different ways of approaching a character. What makes their story even more astonishing is their longevity and friendship. My grandparents followed them well into their later years, my parents grew up watching them, and here I am, writing about them today. If longevity and masterclass performances are what we measure, I would probably place them at number one and number two, respectively. How many actors can remain relevant across generations, not merely by repeating what made them popular, but by continuing to surprise audiences who have watched them for decades?

Mammootty: The Character Comes First

Mammootty has described himself as a method actor, but I have always seen him differently. To me, he is a classical, observational and characterisation-driven actor, rather than someone who necessarily follows the Lee Strasberg school of method acting. I am not saying he doesn't use method acting in his own way, but the quality that stands out to me is his ability to observe, construct and inhabit a character. What fascinates me is his ability to shed his own personality and become someone entirely different. You can see a Mada in Ponthan Mada, a Bhaskara Patelar in Vidheyan, an Ahmed Haji in Paleri Manikyam, or a Pranchiyettan in Pranchiyettan and the Saint. These are not merely different characters played by the same actor; they feel like entirely different people who happen to be portrayed by Mammootty. His voice, posture, expressions, mannerisms and even the way he occupies a room change with the character. Watch the way Patelar carries himself, the way Mada speaks and moves, or the way Pranchiyettan's insecurities are reflected in his behaviour. Mammootty doesn't simply change his appearance and deliver the dialogue. He changes the entire person. And then you have Nanpakal Nerathu Mayakkam, where he plays Sundaram, a man who appears to become someone else, and Kaathal: The Core, where he takes on a character so far removed from the image many people associate with him. These performances require a kind of confidence that comes from being willing to abandon the comfort of one's own screen persona. And then there is Bramayugam, where he plays Chathan with such an extraordinary presence that I genuinely struggle to imagine another actor doing justice to that role. The director reportedly said that had Mammootty turned down the film, it might have been shelved. I can understand why. There is something about Mammootty's ability to disappear into a character that makes him almost irreplaceable in certain roles.

Mohanlal: The Actor Who Seems to Have No Limits

Mohanlal, on the other hand, is a completely different phenomenon. I sometimes feel that he plays a Mohanlal in all his films, but I don't mean that as a criticism. There is a recognisable quality to him, a certain naturalness and spontaneity that seems to belong entirely to his own acting instrument. Even when he is playing someone else, there is a Mohanlal-ness that I cannot quite separate from the character. And yet, what an actor! His performances often feel effortless, as though he is not acting at all. The smallest expression, a slight movement of the eyes, a pause before a line, the way he reacts to another character, can carry more meaning than an entire monologue. Iruvar is one of the peak performances by a male actor, in my opinion. The restraint, the ambition, the vulnerability and the gradual transformation of the character are all handled with such remarkable precision. And then there is Vanaprastham. I don't think a single actor, dead or alive, could have played Kunjikuttan quite the way Mohanlal did. The intensity, the body language, the transformation, the sheer physical commitment to learning Kathakali for the film. Fuck, that is beyond Christian Bale levels of dedication. It is not simply about learning the movements of an art form; it is about making those movements part of the character's inner life. Mohanlal's physicality is one of his greatest strengths. He can communicate anger, affection, embarrassment, arrogance or despair through the way he stands or moves. His range seems almost impossible to contain, and I struggle to think of many roles that would be beyond him. But there are certain characters, particularly those requiring an exceptionally compelling villainous or historical presence, where I feel Mammootty has a different kind of advantage. Mohanlal can be extraordinary in almost anything, but that doesn't mean he can be every actor at once.

They Could Play Each Other, But Never the Same Way

I don't think Mohanlal could play Bhaskara Patelar in Vidheyan or Pazhassi Raja in Kerala Varma Pazhassi Raja the way Mammootty did. Similarly, I don't think Mammootty could play Kunjikuttan in Vanaprastham or Mangalassery Neelakandan in Devasuram the way Mohanlal did. They could absolutely play each other's roles, but they would play them in their own way, and that is the beauty of having two such distinct actors. Imagine Mohanlal as Patelar. He would undoubtedly bring something fascinating to the character, but I don't think he would produce the same terrifying, almost animalistic authority that Mammootty brought to him. Likewise, imagine Mammootty as Neelakandan. He could certainly make the character compelling, but the particular combination of arrogance, charm, impulsiveness and vulnerability that Mohanlal brought to that role belongs to him. This is not about one actor being incapable of playing a role. It is about the fact that their artistic instincts are different. They would approach the same character from different directions, and the result would be two entirely different interpretations. That is what makes them two schools of acting rather than simply two actors competing for the same throne. Mammootty's strength, to me, lies in the distance he can travel from himself to the character. Mohanlal's strength lies in how much he can bring out of his own extraordinary acting instrument. One seems to disappear into the role, while the other seems to discover an entirely new dimension of himself through it. And both approaches can produce greatness.

A Personal Preference, Not a Verdict

I don't think there is a clear winner, and I don't think there ever will be. We can compare performances, discuss technique, argue about roles and spend another fifty years debating the same question. But ultimately, we are comparing two different kinds of greatness. Mohanlal has a natural brilliance and a range that seems almost impossible to contain, while Mammootty has an extraordinary ability to transform himself into characters that feel completely separate from the man playing them. If I had to lean towards one, I would probably choose Mammootty, simply because of that disappearance into character. When I watch his cinema, I don't see Mammootty. I see Mada, Patelar, Ahmed Haji, Pranchiyettan, Chathan or Sundaram. I see a man who can play a terrifying feudal landlord, a vulnerable ordinary person, a strange supernatural presence and a deeply flawed individual, sometimes within a remarkably short span of time. You need an incredible calibre of actor to pull that off. Mohanlal does all these things too, of course, and his performances are no less remarkable. But I feel Mammootty's transformations are often more visibly radical, and that is the particular quality of acting I admire most. I am neither a learned film reviewer nor an analyst, and I could be completely wrong about how I understand their techniques. But I don't think I need a degree in film criticism to know what moves me when I watch a performance. Perhaps that is enough. Two actors, five decades, generations of audiences, and a friendship that has outlasted every comparison. The Big Ms. What a privilege it has been to grow up watching them, and what a privilege it will be to keep watching them.
