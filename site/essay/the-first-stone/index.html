The First Stone

I no longer know what I believe about God, but the words of Jesus still make me stop and think.

Though I was born and raised a Christian, I find it difficult to call myself religious anymore, at least not in the traditional sense. I do not attend church regularly, quote scripture in everyday conversations, or shape my life around divine commandments. But I cannot call myself an atheist either. I do not know whether there is a higher power, and I am not convinced that I need to arrive at a definite answer. I suppose agnosticism comes closest to describing where I stand, though even that feels like putting a label on something I am still trying to understand. I am open to belief, but unconvinced. For now, I find myself more comfortable admitting what I do not know than pretending to be certain.

Being Called by Your Name

Recently, I started watching The Chosen. I did not expect much from it. I thought I would watch it out of curiosity, perhaps appreciating it as a retelling of a familiar religious story without becoming particularly invested in it. Instead, a moment at the end of the first episode caught me off guard. Throughout the episode, everyone calls this troubled woman Lilith, and we come to know her by that name too. It is only when Jesus approaches her that we learn who she really is. His entrance is almost unnoticeable, nothing grand or theatrical. He simply walks up to her and gently touches her shoulder. When he calls her Mary, Mary Magdalene, there is no elaborate speech or dramatic explanation. He simply recognises her. I did not expect that moment to affect me as much as it did. Perhaps it was the simplicity of it. Everyone else sees a woman who has lost herself, but Jesus sees Mary. He does not begin by asking her to explain herself or prove that she deserves to be helped. He calls her by the name that belongs to her, as though the person she once was has not disappeared completely. There is also something about Lilith herself that I find difficult to ignore. I am not entirely sure how to categorise her in terms of mythology, folklore and religious tradition, but she is one of the most fascinating figures I have come across. There is so much surrounding her name, and I suspect I might write about her at some point. In The Chosen, the name Lilith becomes the identity by which everyone knows this woman, even the audience, until Jesus calls her Mary. It is a fictionalised portrayal of a biblical encounter, but something about it felt deeply human. Sometimes, being seen for who you are, rather than being judged for what you have become, can make all the difference.

Recognition as Grace

I do not know whether Jesus was God. I am not sure I will ever know. But I can understand why people who believe in him find something extraordinary in the way he treated others. Even if I set aside the question of his divinity, the person portrayed in these stories is remarkable: someone who could look at people whom society had rejected and see something beyond their mistakes, reputations and circumstances. We are quick to reduce people to the worst thing they have done, the choices they regret or the parts of themselves they struggle to hide. We want explanations, apologies and evidence that someone deserves another chance. Sometimes, we make it almost impossible for people to be anything other than the version of themselves we have already decided they are. In that scene, Jesus does not offer Mary a complicated argument about who she ought to be. He recognises her. And perhaps that is why the moment stays with me. Whatever one believes about miracles, there is something extraordinary about being recognised when you have lost the ability to recognise yourself. I may not know whether grace comes from God, but I understand why someone might experience it in a moment like that.

The Weight of a Single Sentence

Another story that has stayed with me is the account of the woman who is about to be stoned. The crowd is ready to punish her, and the punishment is presented as justified by the law. Jesus does not respond with violence or attempt to overpower the crowd. Instead, he says, "Let the one who has never sinned cast the first stone." It is such a simple sentence, yet it changes the entire situation. The attention shifts from the woman being judged to the people doing the judging. One by one, they leave. What strikes me is that Jesus does not need to prove that the woman has done nothing wrong. He does not make her innocence the condition for her being spared. Instead, he asks the people who are ready to punish her to examine themselves. The story does not require us to pretend that wrongdoing does not matter. It asks whether we are ever as free from fault as we imagine ourselves to be when we condemn someone else. Holding someone accountable and believing that they deserve to be humiliated or destroyed are not the same thing. We can acknowledge wrongdoing without reducing a person to it. We can disagree with someone's actions without appointing ourselves the final authority on their worth.

The Ease of Judgment

That is what makes the story feel relevant even now. We have found countless ways to judge one another, and the internet has made the process almost effortless. Someone makes a mistake, says something offensive or is accused of doing something wrong, and before long, thousands of strangers have decided what kind of person they are. Sometimes criticism is justified. Sometimes people need to be held accountable. But there is also a peculiar pleasure in watching someone else fall from grace, especially when we can convince ourselves that we would never make the same mistake. I am not exempt from this. It is easy to recognise the cruelty of public judgment when someone else is on the receiving end of it. It is considerably harder when we dislike the person being judged or believe they deserve what is happening to them. Perhaps that is why the story of the woman and the crowd matters. It does not ask us to abandon our sense of right and wrong. It asks us to approach it with some humility. Before deciding what another person deserves, we might consider how much we really know about them, how easily we excuse our own failures, and whether punishment has become more important to us than understanding. For all the centuries that separate us from the world in which these stories are set, that question has not become any easier to answer.

What Still Remains

I do not know whether I will return to the religious life I grew up with. I may never believe in God in the way I once did, if I ever did with the certainty I now associate with religious belief. Watching The Chosen has not suddenly resolved my doubts, nor has it persuaded me to accept everything Christianity teaches. But it has made me look at Jesus differently. What stays with me is not a theological argument or a demonstration of divine power. It is the way he treats people in these stories: the willingness to look beyond a reputation, to question a crowd that believes itself righteous, and to offer dignity to someone who has been denied it. Perhaps believers see these moments as evidence of God's nature. I can understand that. I see something meaningful in them even without knowing whether I share that belief. I do not think I have to settle the question of Jesus's divinity before I can consider the value of what he stood for. I began watching the series without expecting it to affect me. I am still uncertain about what I believe, and I do not want to replace one unquestioned certainty with another. But I find myself returning to those scenes, particularly the moment when Jesus calls Mary by her name. Maybe it is because being known, without first having to justify your existence, is something most of us need more than we admit. And maybe the story of the first stone stays with me for a similar reason. It reminds me that the people we condemn are not the only ones whose lives deserve scrutiny. Before we join a crowd in deciding who deserves punishment, there is a question worth asking ourselves. Not whether we are perfect, but whether we have been as willing to examine our own faults as we have been to expose someone else's. I do not know if that is faith. Perhaps it is not. For now, I am content to leave the question unanswered.
