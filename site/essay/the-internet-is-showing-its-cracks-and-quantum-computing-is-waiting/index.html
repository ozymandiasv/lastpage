The Internet Is Showing Its Cracks, and Quantum Computing Is Waiting

A critical flaw in PHP has exposed how fragile our digital world can be. As AI accelerates cyberattacks and quantum computing threatens the foundations of encryption, the internet we know may be approaching its biggest transformation yet.

The recent discovery of critical vulnerabilities in PHP has made me think about just how fragile the internet really is. PHP is one of the technologies that quietly powers a huge part of the web, working behind the scenes of websites, applications and services that millions of people use every day. The newly disclosed flaws could allow attackers to execute code remotely on vulnerable servers without even logging in. Security agencies have issued urgent warnings, and organisations have been forced to identify vulnerable systems and apply patches. What unsettles me is not simply that a flaw exists. Software will always have flaws. It is the realisation that a weakness buried deep inside a widely used technology can put systems across the world at risk. We have built an extraordinary amount of modern life on layers of software that most of us will never understand, let alone inspect. We trust them with our money, conversations, photographs, businesses and identities. Yet sometimes, a mistake in a small part of that enormous structure is enough to put everything connected to it in danger. (Google Cloud)

When AI Makes Finding the Weakness Easier

What makes this moment more unsettling is that artificial intelligence is changing the way vulnerabilities are discovered and exploited. AI-assisted research has played a role in uncovering weaknesses like the recent PHP flaws, demonstrating how machine intelligence can help researchers find problems that may have escaped attention for years. The same capabilities, in the wrong hands, could help attackers identify vulnerable systems, automate parts of their attacks and move faster than the people responsible for defending against them. A vulnerability that once required rare expertise and considerable time to investigate could become easier to discover and exploit at scale. The consequences are not limited to a website being taken offline. A compromised server can expose private information, damage databases or become a foothold for further attacks. I think we are entering a period in which the old assumption that security teams can simply react after a problem appears will become increasingly difficult to maintain. Software will need to be tested more continuously, systems will need to be updated faster, and organisations will have to think about security before a vulnerability becomes tomorrow���s headline. The internet is not suddenly going to collapse, but the contest between those who find weaknesses and those who repair them is becoming faster and more demanding. (AP News)

Quantum Computing Is a Different Kind of Threat

But there is another challenge approaching, and it reaches deeper than any individual software flaw: quantum computing. Today’s internet relies on cryptographic systems that help secure online banking, private communications, digital identities and the exchange of information between devices. Powerful enough quantum computers could eventually break some of the mathematical systems on which much of this security depends. We do not yet know when a machine capable of doing this will exist, and today’s quantum computers are not powerful enough to break the encryption that protects the internet at large. But the preparation has already begun. Researchers and standards organisations are developing post-quantum cryptography, which uses mathematical techniques designed to resist attacks from quantum computers, and important standards have already been published. There is also a particularly troubling possibility: attackers can collect encrypted information today and store it until future technology makes it possible to decrypt it. Sensitive data that must remain private for years could therefore be at risk even before a sufficiently powerful quantum computer arrives. I find this unsettling because it means the problem is not something we can postpone until the technology is ready. We have to rebuild parts of our security infrastructure in advance, while the existing internet is still running. (NIST)

The Internet We Use in Five Years

I suspect the next few years will bring changes to the internet that most people will barely notice at first, even as the technology underneath them changes significantly. Websites will continue to open, messages will continue to arrive, and people will carry on shopping, working and watching videos. But behind that familiar experience, security protocols will be updated, encryption methods will change, software will be rewritten, and AI will become more deeply involved in both protecting and attacking digital infrastructure. The transition to post-quantum security alone will require years of coordination across browsers, operating systems, servers, businesses and governments. Not every part of the internet will be replaced, and quantum computing will not magically make every existing security system useless overnight. Still, I think we should stop imagining the internet as a finished invention. It is an evolving structure, and some of the assumptions on which it was built are being challenged at the same time. The PHP vulnerabilities are a reminder that even the systems we depend on today can contain weaknesses that remain hidden for years. Quantum computing represents a more fundamental challenge to some of the protections we rely on, while AI is accelerating the pace at which weaknesses can be found. We may look back on this period as the beginning of a major security transition, one that changes how the internet protects our data and how much trust we can place in the systems around us. The internet of the near future may look familiar on our screens, but underneath, it could be a very different machine.
