The Quiet Way to Silence a Newsroom

Access, not censorship, is becoming the tool of choice for governments who dislike scrutiny.

A few days ago, I wrote about press freedom in India and how accountability can slowly disappear without anyone officially banning the press. It happens not through some dramatic shutdown of newspapers or mass arrests of journalists, but through something much simpler, making sure difficult questions never reach the people who are supposed to answer them. You can read that piece here, The Silence Strategy. Today, the same question came back to me from the other side of the world. Donald Trump has announced that CNN, MS NOW and Politico are being banned from the White House, accusing them of repeatedly reporting what he calls "fake news." He also suggested that other media organisations could follow.

The Problem With the Question

What bothers me isn't whether CNN is a good news organisation. It isn't whether Politico has published something wrong, or whether Trump genuinely believes these outlets have treated him unfairly. News organisations can get things wrong, and politicians have every right to criticise them. The problem starts when criticism from the government becomes control over access. The White House is not a private office. Journalists are there to question the President, report what happens and provide information to everyone outside those walls. Removing organisations because of what they report changes that relationship completely. And the timing makes this even more interesting. The journalists from these organisations were still inside the White House after Trump announced the ban, with no immediate indication that the order had actually been implemented.

The Same Pattern, Different Country

In India, I was writing about journalists asking questions and governments simply refusing to answer them. Sometimes the question disappears into silence. Sometimes the attention is redirected towards the journalist instead. In Washington, the mechanism is different. The President is openly saying that certain news organisations should not have access to the White House because of what they report. The circumstances and the systems could hardly be more different, yet the basic problem is oddly familiar. What happens once people in power stop treating an uncomfortable question as something they actually have to answer?

When Access Becomes a Weapon

A press conference is supposed to work in a fairly simple way. The government speaks. Journalists question it. The public gets to hear both. But if access can be withdrawn whenever a journalist becomes sufficiently critical, the incentive changes. You don't have to tell a journalist what to write. You just have to make them wonder what will happen if they write it. That is a much quieter form of pressure, and it doesn't require banning every newspaper, television channel or website. A few examples can be enough to make everyone else understand where the line is.

The Pattern Underneath

The press isn't supposed to be right all the time. It isn't supposed to be friendly to the government either. Its job is to ask questions, including the questions politicians would rather not answer. Trump can call CNN "fake news." The Indian government can disagree with journalists. Governments everywhere can challenge reporting they believe is false. But the answer to bad journalism should be more scrutiny, more evidence and more journalism. Because once the people in power get to decide which journalists are allowed to question them based on what those journalists report, the press conference may still look exactly the same. The cameras are still there. The microphones are still on. But the question has already been silenced.
