The Trouble With a Boring, Happy Couple

From American sitcoms to Indian soaps, entertainment shapes our sense of normal more than our actions.

I was watching the trailer for a series called The Miniature Wife, and a comment underneath it caught me off guard. Someone was thrilled that Hollywood had finally shown a healthy romantic relationship. Why would someone specifically notice and celebrate the fact that a fictional series was showing a healthy relationship? And considering that The Miniature Wife is a sitcom, I also have my doubts about how long that healthy relationship is going to remain healthy. A couple that communicates, trusts each other and stays faithful makes for a great marriage. It doesn't make for great television. Add an ex who won't stay gone, a secret affair, a love triangle, and you've got a season's worth of plot. That comment stuck with me longer than the show itself. Entertainment doesn't tell us how to live, but it shapes what we think normal looks like.

Not About Propaganda

Let me be clear about what I'm not saying. I'm not accusing Hollywood of pushing a culture on anyone, and this isn't a propaganda argument. Nobody starts sleeping around because they watched a sitcom. What I'm talking about is familiarity. See something enough times and it starts to feel normal, whether or not it actually is. And entertainment has a strange power here, since it can pack years of behaviour into a few hours. A character can have several relationships, sleep with several people, break up, find another partner, return to an ex and have another dramatic relationship, all within the same season. Nobody watching this necessarily thinks, "This is what I am supposed to do." But after watching hundreds of characters behave this way, it is possible to start developing an impression that this is simply what modern relationships look like.

Fiction vs. Statistics

This is where I think the gap between fiction and statistics becomes important. The stereotype created by some American television and movies can make it appear as though everybody is constantly dating, having casual sex, changing partners and sleeping with different people. But the actual statistics are considerably less dramatic. The U.S. National Survey of Family Growth, using data collected between 2015 and 2019, found that sexually experienced Americans aged 25 to 49 had a median of 4.3 lifetime opposite-sex partners for women and 6.3 for men. Over the previous twelve months, only about 2% of women and 2.9% of men aged 15 to 49 reported five or more partners. These numbers don't mean that casual relationships or hookups are rare in America, nor do they tell us how every individual lives. They simply demonstrate something I think gets lost when we watch too much fictional entertainment: the television version of American dating culture is not necessarily the statistical version of American dating culture. If a character has three relationships in one season, that is because the writers need three relationships to create three stories. An ordinary person might spend years with one person, which is probably much closer to reality, but it doesn't give a television writer nearly as much material.

What The Research Actually Shows

There is also research suggesting that entertainment can influence people, particularly younger people, and this is where the discussion gets more complicated. A longitudinal study of 1,792 American adolescents found an association between exposure to sexual content on television and later sexual behaviour. Another study found that the relationship could run in both directions: young people who consumed more sexual media were more likely to progress sexually, but young people who were already becoming sexually active were also more likely to consume sexual media. I find the second part particularly important because it stops us from reducing the entire thing to "television makes people behave this way." People influence entertainment just as much as entertainment influences people. If a particular type of relationship becomes interesting to an audience, writers produce more of it. When people see more of it, it becomes more familiar. When something becomes familiar, it can become more socially acceptable or at least less surprising. Eventually the behaviour becomes part of the culture that produces the next generation of entertainment. It is therefore much more complicated than somebody sitting in Hollywood deciding what everyone else should believe.

Behaviour vs. Expectation

Perhaps the more interesting influence isn't even behaviour itself. It's expectation. Take someone who's never been in a serious relationship but has spent years watching American sitcoms. Friends sleep together, exes keep coming back, one-night stands turn into funny stories, infidelity gets resolved by the next episode. That person doesn't copy any of it. They just start assuming this is what dating looks like for everyone else. Saying "television made someone do something" is a different claim from saying "television shaped what they think other people do." The second is far more defensible, and there's research on sexual media exposure showing entertainment can shift how people perceive social norms, especially among adolescents.

Why Healthy Relationships Are Hard To Write

There is another reason this happens, and it has very little to do with morality. Healthy relationships are simply difficult to write. Imagine a couple who love each other, trust each other, communicate when they have problems and remain committed to each other. What exactly is the conflict? You can certainly write an interesting story about them, but you have to create something outside the relationship to generate the drama. Now imagine another couple where one person is secretly seeing someone else, the other suspects something, an ex returns, their friend knows the truth and nobody is willing to tell anybody anything. That is practically a television show writing itself. Drama requires conflict, and romantic conflict is one of the easiest forms of conflict because almost everyone understands it. This is why entertainment can end up exaggerating dysfunctional relationships without necessarily having any intention of promoting them. The unhealthy relationship is simply more useful to the story.

The Sitcom Reset

Comedy makes this even more interesting because things that would be emotionally devastating in real life can become jokes when placed inside a sitcom. Someone cheats, everyone finds out, there is an awkward conversation, the audience laughs and then the next episode begins. Real life doesn't have a twenty-two-minute runtime followed by an advertisement break and an episode reset. Infidelity can destroy trust, friendships, marriages and families, and there can be consequences that last for years. A fictional character can make a terrible decision on Monday and be completely fine by Friday because the writers have another storyline to deal with. I am personally against infidelity, but that isn't really the point I am trying to make here. Two consenting adults choosing to have a casual relationship is one question, while somebody breaking an agreement of exclusivity with their partner is another. People can have very different ideas about relationships and sexuality without those things being identical to cheating. What interests me here is not telling adults how they should live, but understanding what happens when fictional behaviour becomes so common on screen that it starts to look statistically normal.

The Indian Comparison

This is where I think India becomes an interesting comparison. Indian television has its own very different version of this problem. The traditional soap opera has often revolved around marriage, family honour, female chastity, sacrifice, suspicion and the preservation of the family. The "good woman" is frequently defined through her loyalty to her husband and family, while her character is repeatedly tested through suspicion, betrayal, marriage problems and questions about her sexual purity. I am not saying every Indian serial is like this, and certainly not every Indian family lives this way. But anyone who has spent enough time around Indian television knows the pattern. If someone watched only these serials, they could develop a rather distorted impression of what Indian families and relationships are actually like. In that sense, the difference between Indian and Western entertainment is fascinating. One can exaggerate traditional ideas around chastity, marriage and family honour, while the other can exaggerate sexual experimentation, dating and relationship turnover. They may look like opposite cultural extremes, but they have something important in common. Both are taking parts of real life and magnifying them because those parts make good stories.

Fiction Is Not A Census

This is why I am reluctant to argue that one culture is simply "better" or "worse" than another based on entertainment. A television series is not a census. A soap opera is not a sociological survey. A sitcom isn't evidence that millions of people are living exactly like its characters. Fiction is selective by its very nature. It takes ordinary life, strips out most of the boring parts and keeps whatever produces emotion, conflict, comedy, fear or curiosity. If a television series contains ten characters and eight of them have complicated romantic lives, that doesn't mean eight out of ten people in the real world have complicated romantic lives. It means the writers discovered that complicated romantic lives keep people watching.

What "Normal" Actually Means

The word "normal" is probably where we get into trouble. Something can be normal because it is common. It can be normal because society accepts it. Or it can simply feel normal because we have seen it so many times. Those three things are not necessarily the same. Something can be relatively uncommon in society but appear constantly in entertainment because it makes a good story. Something can be extremely common in real life but almost never appear on television because it is boring. Millions of people quietly maintaining long-term relationships probably don't produce much drama, which is exactly why they can become almost invisible in entertainment. Meanwhile, a tiny percentage of people doing something unusual can appear in hundreds of movies and series because unusual behaviour is far more interesting to watch.

Back To That Comment

That brings me back to the strange comment I saw underneath The Miniature Wife. Someone saw a fictional healthy relationship and immediately felt it was unusual enough to celebrate. Maybe that says something about the show. Maybe it says something about the audience. Or maybe it simply demonstrates the fundamental problem with entertainment: healthy relationships are often less dramatic than unhealthy ones. There is no affair to uncover, no mysterious ex, no love triangle, no betrayal and no dramatic breakup in the rain. There are just two people who like each other, trust each other and try to make their relationship work. That may not make particularly exciting television, but it is probably one of the most ordinary things in real life. I don't think the answer is to stop watching American movies or television, nor do I think entertainment needs to become a moral classroom where every character has to demonstrate the correct way to live. People are perfectly capable of watching fictional characters without copying them. At the same time, it is worth remembering that entertainment is entertainment first and a representation of society second. The Indian woman whose character is questioned for hundreds of episodes is not necessarily representative of Indian women. The American character who has five relationships in a season is not necessarily representative of American adults. Both are fictional characters placed inside stories designed to keep us watching. The interesting part is what happens when we forget that distinction. Fiction doesn't have to force us to change our behaviour to influence us. Sometimes it only has to change our perception of what everyone else is doing. Perhaps that is ultimately what bothered me about that comment. Not that someone wanted to see a healthy relationship, because that is perfectly reasonable. What bothered me was the implication that a healthy relationship had become unusual enough in entertainment that its presence alone felt remarkable. Maybe that is simply the result of writers discovering that dysfunction is more entertaining than stability. Maybe it reflects changes in culture. Maybe it reflects what audiences want to watch. Probably it's a mixture of all three. But it's worth remembering that the world outside our screens is considerably less dramatic than the world inside them. Most people are not living inside a sitcom, and most relationships do not contain enough betrayal, scandal and romantic chaos to fill twelve episodes. Sometimes the boring version of life is actually the normal version of life, and perhaps we should be careful not to mistake the things that make good television for the things that are most common in reality.
