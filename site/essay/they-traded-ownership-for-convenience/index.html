They Traded Ownership for Convenience

The internet didn’t just change how we consume media. Somewhere along the way, it convinced us to stop owning it.

Recently, I had a small party with a few friends in a remote location where there was absolutely no internet. It didn’t matter at first. We were talking, cooking, laughing and simply enjoying being away from our phones for a while. But later that night, just as we were about to pour ourselves some drinks, somebody asked the most obvious question in the world. “How do we play music?” Almost instinctively every one of us reached for our phones. Spotify. YouTube Music. Nothing. No internet meant no music. It was such a simple moment, yet it made me realise something I had never really thought about before. We weren’t missing music because nobody had brought speakers or because we forgot to download songs. We were missing music because none of us actually owned the music we listened to. We had spent years paying for access to millions of songs, yet the moment somebody else’s servers became unreachable, those millions of songs simply ceased to exist for us. The same thing would happen with films, television series, podcasts, newspapers and almost every other form of digital entertainment. Somewhere along the way we stopped owning things and became comfortable renting every part of our digital lives.

The Subscription Economy Didn’t Sell Us Products, It Sold Us Dependence

People often praise the subscription economy because it is convenient, and to be fair, it is. For a small monthly payment you suddenly have access to millions of songs, thousands of films, television series, books, software and cloud storage. On paper it feels like the greatest deal humanity has ever invented. But convenience always comes at a price, and I don’t think that price is the monthly subscription. The real price is ownership. Today most people don’t own their music. They don’t own their films. They don’t own the software they use every day. Companies have even started convincing people that renting furniture, renting clothes, leasing phones and leasing laptops is somehow the new normal. Apple wants you to keep paying forever instead of actually owning the device in your hand. Software companies don’t sell software anymore, they sell subscriptions. Newspapers don’t sell newspapers anymore, they sell subscriptions. We are slowly moving toward a world where almost everything meaningful exists only as long as somebody else’s servers decide to keep serving you. The internet gave us access to everything, but in exchange it quietly took ownership away from us.

This Isn’t About Productivity. It’s About Owning Yourself

Unlike many people who criticise social media, this is not another lecture about productivity. Productivity for what exactly? To work harder so somebody else becomes richer? To spend your life optimising yourself only to buy more subscriptions that you never truly own? I honestly don’t care if someone spends an evening reading a novel, watching films, listening to music or simply staring into the abyss doing absolutely nothing. In fact, I think staring into the abyss is healthier than endlessly feeding an algorithm that has spent years learning exactly how to keep your attention for one more reel, one more short, one more recommendation. This is not about wasting time. This is about ownership. The average person spends 5-10 hours every day looking at a screen, yet very little of what they consume actually belongs to them. Algorithms decide the next song, the next video, the next article, the next film, the next outrage and even the next opinion. We think we have unlimited choice because there are millions of options, but the truth is we rarely choose anymore. We simply accept whatever the algorithm places in front of us.

Build Something That Actually Belongs to You

I am not a software engineer. I don’t work in technology. Everything I built was done with curiosity, a lot of trial and error, and yes, the help of the same AI that may or may not destroy us someday. Today I have my own Debian server running on old hardware. My music belongs to me. My films belong to me. My television series belong to me. My audiobooks, podcasts, photographs, documents and even my AI models belong to me. I write on my own blog. I store my own photos. I run my own text-to-speech models. None of it depends on whether Netflix decides to remove a film, Spotify loses a license, Google changes a policy or my internet connection disappears. The funny part is that people imagine this requires expensive hardware when it really doesn’t. An old i3 laptop with 8 GB of RAM is enough to run an astonishing amount of software today. Your phone, your television, your tablet and your laptop can all connect to it. You can carry your music into the middle of a desert without worrying about mobile data, subscriptions or whether someone else’s cloud still remembers you. For the first time in years I genuinely feel like I own my digital life instead of merely renting access to it.

Buy Yourself Back

I am not telling anyone to cancel every subscription tomorrow. Convenience has value. But I think we should at least recognise what we traded away. We traded ownership for convenience, and we accepted that trade so willingly that most people no longer even realise it happened. Own your music if you can. Own your photographs. Own your writing. Own your memories. Own your data. Build something that exists because you decided it should exist, not because a corporation allows it to. Even if you never build a home server, at least understand the difference between access and ownership. Because the moment somebody else controls everything you consume, they begin controlling far more than your media. They begin controlling your choices. And that, to me, is a trade humanity should never have made. And let me make one thing very clear. I have spent literally nothing building this setup. Everything runs on an old machine that most people would probably throw away or leave collecting dust in a corner. So before you think this requires expensive hardware or a huge investment, try picking up that old laptop or desktop you no longer use. Chances are, it is still more than capable of giving you complete ownership over your digital life. Peace.
