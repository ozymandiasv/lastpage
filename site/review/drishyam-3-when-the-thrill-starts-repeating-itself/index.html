Drishyam 3: When the Thrill Starts Repeating Itself

Unpredictability made Drishyam special, but repeating the same trick can only work for so long.

Type Movie
Rating 6/10
Stars ★★★★★★☆☆☆☆

Spoilers ahead. Drishyam 3: The Conclusion was, overall, a good movie. It kept the franchise’s atmosphere intact, had enough tension to keep me watching, and unlike the Malayalam Drishyam 3, I never felt that the film had completely lost its grip on the thriller aspect. But the biggest problem is also the most obvious one: it felt repetitive. And strangely enough, if I had to compare the Hindi and Malayalam versions, I think the Hindi film actually outsmarted the Malayalam one this time. I never thought I would say that about Drishyam, because the Malayalam films are where this entire thing came from, but here we are.

Unpredictability Is Not Always Good

The Malayalam Drishyam 3 had something the Hindi version did not: it was genuinely difficult to predict where it was going. The problem is that unpredictability by itself does not automatically make a story good. A film can surprise you and still leave you wondering why the story went there in the first place. That was my biggest issue with the Malayalam version. There were moments where I genuinely could not predict what was coming, but the eventual payoff did not give me the satisfaction that Drishyam and Drishyam 2 once did. The first two films were almost the perfect combination of being unpredictable and making sense once everything was revealed. You could look back at the entire story and realise that the pieces had been sitting there all along. That is what made them so good.

Drishyam Was Built on the Perfect Alibi

The original Drishyam and Drishyam 2 were brilliant because Jeethu Joseph understood that the real thrill was not simply discovering who committed the crime. It was watching someone construct an entire reality around it. The manipulation of dates, the carefully manufactured alibi, the body, the police investigation and the constant fear that one tiny mistake could destroy everything created a very particular kind of tension. There have always been comparisons between Drishyam and Keigo Higashino’s The Devotion of Suspect X, particularly because of the murder cover-up and the construction of an alibi, although Jeethu Joseph has denied that Drishyam was an adaptation or copy. Personally, I would call it loosely inspired at most rather than directly adapted. Whatever the origin of the similarities, Jeethu took the basic idea and built something distinctly his own around it. Drishyam 2 then took an enormous amount of time building towards its conclusion, but when the ending finally arrived, that long build-up suddenly felt justified.

But What Exactly Changed This Time?

That is where Drishyam 3 loses me. I kept waiting for the moment where the film would make me realise that I had completely misunderstood what I had been watching, but most of the time I was already several steps ahead. It felt less like a new game and more like Vijay playing another variation of the same game we had already watched twice. The basic pleasure of Drishyam was always trying to figure out how Vijay would stay ahead of the police. Here, the question became almost routine. Even when the film introduced new characters and new complications, I kept feeling that I had seen the structure before. There is nothing inherently wrong with giving a franchise a familiar identity, but when the identity of the franchise is built around outsmarting the audience, familiarity becomes a problem.

And Then There Is That Courtroom

The courtroom sequences are probably where my suspension of disbelief finally gave up and went home. The Drishyam films have never exactly been documentaries about how Indian courts and police investigations work, and I am willing to give the series some creative liberty because the entire premise depends on a certain amount of cinematic convenience. But even then, the proceedings sometimes move at such incredible speed that you start wondering which country this court is operating in. The idea that the court can order the exhumation of a body and the police can seemingly leave the courtroom, go and exhume it while the proceedings are still happening, and return with the evidence almost as though they had gone to pick up lunch is wonderfully cinematic but legally hilarious. I can accept it as a creative shortcut because the alternative would probably involve three hours of procedural paperwork, but it does make the courtroom drama considerably less convincing.

The Performances Keep It Alive

The performances are what stop the film from becoming genuinely frustrating. Ajay Devgn is alright as Vijay; by now the character is so deeply associated with him so you know that action, not comparing him with Mohanlal for obvious reasons. Tabu, Jaideep Ahlawat and Rajat Kapoor, on the other hand, brought much more energy to the investigation and conflict. Jaideep especially fits perfectly into this universe because he gives the police side enough intelligence and menace to make Vijay’s situation interesting again. Prakash Raj was perfectly fine, while Shriya Saran and some of the supporting performances did not work for me at all. Kamlesh Sawant was good too, although I have to admit that I was amused by how the franchise once again seemed determined to create a character whose primary purpose is to become a physical threat that Vijay’s family can somehow overcome.

Maybe We Should Stop Now

And honestly, perhaps we should stop making Drishyam films. The Malayalam franchise has already confirmed another instalment, so apparently Georgekutty is not done with us yet. I understand why the makers want to continue. Drishyam is one of those rare franchises where the central character is so fascinating that audiences will naturally want to know what happens next. But there is a point where continuing a story stops being about finding another great story and starts being about finding another reason to keep the character alive. The first two films had a very clear sense of discovery. By the third, I felt like I was watching the franchise remember what made itself successful. Overall, Drishyam 3 is not a bad film. Far from it. It is watchable, reasonably entertaining and still manages to maintain some of the thrill that made this franchise special. But for me, it is the weakest of the Hindi Drishyam films, and I would put the Malayalam third film in roughly the same territory, although for completely different reasons. The Malayalam one had the advantage of unpredictability but struggled to make that unpredictability satisfying; the Hindi one was more entertaining but far too predictable. And perhaps that is the funniest thing about Drishyam at this point: a franchise that became famous for constantly staying one step ahead of the audience has finally started running out of places to hide.
