Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar: When Strategy Isn’t Enough

An engaging Malayalam crime thriller that tries to outsmart its audience but occasionally forgets to outsmart its own plot holes.

Type Movie
Rating 7/10
Stars ★★★★★★★☆☆☆

!!!SPOILER!!!! Malayalam cinema returns to one of its favourite territories: hiding a crime and staying one step ahead of the investigation. And naturally, it is difficult to discuss Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar without bringing up Drishyam. The films approach their central idea differently, though. While Drishyam places considerable emphasis on strategy, circumstances and the occasional stroke of luck, Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar attempts to build its tension around calculated decisions and the characters’ understanding of police procedures. Overall, I thought it was a good watch, certainly not on the level of Drishyam, but decent enough to keep me interested. The premise is compelling, and watching police officers attempt to conceal a crime while another officer investigates it creates an interesting battle of wits. However, the film’s biggest problem is that it wants us to admire the planning without always giving us enough reason to believe that the plan could actually work.

A Clever Plan, An Incomplete Investigation

What bothered me most was not the strategy itself, but the weaknesses in the investigation that supposedly challenges it. Consider the car that is moved a considerable distance. How does such a movement escape CCTV coverage entirely? Was there no camera along the route, near a junction, at a business establishment or anywhere else that could have provided a lead? Of course, not every road has surveillance cameras, and the absence of CCTV footage is not automatically a plot hole. But when a film builds its suspense around a detailed police investigation, it needs to establish why investigators cannot access the evidence that would ordinarily be available. The same problem appears in other parts of the investigation, where the screenplay seems to move past questions that deserved closer attention. The investigators are supposed to be competent enough to challenge the cover-up, yet the film occasionally makes their job easier by overlooking possibilities that the audience can think of without much effort. A thriller does not need to explain every single detail, but it cannot keep asking us to admire the intelligence of its characters while expecting us to ignore the obvious questions.

The Bullet and the Convenient Science

The sequence involving Aleena removing the bullet with a hair slide was another moment that made me question the film’s commitment to plausibility. Even if we accept that such an act might be physically possible under particular circumstances, the more important question is whether the film adequately establishes those circumstances. Removing a bullet is not simply a matter of finding the right object and pulling it out. The location and depth of the wound, the risk of further injury, the condition of the person involved and the possibility of leaving traces all matter. A thriller can take creative liberties, but it should make those liberties feel convincing within its own world. The same applies to the forensic investigation. Ballistics, chemical analysis and other scientific methods can make a crime thriller more interesting precisely because they can expose mistakes that the characters believe they have successfully concealed. Here, however, some of the scientific and investigative elements feel more convenient than convincing. There are other questionable details throughout the film, but the larger problem is the cumulative effect: every time the screenplay relies on an unlikely coincidence or an insufficiently explained procedure, the carefully constructed strategy begins to feel less impressive.

The Courtroom Is Not a Stage for Last-Minute Revelations

I also wish Malayalam thrillers would move away from the familiar fantasy of courtroom proceedings in which ballistic reports, chemical tests and other crucial pieces of evidence suddenly appear at the last moment, leaving everyone present, including the lawyers, apparently clueless. The sequence in Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar left me wondering what kind of proceeding I was watching. Was it a formal trial, some form of judicial inquiry, or another kind of hearing? The absence of a clearly established procedural context made the scene even more confusing. Real legal proceedings have their own processes for collecting, examining, disclosing and challenging evidence. The defence and prosecution do not ordinarily discover the entire case for the first time in a dramatic courtroom reveal. Nor does scientific evidence become conclusive simply because someone presents a report at the right moment. Cinema obviously needs drama, and a courtroom can be an excellent place for a revelation. But that revelation becomes more satisfying when the groundwork has been laid beforehand and the audience understands its legal significance. A well-written cross-examination, a contradiction in testimony or an unexpected implication of evidence already introduced could deliver the same punch without making the entire judicial process look like a magic show.

A Good Watch That Relies Too Much on Plot Armour

Despite these flaws, I would not call Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar a bad film. Its central idea is interesting, and its emphasis on calculated moves rather than simply repeating the familiar formula of Drishyam gives it an identity of its own. The problem is that strategy works only when the obstacles are convincing. If the characters succeed because they have genuinely anticipated the investigators’ moves, that is satisfying. If they succeed because surveillance is conveniently absent, investigative possibilities are overlooked, scientific procedures become elastic and the legal system suddenly behaves in ways that serve the screenplay, then their intelligence becomes difficult to appreciate. In the end, the film has more plot armour and convenient turns than I would have liked. Drishyam is not flawless either, but its central achievement is making us think about the possibility of a cover-up and the consequences of every calculated decision. Pradhama Drishtya Kuttakkar reaches for a similar satisfaction without always earning it. It remains a decent thriller and a worthwhile watch, but I wish its investigation had been as carefully calculated as the crime it was trying to conceal.
